# A Little Princess

## A Simple-English Retelling

*Based on the supplied nineteen-chapter abstract of the story by Frances Hodgson Burnett.*

**Reading level:** A1–A2 English

## Chapter 1 - Sara

Sara Crewe was seven years old when she came to London with her father, Captain Crewe. They had lived in India for many years. Sara loved India, but she loved her father most of all.

The ship journey was long. Sara spent many hours beside her father. He told her about England, cold streets, and a school called Miss Minchin’s Select Seminary for Young Ladies. He had chosen the school because it had a good name. He also paid for a private room, pretty dresses, many books, and a large doll.

“You must give your doll a name,” Captain Crewe said.

Sara looked at the doll’s serious face. “Her name is Emily,” she answered. “She will be my friend.”

At the school, Miss Minchin hurried out to meet them. She smiled more brightly when she saw Captain Crewe’s fine clothes and luggage.

“Your daughter shall have everything she needs,” Miss Minchin promised.

Sara stood close to her father. She did not like the tall, busy school or the many strange faces. Still, she tried to be brave.

Miss Minchin showed her the private room. It had a warm fire, a bed with curtains, and a shelf for her books. Emily sat on a chair and looked as if she owned the room already.

“You are a little princess,” Miss Minchin told Sara.

Sara shook her head. “I am only Sara. A princess should be kind. Money does not make a person good.”

Miss Minchin did not know what to say. Captain Crewe smiled, but his eyes were sad. He did not want to leave his daughter.

Before he went, Sara held his hand tightly.

“I will think of you every day,” she said.

“And I will think of you every hour,” he replied.

When his carriage disappeared, Sara went to the window with Emily in her arms. London was gray and cold. Her new life had begun, but the person she loved was suddenly far away.

### Sara’s First Evening

Sara stayed at the window until the carriage became a tiny dark mark and then disappeared among other carriages. She pressed her forehead to the cold glass.

“He will come back,” she told Emily.

Emily sat very still. She had a round face, painted eyes, and a blue dress. She did not look like a doll who worried about anything. Sara sometimes thought Emily knew more than she showed.

“He will come back,” Sara said again. “And until then, we shall have things to do.”

She turned away from the window. The room looked larger now. When her father had been there, the bed, chairs, and books had seemed like parts of a visit. Now they were parts of a life.

Sara opened the trunk that held her clothes. She had many dresses. Some were bright and soft. One had little silver buttons. Another had a green ribbon at the waist. She touched them, but she did not feel happy.

“A dress cannot answer a letter,” she told Emily.

She found paper and a pencil. Her father had told her to write whenever she wished. Sara sat at the desk and began a letter.

Dear Papa,

I am in my room. It is warm, and Emily is with me. The school is very large. Miss Minchin speaks in a loud voice, but I think she wants the school to be good. I will try to be brave. Please think of me when you are away.

She stopped. She wanted to write that she was lonely, but the word seemed too small. Loneliness filled the room like fog.

She added, I will make friends if I can.

Then she folded the letter. She did not know when she could send it, but making the letter gave her a job. A job was a small bridge between one hour and the next.

A bell rang in the hall. It rang once, then again, as if it had forgotten something.

Sara picked up Emily and went downstairs.

The dining room held a long table. Older girls sat on one side, and younger girls sat on the other. Plates moved. Chairs scraped. Someone dropped a spoon.

Miss Minchin stood near the door.

“Sara, you may sit here,” she said, pointing to a place near her.

Sara saw that other girls were looking at her. A girl with dark hair whispered to another girl. Ermengarde looked at Sara and then quickly looked at her plate.

“Good evening,” Sara said to the girls near her.

Nobody answered at first. Then Ermengarde said, “Good evening.”

Miss Minchin gave Sara a bowl of soup. “You must learn the school rules. We rise early. We speak politely. We do not leave the table without permission. We do not make noise in the halls.”

From the far end of the room came a loud crash.

Miss Minchin closed her eyes.

“That was a cup,” one girl whispered.

“It was a very noisy cup,” Sara whispered back.

Ermengarde covered her mouth. A little laugh escaped between her fingers.

Miss Minchin looked at them. “What is funny?”

“Nothing,” Ermengarde said quickly.

Sara lowered her eyes, but her cheeks were warm. She had not meant to make trouble. It was simply difficult to sit beside a very noisy cup without thinking about it.

After soup came bread, potatoes, and a small piece of pudding. Sara noticed that a young girl across the table was trying to hide her hands under her napkin. The girl had a blue mark on one finger.

“Are you hurt?” Sara asked.

The girl shook her head. “I cut it while opening a box.”

Sara reached for the little cloth napkin beside her plate. “You can wrap it with this.”

The girl looked toward Miss Minchin.

“It is clean,” Sara said. “And I do not think the napkin has plans for the evening.”

The girl took it. Her name was Jessie. She tied the cloth around her finger.

Miss Minchin saw the exchange, but she said nothing. Sara wondered whether a school rule covered napkins that helped fingers.

When dinner ended, the girls left in groups. Sara was about to follow Ermengarde when a tall girl stepped into her path.

“You are the new rich girl,” she said.

“I am the new Sara,” Sara answered.

The tall girl frowned. “Do you have a pony?”

“No.”

“A carriage?”

“My father has one.”

“A hundred dolls?”

“Only one. Emily would not enjoy a crowd.”

The tall girl did not know whether Sara was joking. Sara smiled politely and walked on.

In the hall, Ermengarde caught up with her.

“You answered well,” she said.

“Did I?”

“I think so. Lavinia likes to ask questions that are not really questions.”

“Then perhaps she needs better questions.”

Ermengarde looked frightened. “Do not say that where she can hear you.”

“Why?”

“Because she hears very well.”

Behind them, Lavinia called, “I heard that!”

Sara and Ermengarde stopped. Then they both began to laugh. Even Lavinia smiled for one second before she remembered that she was meant to be annoyed.

### A Tour of the School

The next morning, a maid showed Sara the school. She moved so quickly that Sara had to hurry to keep up.

“This is the schoolroom. This is the music room. This is the stairs to the upper rooms. Do not touch the bell rope. Do not open the cupboard. Do not go into the garden when the door is locked.”

Sara counted the rules on her fingers.

“Are there rules about breathing?” she asked.

The maid stopped. “What?”

“I only want to know what to do if I forget.”

The maid tried not to smile. “Breathe quietly.”

The schoolroom had tall windows and rows of desks. A large blackboard stood at the front. Someone had drawn a cat in one corner. The cat had six legs and a hat.

“Who drew that?” Sara asked.

“Nobody knows,” said Ermengarde, who had joined the tour. “It appeared during arithmetic.”

“It is a good cat. It has enough legs to run away from arithmetic.”

Ermengarde laughed again. She seemed surprised that she had done so.

Sara saw a cupboard full of maps. She liked maps because they made faraway places feel close. India was marked in red. London was a small dot compared with the wide land around it.

“Can you read all the names?” Ermengarde asked.

“Not all. But I can learn them.”

“I cannot remember even one.”

Sara pointed to a long name. “You can remember this one as a sleepy dragon. It has a head, a tail, and too many bumps.”

Ermengarde repeated the name. She changed the middle into a dragon sound. It was not correct, but she remembered the shape of the word.

“You see?” Sara said. “A map is only a story that has forgotten its sentences.”

The maid called them onward.

They passed the kitchen. Warm smells came from inside. A cook carried a tray of buns. Sara’s stomach made a small sound.

Ermengarde heard it. “Was that you?”

“No. It was the school asking for breakfast.”

“The school has a stomach?”

“A very large one.”

They tried to walk quietly, but both girls were smiling.

The garden was behind a high wall. In summer, it must have been green. Now the trees were bare, and leaves covered the ground. A small black bird hopped along the path.

“That bird looks cold,” Sara said.

“It has feathers.”

“Feathers are not the same as a coat.”

Sara broke a little piece of bread from the breakfast she had saved and placed it near the wall. The bird waited until they moved away. Then it hopped over and carried the bread under a bush.

“You have made a friend,” Ermengarde said.

“It is too soon to say. The bird may only like bread.”

“That is how some people make friends too.”

Sara looked at her. Ermengarde’s face was serious.

“Then we should offer better bread,” Sara said.

### The First Night Letter

That evening, Sara returned to her room and found the letter to her father still on the desk. She asked Miss Minchin if it could be sent.

“Of course,” Miss Minchin said. “But you must not expect an answer every day.”

“I will not. I would like him to know that I am well.”

Miss Minchin glanced around the room. “You have a great deal here.”

Sara looked at the warm fire, the curtains, and the shelves of books. “Yes.”

“Many girls would be very pleased.”

“I am pleased with the books.”

“And the clothes?”

“They are pretty.”

“And the doll?”

Sara held Emily. “She is important.”

Miss Minchin seemed to expect Sara to say that everything was wonderful. When Sara did not, she gave a little sigh.

“You must learn to be grateful.”

Sara thought about the word. “I am grateful to my father. He gave me these things because he loves me.”

Miss Minchin’s mouth became a thin line. “You must also be grateful to the school.”

“I will try to learn its lessons.”

“That is not what I mean.”

Sara did not understand. She had not asked the school to love her. She only hoped it would allow her to learn.

After Miss Minchin left, Sara put the letter near the door. She made a small plan. Every evening, she would write one true thing, one kind thing, and one funny thing for her father.

The true thing was that she missed him.

The kind thing was that Jessie’s finger felt better.

The funny thing was that the school had a cat with six legs.

She told Emily the plan.

“You may help,” Sara said. “You can be in charge of the funny things.”

Emily continued to look serious.

“I know,” Sara said. “It is a great responsibility.”

Sara climbed into bed. The school made sounds around her. Footsteps passed in the hall. A door closed. Somewhere, a girl called for water. The building seemed full of lives that did not know her yet.

She imagined her father reading her letter in India. Perhaps he was sitting under a fan. Perhaps the air smelled of warm dust and flowers. Perhaps he was looking at the same moon.

Sara turned toward the window. The London sky was dark, but a few stars showed through it.

“Good night, Papa,” she whispered.

Then she whispered to Emily, “Good night, Princess.”

It was the first time she used that name for herself as a game. She did not know that the word would follow her through many hard days. For now, it was only a small secret between a girl, a doll, and the night.

### The Morning Bell

Sara woke before the bell. For a moment, she forgot where she was. She expected to hear birds in the garden at home and her father asking for tea. Instead, she heard a cart bump over the street outside.

Emily sat on the chair. In the gray light, she looked like a very serious guest who had spent the night thinking.

“Did you sleep well?” Sara asked.

Emily said nothing.

“I did not either. We shall improve tonight.”

The bell rang. Doors opened along the hall. Girls hurried past in slippers. One girl carried a brush in her mouth while trying to button her coat.

“You cannot brush your hair and dress at the same time,” Sara told her.

The girl tried to answer. The brush fell out. “I can do three things. I can also fall down.”

Sara caught her arm before she slipped on the rug.

“There,” Sara said. “You have completed the third thing.”

The girl laughed and ran away.

At breakfast, Sara sat beside Ermengarde. The table was full of porridge, bread, and small dishes of butter. Sara thought of the kitchens in India, where spices made the air smell warm even before breakfast.

“Do you like London?” Ermengarde asked.

“I do not know yet. London has not introduced itself properly.”

“It is very big.”

“So is India. I liked India because every day had a surprise.”

“What kind of surprise?”

“A monkey in a tree. A bright bird on a wall. A man selling sweet fruit. Once, a goat walked into our sitting room.”

Ermengarde stared. “What did your father do?”

“He invited the goat to leave. The goat did not accept.”

“How did you remove it?”

“We offered it a book. It preferred a curtain.”

Ermengarde laughed so hard that a little porridge jumped from her spoon. She looked worried, but Miss Minchin was speaking with another teacher and did not notice.

Sara used a piece of bread to draw a tiny map on her plate. She showed Ermengarde where India might be and where London sat far away.

“Your father is there now?” Ermengarde asked.

“He is traveling to his work. He may be on a train, or he may already be on a ship.”

“Do you feel afraid?”

Sara looked toward the windows. “Yes. But I can feel afraid and still eat breakfast.”

Ermengarde considered this. “That is useful.”

“It is one of my best skills.”

After breakfast, a maid brought Sara to the hall. Sara placed her father’s letter in the school post box. She watched it disappear through the narrow opening.

“Now it is traveling,” she said.

She imagined the letter walking down the street with a tiny suitcase. It would cross bridges, ride trains, and find a ship. Emily would be the letter’s guard.

“Do not let it lose its hat,” Sara told the doll.

The maid heard her. “Does the doll have a hat?”

“Not yet. She is waiting for the right adventure.”

The maid looked at Emily’s painted face. “You have a lively imagination.”

“It is useful when a person is new.”

The maid’s expression softened. “Yes. It can be useful.”

She led Sara toward the schoolroom. The morning light made the windows shine. Sara held her books in both arms. They were heavy, but they felt like friends.

At the door, Sara paused. She heard the girls talking inside. Soon they would begin their lessons. Soon she would meet a French teacher who would ask questions she could answer.

She did not know that Miss Minchin had already decided what sort of girl Sara must be. Sara only knew what sort of girl she wanted to be.

She stood a little straighter, touched Emily’s ribbon, and entered the room.

Before the teacher came, Sara found a loose button on a younger girl’s coat. She took a needle from the sewing basket and fixed it with three small stitches.

“My mother does that,” the girl said.

“Then your mother has good hands.”

The girl touched the button. “Will you sit with me at lunch?”

“If the school permits it.”

“We can ask the school.”

The girl marched to Miss Minchin and asked. Miss Minchin looked surprised, but she agreed. Sara learned that a new place could feel less strange each time she helped someone find a button, a map, or a little courage.

At the schoolroom door, Sara looked back once. The long hall was still strange, and her father was still far away. But the button was safe, the letter was traveling, and one girl had promised to sit with her. Sara decided that these were enough reasons to begin.

She placed her books on a desk. The six-legged cat watched from the blackboard. Sara gave it a solemn nod.

“You may guard the room,” she whispered. “I will do the lessons.”

The cat, being made of chalk, agreed completely.

Sara sat down, opened her notebook, and wrote the date. It was a small mark on a page, but it told her that this new life had a first morning. A first morning could be followed by a second one, and that thought made the room feel possible.

She smiled at Emily. “Now we are ready.”

The teacher lifted the bell, and the first lesson began.

Sara opened her notebook. She still missed her father, but she was no longer standing alone in the doorway.

## Chapter 2 - A French Lesson

The next morning, Sara went to her first French lesson. Miss Minchin came with her and spoke as if Sara knew nothing.

“Sara has lived in India,” she told the French teacher. “She has never learned French properly.”

Sara tried to speak. “My father taught me French. I have spoken it since I was very young.”

Miss Minchin waved one hand. “Do not pretend, child. You must begin with the easy words.”

The teacher entered the room. He was a kind man with bright eyes. He asked Sara a question in French.

Sara answered him in French. She told him about India, her father, the long trip, and Emily. Her words were clear and quick. She understood every question he asked.

The teacher laughed with surprise. “This child speaks very well. She knows more French than many older pupils.”

Miss Minchin’s face became red. She looked at Sara as if Sara had played a trick.

“Why did you not tell me?” she demanded.

“I tried,” Sara said softly.

The other girls had been listening at the door. Ermengarde looked amazed. Lavinia looked annoyed. Sara did not look proud. She only wished the lesson would end so she could return to Emily.

The French teacher gave Sara a difficult story to read. Sara read it aloud and then explained it to a younger girl who could not follow the words.

“You are clever,” Ermengarde said later.

Sara put her book away. “Perhaps. But being clever is not a reason to make someone else feel small.”

That answer made Ermengarde watch Sara with new interest. The girls now knew that Sara was well educated. They did not yet know that she was also ready to share what she knew.

Miss Minchin, however, remembered only her own embarrassment. She was pleased that Sara’s father was rich, but she did not like a child who could surprise her.

### The French Room

The French teacher’s name was Monsieur Dufarge. He had a round hat, a neat beard, and a habit of tapping his pencil when he was thinking. He liked pupils who tried. He liked mistakes that showed a person was learning. He did not like girls who laughed at another girl’s mistake.

On Sara’s second French morning, he wrote a sentence on the board. It was about a small fish, a fresh peach, and a foolish frog. The sentence had many sounds that made the girls twist their mouths.

“Read it,” he said.

Lavinia read the first two words and stopped.

Ermengarde read the first word and then looked at the ceiling.

Sara read the whole sentence. She made the frog sound especially foolish.

Monsieur Dufarge smiled. “Very good. Now say it faster.”

Sara tried. The words flew together. She said the fish was a frog and the peach was wearing a hat.

The room became quiet. Then Monsieur Dufarge laughed.

“A new translation,” he said. “Not correct, but very lively.”

Sara bowed. “The frog looked as if he needed a hat.”

“Perhaps he did. But let us help the fish first.”

The lesson became a game. Each girl had to read the sentence faster without changing the fish into a frog. The room filled with strange sounds. One girl called the peach a beach. Another made the frog sound like a door.

Even Miss Minchin, who was watching from the back, almost smiled. Then she remembered that Sara had surprised her and made her face serious again.

After the lesson, Monsieur Dufarge asked Sara to stay.

“Where did you learn French?”

“My father spoke it with me. He said that a language is a bridge. If you know the bridge, you can meet more people.”

“Your father was right.”

“He is right about many things.”

“Does he speak French like you?”

“He speaks more slowly. He says I run over the words.”

“Then you must learn when to run and when to walk.”

Sara nodded. “I can practice.”

Monsieur Dufarge gave her a small blue book. It contained short stories. “Read one each week. Tell me what you think. Do not only tell me what the words mean.”

Sara opened the book. “What else is there?”

“Tell me whether the story is wise, silly, kind, or rude.”

Sara liked this task. She could learn words and also make judgments. A story, she thought, was like a person. It might look pretty and still be unkind.

### A French Club

Sara began a little French club at the end of the school day. Ermengarde came because Sara promised to make the words into stories. Jessie came because she liked the sound of the language. A younger girl came because she thought the word for butterfly sounded like a song.

Sara drew pictures on the blackboard. A red apple became an angry apple. A chair became a tired chair. A spoon became a brave spoon that had decided to cross a bowl of soup.

“Why is the spoon brave?” Jessie asked.

“Because the soup has pepper in it.”

“Pepper is not a dragon.”

“You have not met the pepper in our kitchen.”

The girls practiced the new words. They acted out a tiny play about a lost hat. Ermengarde played the owner. Jessie played the hat. Sara played the wind, though she made the wind sneeze.

“The wind should not sneeze,” Ermengarde said.

“Why not? It has been near the dust.”

The play was silly, but every girl remembered the words for street, door, hat, wind, and search. When the girls made a mistake, Sara did not groan.

“Try it again,” she said. “The word is waiting for you.”

Ermengarde repeated a phrase three times. On the fourth try, she said it correctly.

“I did it!” she cried.

“You did. The word has moved into your house.”

“It may leave again.”

“Then we shall invite it back for tea.”

The club lasted only ten minutes. Miss Minchin did not allow extra noise in the schoolroom. But ten minutes was enough for the girls to learn, laugh, and feel less afraid of a difficult lesson.

One afternoon, Miss Minchin entered while Sara was drawing a large frog.

“What is this?” she asked.

“A French frog,” Sara answered.

“Why is it wearing a crown?”

“It won the race across the pond.”

Miss Minchin looked at the girls. “Are you using lesson time properly?”

“Yes,” Sara said. “The girls are practicing words.”

“Words do not need pictures.”

Ermengarde looked at the frog. “This one does.”

Sara hid a smile behind her hand.

Miss Minchin pointed to the board. “Read the sentence.”

Ermengarde read it. She made only one small mistake. Jessie corrected it. Lavinia, who had been passing the door, stopped to listen.

Miss Minchin looked annoyed, but she could not say the girls had learned nothing.

“Erase the frog,” she ordered.

Sara picked up the cloth. “May I keep the crown?”

“No.”

“It is a very small crown.”

“Erase it.”

Sara erased the crown. The frog looked less important, but it still remembered winning the race.

### A Letter in Two Languages

On Saturday, Sara received a letter from her father. It was short. He had been busy, but he told her that he was proud of her. He asked whether she liked the school and whether Emily had found a good chair.

Sara read the letter once in English. Then she read it again in French for practice. Some of the words felt different in French, as if they had put on new shoes.

She wrote her answer in both languages. In English, she told her father about the six-legged cat, the goat story, and the French frog. In French, she told him that the school was large and that she had met a girl who was learning bravely.

She did not write Ermengarde’s name because Ermengarde was shy about being mentioned in letters. Sara wrote, I helped someone who thinks she cannot learn. She can learn. She only needs time and a friend.

Sara took the letter to Monsieur Dufarge.

“Will you correct it?” she asked.

He read the French part. “There are three mistakes.”

Sara reached for her pencil.

“But the meaning is clear, and the feeling is warmer than the grammar.”

“Can a feeling have grammar?”

“A feeling has its own rules. You must still learn the language, but do not forget the heart.”

Sara corrected the three mistakes. She copied the letter neatly and sent it to her father.

That evening, she imagined him reading the two versions. Perhaps he would understand every French word. Perhaps he would pretend to struggle so she could explain them.

“Papa will say the frog is too proud,” Sara told Emily.

Emily sat beside the inkpot. Sara moved her before she could fall in.

“You must be careful. A doll in an inkpot is not a good ending for any story.”

### Miss Minchin’s Test

Miss Minchin decided to test Sara in front of two visitors. She believed the child’s French ability had been exaggerated. She also wanted the visitors to see that the school had produced a clever pupil.

The visitors were two ladies who wore matching gray hats. They sat in the parlor while Sara stood near the piano.

“Speak to her in French,” Miss Minchin told one lady.

The lady asked Sara about the weather. Sara answered. The lady asked about books. Sara answered. Then the lady asked whether Sara missed India.

Sara paused. This question was not in any lesson.

“Yes,” she said in French. “I miss the warm air and the colors. I miss the sound of people speaking in the market. But I do not want London to think I dislike it. London is still learning how to be my friend.”

One visitor smiled. “That is a thoughtful answer.”

Miss Minchin seemed pleased. “Sara has a very unusual mind.”

Sara looked at her. “Everyone has a mind. Some minds are noisier than others.”

The gray-hatted ladies laughed. Miss Minchin did not.

After they left, Miss Minchin said, “You must not speak too freely to visitors.”

“Did I say something wrong?”

“You made the school sound strange.”

“It is strange. I am new.”

“You must remember that people judge a school by its pupils.”

Sara considered this. “Then the pupils should be kind to one another. That would be a good sign.”

Miss Minchin walked away before she had to answer.

### The Word Sara Chose

At the end of the week, Monsieur Dufarge asked each girl to choose one French word she liked. Jessie chose the word for moon. Lavinia chose the word for beautiful. Ermengarde chose a word that meant brave, although she said she was not brave yet.

Sara chose the word for friend.

“Why that word?” the teacher asked.

“Because a friend helps a person understand what she could not understand alone.”

Ermengarde looked down, but her smile showed that she had heard.

Monsieur Dufarge nodded. “A good choice.”

Sara closed her book. The first lesson had begun with Miss Minchin thinking Sara knew nothing. It had ended with many girls discovering that a hard word could become friendly if someone opened the door for them.

Sara knew there would be more lessons, more mistakes, and more people who judged too quickly. She also knew she could answer with patience. If patience failed, she could draw a frog.

### A Day of Mixed-Up Words

The following Tuesday, Monsieur Dufarge brought a basket of ordinary things to the French room. There was a red scarf, a wooden spoon, a small mirror, a green apple, and a shoe that had lost its mate.

“Name each thing,” he said.

The girls began. The scarf was easy. The apple was easy. The mirror was easy until Ermengarde held it upside down and called it a window.

“It is a window for your nose,” Sara said.

Ermengarde looked into it. “My nose looks worried.”

“It has heard about the test.”

Everyone laughed, including Monsieur Dufarge.

Then he held up the spoon. Lavinia gave the wrong word. Jessie gave another wrong word. Sara pretended that the spoon had been invited to the lesson as a guest.

“Good morning, Spoon,” she said. “Are you a hat?”

Ermengarde answered in French, “No, Spoon is a spoon.”

“Are you a horse?”

“No, Spoon is a spoon.”

“Are you a very small boat?”

Ermengarde pointed to the bowl of the spoon. “Perhaps a boat.”

“Excellent,” said Monsieur Dufarge. “Now you have used the word in a new sentence.”

The class made a tiny story. The spoon sailed across a bowl of soup. The apple was the captain. The scarf was the flag. The mirror was the sea, although Ermengarde insisted that it was a sea for noses.

When the lesson ended, Sara wrote the story in her notebook. She added a picture of the shoe. The shoe had a sad face because its mate was missing.

“Why is the shoe sad?” Jessie asked.

“It cannot go dancing alone.”

“Shoes do not dance.”

“That is why this shoe is sad. It has dreams that nobody understands.”

Jessie looked at her own shoes. “My shoes understand each other.”

“You are lucky.”

The next day, the girls found the missing shoe behind a curtain. It had not been lost at all. A younger girl had hidden it there because she wanted to see whether someone would search.

“The shoe has returned,” Sara announced.

Ermengarde placed the two shoes side by side. “They look pleased.”

“They have much to discuss.”

The girls practiced the word for return. By the end of the week, every girl remembered it.

### Sara’s Quiet Correction

Not every girl liked the club. One afternoon, Lavinia told Sara, “You act as if you are the teacher.”

“I am not the teacher.”

“Then stop correcting people.”

Sara closed her notebook. “I will stop when they ask me to. Monsieur Dufarge asked me to help.”

“You think you are better than us.”

Sara looked honestly surprised. “No. I think we are all better when we help each other.”

Lavinia crossed her arms. “You always have an answer.”

“I have many questions too.”

“Such as?”

“Why do you want me to fail?”

Lavinia opened her mouth and then closed it. She had no answer that sounded good.

Sara did not press her. She picked up the blue storybook and offered it to Lavinia.

“Would you like to read this part? The girl in it is very brave, but she makes a foolish choice.”

Lavinia took the book. “Is she foolish like a frog?”

“More like a frog with a crown.”

Lavinia smiled despite herself. She read the passage. Her French was not perfect, but it was good. When she finished, Sara did not clap. She simply said, “That was clear.”

Lavinia gave the book back. “You can help me tomorrow. But do not draw a crown on my head.”

“I would draw a hat. Crowns are difficult.”

Miss Minchin never heard about the hat. The school went on with its lessons, bells, meals, and rules. But the French room had changed. It was no longer only a place where girls feared mistakes. It had become a place where a mistake could turn into a joke, and a joke could help a word stay in the mind.

Sara wrote one last line in her notebook that week: A language is a door. A kind person holds it open.

Then she drew the French frog beside the sentence. This time, she gave him two hats.

On the last day of the month, Monsieur Dufarge asked the girls to speak without notes. Ermengarde told the story of the lost hat. She forgot one word, stopped, and looked at Sara.

Sara did not say the word. She pointed to the empty space in the air as if the word were hiding there.

Ermengarde thought. “Wind!” she cried.

The teacher nodded. “Exactly.”

Ermengarde had found the word herself. She left the room smiling, and Sara felt as pleased as if she had won a prize.

That afternoon, Sara found a small note in her desk. It said, Thank you for not saying the word for me. Ermengarde had drawn a tiny door at the bottom of the page.

Sara showed it to Emily. “Look. The door is open.”

Emily’s painted eyes seemed to agree. Sara folded the note carefully and kept it inside her French book.

The note stayed there for a long time. Whenever Ermengarde faced a hard word, Sara opened the book and touched the little door. Ermengarde would take a breath, try again, and usually find her way through.

Sara did not tell her that she sometimes needed the same kind of door. A new country, a new school, and a father far away could make any mind feel foggy. French words were not the only things that needed patience.

One morning, Monsieur Dufarge asked Sara to read a short letter aloud. She read it beautifully, but she rushed through the last line. The teacher raised one finger.

“Walk there,” he said.

Sara read the line again, slowly. Ermengarde repeated it after her. The word felt clearer the second time.

“Even clever people need to slow down,” Monsieur Dufarge said.

Sara wrote this in her notebook: Fast is not always best. A bridge is useful only when people can cross it.

The next day, Ermengarde used the slow-reading rule with a new word. She walked through it one sound at a time and reached the end without help. Sara gave her a quiet thumbs-up. The word stayed where it belonged, and the two girls went to lunch feeling as if they had crossed a very small bridge.

Before the bell ended the lesson, Monsieur Dufarge asked Sara to listen to each girl read. Sara corrected only the sound that needed help. She learned that helping did not mean speaking the most. Sometimes it meant waiting, watching, and letting another person find the word.

After class, Sara put three new rules in her notebook: listen first, speak kindly, and leave room for another voice. She decorated the rules with tiny blue dots. Ermengarde added a fourth rule beneath them: celebrate small victories. Together they drew a bridge beside it.

“That bridge is becoming very busy,” Sara said.

“It carries words,” Ermengarde replied.

“Then it needs a strong railing.”

Sara drew one. It looked like a crooked line, but they agreed it was strong enough for a first lesson.

## Chapter 3 - Ermengarde

Ermengarde St. John sat at the back of the schoolroom. Her books were always open, but the lessons seemed to run away from her.

When the teacher asked her a question, Ermengarde forgot the answer. When she tried to read, the letters seemed to dance.

“You are hopeless,” Miss Minchin often said.

Ermengarde began to believe it. She thought clever girls would never want to speak with her.

One afternoon, Sara found her crying behind a bookcase.

“What happened?” Sara asked.

“I am stupid,” Ermengarde said. “I shall never learn anything.”

Sara sat beside her. “That sounds like a very unpleasant story. Who told it to you?”

“Everyone.”

“Then we shall write a better story.”

Ermengarde looked up. “How?”

Sara opened the history book. The page was about a king, a bridge, and a battle. It looked dull enough to make a snail yawn.

“Let us imagine the king is afraid of ducks,” Sara said. “Every time he walks near the bridge, a duck follows him. Quack, quack! He cannot lead his army because he is watching his feet.”

Ermengarde began to laugh. Soon the dates and names had become people in a little play. Sara gave each person a voice. Ermengarde acted out the king’s duck problem while Sara read the facts.

The next day, Ermengarde remembered the whole lesson. She even remembered the date.

“I knew you could learn,” Sara said.

“You are clever,” Ermengarde answered. “Why do you want to sit with me?”

Sara looked surprised. “Because I like you. A friend does not need to know every answer.”

After that, Sara helped Ermengarde whenever she could. She never did the work for her. She found a way to make the work less frightening.

Ermengarde slowly stood straighter. She was still forgetful, and some lessons were still hard. But she no longer thought that a hard lesson was proof that she was a bad person.

Sara’s friendship taught her something important: intelligence is most useful when it opens a door for another person.

### The Difficult List

Ermengarde kept a list of the things she believed she could never do. She wrote it in a small notebook with a purple cover.

The list said:

- Remember long dates.
- Spell difficult names.
- Read a page without getting lost.
- Do sums without making the numbers angry.
- Answer a teacher before the teacher sighs.

Sara read the list at Ermengarde’s desk.

“The numbers are not angry,” Sara said.

“They look angry.”

“They look like small doors. You must open one at a time.”

Ermengarde tapped the notebook. “I have tried. My head becomes full of fog.”

“Then we shall give the fog something to do.”

Sara drew four boxes on a sheet of paper. The first box held a date. The second held a name. The third held a picture. The fourth held a silly sentence.

“Every lesson can visit four boxes,” she explained. “The date tells us when. The name tells us who. The picture shows what happened. The silly sentence helps the memory stay.”

“What silly sentence can help with a battle?”

“The king forgot his boots.”

“Did he really?”

“Not in the book. But he will not forget them now.”

Ermengarde was uncertain, but she tried. The date went into the first box. The king went into the second. Sara drew a bridge in the third. In the fourth box, the king wore boots on his hands.

Ermengarde laughed. Then she repeated the date.

“You remembered it,” Sara said.

“Because the boots are wrong.”

“A wrong picture can sometimes point to the right fact.”

They made a new page for every lesson. Soon Ermengarde’s purple notebook was full of small boxes. It was not a magic book. It did not make every answer easy. It did give her a path through the fog.

### The First Test

On Friday, the teacher announced a history test. Ermengarde’s face became white.

“I cannot do it,” she said.

“You have not seen the questions.”

“They will be terrible.”

“Perhaps they will be polite questions.”

“Questions are never polite.”

“Then we shall teach them manners.”

Sara helped Ermengarde study, but she made one rule. Ermengarde had to answer first. If she was stuck, Sara could give a clue. Sara would not give the answer immediately.

The first question asked for a date. Ermengarde remembered the king’s boots and wrote the date.

The second question asked for a name. She remembered the bridge.

The third question asked what happened. She drew the picture in her mind and wrote two sentences.

When the test ended, Ermengarde did not know whether she had done well. She only knew she had finished.

“I did not run away,” she told Sara.

“That is already a victory.”

The teacher returned the papers the next day. Ermengarde had not received the highest mark. She had made mistakes with two names. But she had answered most of the questions.

Miss Minchin looked at the paper and said, “This is better than usual.”

Ermengarde held the paper close. The words were not full of praise, but they did not crush her.

Sara whispered, “Your notebook opened a door.”

Ermengarde whispered back, “The king’s boots opened it.”

### A New Kind of Practice

Ermengarde wanted to help Sara in return. She knew Sara missed her father, even when Sara spoke brightly.

“You should make a notebook for missing people,” Ermengarde said.

Sara paused. “What would it contain?”

“A box for remembering. A box for waiting. A box for feeling better.”

“What goes in the last box?”

Ermengarde thought. “Cake.”

“A fine plan.”

“And a friend.”

Sara smiled. “An even finer plan.”

They made a pretend notebook. The first page held a drawing of Captain Crewe’s ship. The second held a picture of Sara and her father walking in a garden. The third held a list of things Sara could do while waiting: read, write, help Lottie, feed the bird, and teach French words.

“You forgot Emily,” Ermengarde said.

“Emily is in every box.”

Ermengarde drew a large circle around the whole page. “Then she is the cover.”

Sara laughed. “She may be pleased.”

The plan helped Sara too. It did not make the waiting shorter. It made the waiting have a shape.

### The History Players

The school was preparing a small evening show. Older girls would sing. Younger girls would recite poems. Ermengarde was expected to read a history passage.

She was terrified.

“I will forget every word,” she said.

“Then let us give every word a friend,” Sara answered.

They turned the passage into a play. Ermengarde became a queen. Sara became the bridge. Jessie became a messenger. The important date was written on a card and hidden inside the queen’s crown.

“A queen cannot read a card from her crown,” Ermengarde said.

“She can if the crown is very helpful.”

They practiced in Sara’s room. Ermengarde spoke too softly. Sara placed a pillow at the back of the room.

“Speak to the pillow.”

“Why?”

“It is sitting in the last row and cannot hear you.”

Ermengarde tried again. Her voice grew stronger.

Lottie wandered in wearing one sock.

“Why is the queen wearing a crown?” she asked.

“Because she is a queen.”

“Why is the bridge wearing a blue ribbon?”

“Because it is a very fashionable bridge.”

Lottie decided that the bridge needed a second ribbon. Sara tied one around her wrist so the bridge could look grand.

On the evening of the show, Ermengarde stood before the school. Her hands shook. She looked at the audience and saw Miss Minchin, the teachers, the girls, and Sara near the side curtain.

Sara touched two fingers to her own wrist. It was their signal for the blue ribbon.

Ermengarde remembered the queen, the bridge, the date card, and the pillow in the last row. She began to read.

Her voice was not perfect. She stopped once. But she found her place and continued. When she finished, the audience clapped.

Lottie clapped so hard that her loose sock fell off.

Ermengarde returned to her seat with shining eyes.

“I did not forget every word,” she said.

“No. You remembered yourself too.”

### A Letter from Home

Ermengarde’s father visited the school a few weeks later. He was a quiet man who wore a brown coat and carried a book under his arm.

Ermengarde ran to him, then stopped because she remembered that she was meant to walk politely.

“You may run,” he said. “I am not a teacher.”

She ran.

During tea, he asked about her lessons. Ermengarde looked at Sara.

“I have a notebook,” she said.

“What is in it?”

“Kings, bridges, dates, and boots.”

Her father looked at Sara.

“Sara helps me remember,” Ermengarde explained. “But she makes me do my own answers.”

Her father nodded. “That is the best kind of help.”

He gave Ermengarde a small book of stories. “Read one page each night. Do not worry if you do not understand every word. Keep going until the story shows you what it means.”

Ermengarde held the book carefully. “Will you come again?”

“Yes.”

After he left, she gave Sara a serious look.

“You must not stop being my friend.”

“I do not plan to.”

“Even if I become very clever?”

“Especially then. Clever people need friends who remind them not to wear boots on their hands.”

Ermengarde looked at the history notebook. “The king was comfortable.”

“That is not the same as correct.”

They laughed together. Ermengarde still had difficult lessons ahead, but she no longer faced them alone. Sara still had lonely moments, but she now had a friend who noticed them.

The school called Ermengarde slow. Sara saw that she was careful, loyal, and brave enough to try again. Sometimes a person needed a different path, not a lower place.

### The Missing Purple Notebook

One rainy afternoon, Ermengarde rushed into Sara’s room with an empty look on her face.

“The notebook is gone.”

“Which notebook?”

“The purple one. The one with the king and the boots. I left it on my desk, and now it has disappeared.”

Ermengarde searched under books, inside drawers, and beneath her pillow. She opened the cupboard three times, although a notebook could not easily hide behind one small shoe.

“Perhaps someone took it,” she said.

Sara did not accuse anyone. “Let us remember where you used it last.”

Ermengarde closed her eyes. “I used it in history. Then I carried it to the garden. Then I put it beside the music room. Then I went to supper.”

“Did you carry anything else?”

“A scarf.”

“Was the scarf purple?”

“No. It was yellow.”

“Was the notebook yellow?”

Ermengarde looked worried. “It may have become yellow.”

They followed her path. Near the music room, Sara found a line of purple crumbs on the floor.

“That is strange,” Ermengarde said.

The crumbs led to a bench. Under the bench sat a small girl with the notebook. She had been looking at the drawings.

“I did not mean to steal it,” the girl said. “I wanted to see the king’s boots.”

Ermengarde took the notebook. She looked ready to complain, but the girl’s hands were shaking.

“You may look at it if you ask first,” Ermengarde said.

The girl nodded.

“And you must not eat the pages.”

The girl blinked. “I did not.”

Sara picked up one purple crumb. “This is paper.”

Ermengarde looked at the book. One corner was missing.

The little girl hid her face.

Sara knelt. “Are you hungry?”

The child nodded.

Ermengarde took the biscuit from her own pocket. She had saved it for later.

“Here,” she said. “It is not a notebook.”

The girl ate the biscuit. Sara and Ermengarde repaired the missing corner with paste. The notebook was not perfect afterward, but the king still had his boots.

“You did not become angry,” Sara said.

“I wanted to. But she looked more frightened than wicked.”

“That is an important thing to notice.”

Ermengarde wrote a new line in the notebook: Before I decide what a person is, I should ask what happened.

### The Spelling Race

The school held a spelling race on a bright afternoon. The teacher wrote ten words on the board. The girl who spelled the most correctly would receive a small silver pencil.

Ermengarde wanted the pencil, but she did not want anyone to know. She kept looking at it on the teacher’s table.

“You may want it,” Sara said.

“I do not.”

“Your eyes have been visiting it.”

“My eyes are curious.”

Sara whispered, “Curious eyes can still practice.”

They made pictures for the ten words. A mountain stood beside a fountain. A rabbit wore a jacket. A window looked out at a yellow moon.

During the race, Ermengarde spelled the first word correctly. Then she missed the second. She whispered the word to herself and pictured the rabbit in the jacket.

The third word was correct. The fourth was correct. The fifth was a long word that looked like a snake.

“Do not let the snake frighten you,” Sara said from her desk. “Count its bumps.”

Ermengarde counted the letters. She spelled the word slowly.

The teacher nodded.

At the end, Ermengarde had seven correct answers. Another girl had eight. Ermengarde did not win the silver pencil, but she had done better than she expected.

The winner came over and offered her the pencil for a moment.

“You can hold it,” the girl said. “It is very smooth.”

Ermengarde held it. “It feels like a little piece of moon.”

Sara said, “Do not spell moon with it. The pencil may become confused.”

The winner laughed and put the pencil away.

Ermengarde returned to her desk. “I wanted to win.”

“Of course.”

“I am disappointed.”

“That is allowed.”

“I thought a good person had to be happy for everyone else.”

“A good person can be happy for someone else and disappointed for herself. Both feelings can sit in the same chair.”

Ermengarde considered this. “The chair may be crowded.”

“Then give them a larger chair.”

### Ermengarde’s Own Lesson

Soon Ermengarde began helping a younger girl with numbers. She used Sara’s box idea, but she changed it.

“My boxes are a kitchen,” she explained. “The ones are peas. The tens are potatoes. The hundreds are large pumpkins.”

The younger girl stared at the page. “Where do the pumpkins go?”

“At the back. They need space.”

The girl solved a sum. Then she solved another. Ermengarde did not give the answer. She asked questions and waited.

Sara watched from across the room. Ermengarde was using what she had learned, but she was not copying Sara’s way. She had found her own path.

“You are becoming a teacher,” Sara said.

“No. I am becoming a kitchen.”

“That may be more useful. Kitchens have food.”

Ermengarde smiled. The school still called her slow when she needed extra time. But she had started to measure herself by what she could do, not by the harsh words of people who had never tried to understand her.

That night, Ermengarde crossed out two lines in her old list. She did not cross them out because the tasks were easy. She crossed them out because she had done them while they were hard. Sara helped her add a new line: Ask for help without feeling ashamed.

“That one is difficult,” Ermengarde said.

“Then it belongs on the list.”

Ermengarde closed the notebook. “Tomorrow I will try.”

“Tomorrow is a good place for trying.”

### The Question She Asked Herself

Ermengarde began to notice the unkind words she said to herself. When she made a mistake, she called herself stupid before anyone else could do it. Sara asked her to imagine that another girl had made the same mistake.

“Would you call that girl stupid?” Sara asked.

“No. I would help her.”

“Then try speaking to yourself as if you were your own friend.”

Ermengarde did not like the idea at first. It felt silly to be kind to the person inside her head. But when she forgot a date, she said, “I need another look,” instead of, “I can never learn.”

The new sentence did not fix the mistake. It gave her enough calm to correct it.

“It is harder than helping someone else,” Ermengarde said.

“That is why it is good practice.”

She wrote the sentence in her purple notebook: Speak kindly inside too.

The schoolroom did not change. Teachers still hurried. Lessons still became difficult. But Ermengarde had begun to carry a kinder voice with her. Sara saw her use it when she helped the younger girl with numbers, and she understood that a friend could open a door, but the other person had to walk through.

Ermengarde soon helped Sara in return. When Sara forgot a French word, Ermengarde pointed to the little door in the notebook. When Sara rushed through a sentence, Ermengarde raised one finger and said, “Walk there.” Sara laughed, slowed down, and tried again.

“You are my teacher now,” Sara said.

“Only for one word.”

“One word can be a beginning.”

Ermengarde smiled. She had learned that friendship was not a ladder with one clever girl at the top. It was a bridge that two people could cross in both directions.

The next week, Ermengarde made her own little list. It said, “Ask when I do not know. Try before I say I cannot. Thank the person who waits.” She showed it to Sara before the lesson began.

Sara read the list twice. “This is a very good list.”

“I copied the idea from you.”

“Then we are both copying the best parts.”

Ermengarde tucked the paper into her pocket. For the first time, a difficult lesson felt less like a wall and more like a door with a handle.

Ermengarde opened that door the next morning. She answered one question in class before Sara could whisper the answer. Her voice shook, but she kept speaking until the sentence was finished. Sara clapped softly under the desk. Ermengarde's smile was brighter than any gold star.

## Chapter 4 - Lottie

Lottie Legh was the smallest girl in the school. She was not yet five years old, and she missed her mother very much.

When Lottie remembered that her mother was dead, she cried until her face became hot. She kicked her shoes, pushed away her dinner, and shouted that nobody loved her.

One morning, the crying became so loud that Miss Minchin ran into the room.

“Stop this noise at once!” she said.

Lottie cried harder. She fell to the floor and held the leg of a chair.

Sara came near her. She did not pull Lottie away. She sat on the floor too, even though her clean dress touched the dust.

“My mother is dead,” Sara said.

Lottie stopped kicking. She looked at Sara through wet eyes.

“Where is she?”

“She is not here. I cannot see her or hear her. I miss her every day.”

“Do you cry?”

“Sometimes. I cried when I was little. I still feel sad.”

Lottie crawled closer. “What do you do when you miss her?”

Sara thought for a moment. “I remember the good things. I also talk to my father. And when I cannot talk to him, I tell stories to Emily.”

Lottie studied the doll. “Does Emily answer?”

“Not with words. She is a very quiet person.”

Lottie gave a small, wet laugh.

Sara told her a story about two little girls who lived in a garden. Their mothers watched them from the stars. The story did not make Lottie forget her mother, but it helped her breathe slowly again.

After that day, Lottie followed Sara everywhere. She called Sara “my Sara” and wanted Sara to sit beside her at meals. Sara helped her wash her face, tie her shoes, and find her lost ribbon.

The work was sometimes tiring. Lottie could lose a ribbon while wearing it. But Sara did not turn away. She understood that a small child could carry a very large sadness.

To Lottie, Sara became a sister, a friend, and sometimes almost a mother.

### Lottie’s Bad Morning

Lottie’s sadness did not end after one story. Some mornings, she woke happily and asked for jam. On other mornings, she woke believing that her mother had gone away only for a moment.

On one such morning, Lottie opened her eyes and called, “Mama!”

No one answered. She climbed out of bed, ran into the hall, and began to search every room. She looked under tables and behind curtains. She opened a cupboard and found only towels.

“Mama is not in the towels!” she shouted.

“That is lucky,” said Sara, who had followed her. “Towels are not very good at giving hugs.”

Lottie did not laugh. She threw a brush across the room.

Sara picked it up. “You are angry with the morning.”

“I am angry with everybody!”

“That is a large job. You may need breakfast first.”

“I do not want breakfast.”

“Then perhaps breakfast wants you.”

Lottie folded her arms. Her face became red.

Sara sat on the floor. She did not tell Lottie to stop crying. She waited until the child’s breathing slowed.

“When I miss my father,” Sara said, “I sometimes want to throw something. I choose a pillow because pillows are forgiving.”

Lottie looked at the bed. “Can I throw a pillow?”

“Yes. But aim away from the window.”

Lottie threw the pillow. It landed on the chair and knocked Emily to the floor.

Sara lifted the doll. “Emily is not forgiving. She is judging you.”

Lottie gave a weak giggle.

They threw two more pillows at a blanket folded over a stool. The blanket became a mountain. When Lottie was tired, Sara helped her put the pillows back.

“Now we have made the anger smaller,” Sara said.

“It is still here.”

“Feelings do not always leave when we ask them. Sometimes they sit nearby until they grow tired.”

Lottie climbed into Sara’s lap. “Will Mama come when I am tired?”

“I do not know.”

Sara never wanted to lie to her. She could not promise that a dead person would return. She could promise to stay.

“I will be here,” Sara said. “We can be tired together.”

Lottie held the edge of Sara’s sleeve. After a while, she agreed to eat toast.

### The Memory Garden

Sara wanted to give Lottie a place for her memories. She found a small wooden box in her room. Inside were a blue ribbon, a smooth stone, a dried flower, and a piece of white paper.

“What is the box for?” Lottie asked.

“It is a garden for things we cannot plant in the ground.”

Lottie looked confused.

Sara put the dried flower inside. “This can be a morning when the sun was warm.”

She put in the stone. “This can be a walk.”

She folded the paper. “This can hold a word you remember.”

“What word?”

“Perhaps your mother’s name.”

Lottie whispered the name. Sara wrote it carefully on the paper. Lottie watched the pencil move.

“Now it is in the box,” Sara said. “It cannot run away.”

Lottie added a button from her dress. “Mama put this on my coat.”

“Then it is a very important button.”

“It is not pretty.”

“Pretty is not the rule for a memory garden.”

They searched for more things. Lottie found a tiny thread, a paper star, and a pebble from a path outside. Sara found a feather.

“What does the feather remember?” Lottie asked.

“A bird that was in a hurry.”

“Did it have somewhere to go?”

“I think it had a meeting.”

Lottie put the feather beside the button. “Perhaps it carried a message to Mama.”

Sara did not say that she knew no way to prove such a thing. The idea made Lottie’s face calmer, and it was not a cruel idea.

They made a lid for the box. Lottie drew a garden on it. The flowers had large faces. One flower wore a hat.

“This flower is Mama,” Lottie said.

“Why does she wear a hat?”

“Because the sun is bright.”

“A wise flower.”

Lottie placed the box under her pillow. She did not look for her mother in every cupboard that day. When she missed her, she touched the box.

### Emily’s Tea Party

Lottie became interested in Emily. She wanted to hold the doll, but Sara was careful. Emily had been her friend for a long time.

“Emily may come to tea,” Sara said. “But she must be polite.”

Lottie set three cups on the floor. The cups were empty.

“Emily wants milk,” Lottie said.

“She is a very quiet drinker.”

“She wants sugar.”

“How many spoons?”

Lottie thought. “Seven.”

“Emily will become a sugar mountain.”

“Then she can climb.”

Sara put one grain of sugar in the cup. Lottie pretended to pour the rest. Emily sat at the table with her hands folded.

“She is not saying thank you,” Lottie complained.

“She says it inside.”

“I cannot hear.”

“You must listen with your imagination.”

Lottie leaned close to the doll. Her eyes became wide.

“She says thank you,” Lottie announced.

“What else?”

“She says the chair is lumpy.”

Sara looked at the chair. “Emily is very honest.”

The tea party continued. Lottie gave Emily a paper cake. Emily refused it because it was too flat. Sara made a taller cake from a folded handkerchief.

“Now it is grand,” Lottie said.

“It may fall.”

It fell at once. Lottie laughed so loudly that a girl in the hall opened the door.

“Is someone hurt?” the girl asked.

“Only the cake,” Sara replied.

“Then it can recover.”

Lottie liked that answer. She put the handkerchief cake back on the plate.

The game gave Lottie a way to care for someone smaller than herself. She fed Emily, tucked in the doll’s blanket, and told her not to be afraid of thunder.

“Emily is not afraid,” Sara said.

“She is pretending not to be.”

Sara looked at the doll. “That is possible.”

### A Small Fight

One afternoon, Lottie saw another young girl sitting close to Sara. The girl had a red ribbon and wanted help with a picture.

Lottie pulled Sara’s sleeve.

“Come with me.”

“I am helping her finish the house.”

“You are my Sara.”

The other girl looked nervous and moved away.

Sara gently held Lottie’s hand. “I am your friend, but I can be her friend too.”

“No.”

“You may feel jealous. You may not push her.”

Lottie’s lower lip shook. “If you like her, you will not like me.”

“That is not how liking works. The heart is not a plate of cake that becomes empty when one person has a piece.”

“It is a big cake?”

“Very big. It has room for you, me, Emily, Ermengarde, Becky, and the girl with the red ribbon.”

Lottie looked at the other girl. “What is her name?”

“Nora,” Sara said.

Lottie thought for a long time. “Can Nora have the door of the cake?”

“She may have a slice.”

Nora came back. Lottie offered her a pencil. The two girls drew the house together. Lottie drew a chimney that leaned to one side.

“The house is falling,” Nora said.

“No,” said Lottie. “It is dancing.”

Sara smiled. Lottie had not learned to share all at once. She had learned one small step.

### A Question for Miss Minchin

Miss Minchin disliked the time Sara spent with Lottie. She wanted Sara to attend lessons and appear polished before visitors.

“You must not let the child depend on you,” she told Sara.

“She is learning to do more things herself.”

“She cries whenever you leave.”

“She cries because she misses her mother. I cannot make that sorrow disappear by leaving her alone.”

Miss Minchin looked at Sara sharply. “You are a child. Do not speak as if you understand everything.”

“I do not understand everything. I understand missing someone.”

For a moment, Miss Minchin had no answer. Sara’s voice was quiet, but she did not lower her eyes.

“Lottie needs rules,” Miss Minchin said.

“Yes. She also needs someone to listen when the rules are not enough.”

Miss Minchin walked away. Sara knew she had taken a risk. She also knew that kindness did not mean agreeing whenever a powerful person spoke.

That night, Lottie cried again. Sara sat beside her bed and told a story about the memory garden. In the story, a little girl placed a button in the soil, and a tree grew with buttons instead of leaves.

“What did the tree do in winter?” Lottie asked.

“It wore a coat.”

“Trees cannot wear coats.”

“This tree had excellent branches.”

Lottie smiled. She held the wooden box under her pillow.

“Will we make the tree tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow we will look for a good pot.”

“A very big pot.”

“A pot that can hold one tree and two impatient gardeners.”

Lottie’s eyes closed. Sara stayed until the child slept.

Lottie still had sad mornings. She still cried when memories arrived without warning. But she began to learn that sorrow could be carried with help. Sara could not replace Lottie’s mother. She could be a steady hand while Lottie grew.

### The Pot and the Tree

The next day, Sara and Lottie searched for a pot for the memory tree. They looked in the garden shed, where old tools leaned against the wall.

“This one,” Lottie said.

It was a tiny pot with a crack down the side.

“It may not hold a tree.”

“The tree can be small.”

“Then it will be a tree for a mouse.”

Lottie held the pot against her chest. “Mice need trees too.”

Sara agreed. They found a larger pot behind a bench. It was dusty and had a spider web across the rim.

Lottie stepped back. “There is a spider.”

“The spider lives here.”

“We need the pot.”

“Then we must ask politely.”

Sara bent down. “Good morning, Spider. We would like to borrow your pot.”

The spider moved one leg.

“She says we may use it,” Sara decided.

“She did not say that.”

“She moved one leg. That is a very short answer.”

Lottie found a leaf and guided the spider onto it. They carried the leaf to a safe corner of the shed.

“Now she has a new garden,” Lottie said.

They filled the pot with soil. Sara gave Lottie a bean seed. It was not a real memory tree, but it could grow.

“What will we call it?” Sara asked.

“Mama.”

“A tree needs a name that helps it know what to do.”

“Mama helps me know what to do.”

Sara pressed the seed into the soil. “Then Mama is a good name.”

Lottie watered it. She used too much water and made a muddy river on the floor.

“The seed may learn to swim,” Sara said.

“It is a strong seed.”

They placed the pot near a window. Lottie checked it every hour.

“It has not grown,” she announced.

“Seeds are shy.”

“It should not be shy. It has a job.”

“Growth takes time.”

“I do not like time.”

“Time does not always ask what we like.”

Lottie crossed her arms. “I will watch it.”

She watched for ten minutes. Then a butterfly passed the window. Lottie followed it outside and forgot the seed for nearly an hour.

When she returned, the seed had not grown. But Lottie had learned that waiting could include other things.

### The Garden Game

Sara made a game for Lottie. Each day, Lottie could choose one thing to notice in the garden. She could notice a leaf, a bird, a cloud, or a stone. She could not choose the same thing two days in a row.

“Why not choose Mama?” Lottie asked.

“You may think about Mama every day. The game is for your eyes.”

On the first day, Lottie chose a yellow leaf. On the second, she chose a blackbird. On the third, she chose a cloud that looked like a hat.

“It is Papa’s hat,” Sara said.

Lottie stared at the cloud. “How do you know?”

“It has a serious shape.”

“Papa’s hat was brown.”

“Clouds do not always use the right color.”

On the fourth day, Lottie chose a stone. She carried it to the memory garden.

“This is a walk with Mama,” she said.

“Was she with you when you found it?”

“I do not remember.”

“Then it may be a walk you wish to remember.”

Lottie placed it in the box. She did not need every memory to be perfect. A small object could hold a feeling even when the exact day was gone.

### A Difficult Question

One afternoon, Lottie asked, “Did Mama leave because I was bad?”

Sara sat beside her. This was not a question that a funny story could answer.

“No.”

“Did she leave because she did not love me?”

“No.”

“Then why?”

Sara took a breath. “Sometimes people die because bodies can stop working. It is not the child’s fault.”

Lottie looked at the memory box. “Can bodies start again?”

“Not after death.”

Lottie’s eyes filled with tears.

Sara held her hand. “I wish I could tell you a different answer. I cannot. But I can tell you that your mother’s love was real. A person can be gone and still have given love that remains.”

“Where does it remain?”

“In memories. In stories. In the way people care for you.”

Lottie leaned against her. “In you?”

“In me too, because you have told me about her.”

Lottie placed her head on Sara’s shoulder. She cried for a long time. Sara did not rush her.

When the crying became softer, Sara said, “Would you like to put something in the box?”

Lottie took a small button. “This one is from Mama’s coat.”

“Then it can stay.”

“Will it remember her?”

“It will help us remember her.”

That difference mattered. Sara could not promise that a button had a mind. She could promise that people could give meaning to a thing.

### Lottie Teaches a Story

After that day, Lottie began telling her own stories. They were not always easy to follow.

“Once there was a princess,” Lottie said.

“What was her name?”

“Lottie.”

“That is convenient.”

“She lived in a house with no mother.”

Sara listened carefully.

“She found a garden. The garden had a tree. The tree had a mother inside.”

“What did the mother do?”

“She watched.”

“From the leaves?”

“From everywhere.”

Lottie pointed at Sara. “Then the princess found Sara.”

“What did Sara do?”

“She sat on the floor.”

“A very useful action.”

“Then she gave the princess a pillow.”

“Also useful.”

“Then the princess stopped being alone.”

The story ended there. It was not a story about bringing the dead back. It was a story about a living child finding a living friend.

Sara told Lottie that she liked it.

“It needs a dragon,” Lottie said.

“What does the dragon do?”

“It guards the cake.”

“The cake is safe.”

“The dragon must be busy.”

### The Storm

One night, a storm shook the school. Thunder rolled over the roof. Lottie woke and began to cry.

Sara went to her room. She carried a candle and Emily.

“The storm is too loud,” Lottie said.

“It is a very large drum.”

“It may break the house.”

“The house has stood through many storms.”

“How do you know?”

“The walls told me.”

Another thunderclap made Lottie jump.

Sara placed a blanket over two chairs. “We are going into the tent.”

Lottie crawled inside. Sara brought the memory box. They sat under the blanket while rain struck the window.

“Tell a story,” Lottie said.

Sara told a story about a cloud who wanted to become a drum. The cloud practiced over the sea. It made thunder so loud that every fish wore ear covers.

“Fish do not have ears,” Lottie said.

“These fish had excellent ears.”

“That is impossible.”

“It was a special cloud.”

Lottie began to laugh. The thunder rolled again, but it no longer owned the room.

When the storm ended, Lottie was asleep. Sara placed the memory box beside her bed and removed the blanket tent.

The next morning, Lottie asked whether the cloud had apologized to the fish.

“It sent them a basket of cake.”

“Then it was a good cloud.”

### A Careful Goodbye

Sara had to leave the school for one afternoon. Lottie held her sleeve.

“Do not go.”

“I will come back before supper.”

“What if you cannot?”

“Then I will send word. I will not disappear without telling you.”

Lottie looked at the memory box. “You made a promise.”

“A true promise.”

Sara returned before supper. Lottie waited by the door.

“You came back.”

“I said I would.”

“The promise worked.”

“Promises need people to do the work.”

Lottie thought about this. “I can make a promise too.”

“What will you promise?”

“I will tell someone when I am sad.”

Sara hugged her. It was a small promise, but it gave Lottie a way to ask for help.

The bean seed eventually made a green shoot. Lottie called it a tree. Sara did not correct her. Some things could be small and still deserve a grand name.

## Chapter 5 - Becky

Becky worked in the school. She carried coal, washed dishes, cleaned rooms, and ran up and down the stairs all day.

She was young, but her hands were rough and her eyes were always tired. Her bed was in a small attic room. In winter, the room was cold enough to make the water in a cup feel like ice.

One afternoon, Sara returned to her room and found Becky asleep on the floor near the fire. Becky woke at once and jumped to her feet.

“Please do not tell Miss Minchin!” she cried. “I was only resting for a minute.”

Sara looked at the girl’s pale face. “You were not doing any harm. You were tired.”

“She will say I am lazy.”

“Then she will be wrong.”

Sara went to the table. There was a plate of bread and cake that she had not eaten.

“Take this,” she said.

Becky stared at the food. “I cannot.”

“You can. I am giving it to you.”

Becky held the plate with both hands. She ate slowly at first, then quickly, because her stomach had been waiting a long time.

“You speak to me like I am a person,” Becky said.

Sara looked puzzled. “You are a person.”

Becky’s eyes filled with tears. Nobody at the school had said it so simply.

Sara began to tell stories while Becky worked. They pretended the attic was a palace. The school was a great kingdom, Becky was a princess in disguise, and Sara was her royal friend.

“I am afraid this princess has a very dirty apron,” Becky said one day.

“That is because she has defeated three mountains of washing,” Sara replied.

Becky laughed. Her laugh sounded strange at first, as if it had been kept in a box.

Soon Becky began to wait for Sara’s stories. Sara never forgot that Becky had real work and real hunger. Their game did not hide the truth, but it gave them courage to face it together.

Sara had money and a warm room then, but she did not think that made her more important. In Becky, she found a true friend.

### The Kitchen Clock

Becky’s day followed the kitchen clock. She rose when the sky was still dark. She carried coal before the cooks arrived. She washed plates while the school slept. When the first bell rang, she was already tired.

Sara began to notice the sounds that came from below her room. There was the heavy lift of a bucket. There was the slap of a wet cloth. There was the squeak of a stair that needed oil.

One morning, Sara went downstairs to ask for a book and found Becky carrying a basket of clean sheets.

“That basket is taller than you,” Sara said.

“It is not. I am shorter than the basket.”

“That is the basket’s opinion.”

Becky shifted the sheets. “I must take these to the upper rooms.”

“May I help?”

Becky looked alarmed. “No. If Miss Minchin sees you carrying sheets, she will say I have made you work.”

“I can tell her that the sheets asked me to help.”

“She will not believe you.”

“The sheets have no witnesses.”

Becky almost smiled, but she shook her head. “You must go to your lesson.”

Sara stepped aside. “Then I will walk beside you and tell you a story.”

They climbed the stairs. Sara told a story about a queen who owned a palace with five hundred beds. Every morning, the queen inspected each bed and asked whether the pillows were comfortable.

“The queen sounds lazy,” Becky said.

“She has people to make the beds.”

“Then she is not like me.”

“No. You are the queen who knows how to make the palace work.”

Becky stopped at the top of the stairs. “I am not a queen.”

“Not today.”

“Will there be a crown?”

“A crown made of soap bubbles.”

“It would not last.”

“Then you must enjoy it quickly.”

Becky laughed. The basket wobbled. Sara held it until Becky found her balance.

### The Attic Room

Sara visited Becky’s room for the first time with permission. It was smaller than Sara’s room and had one narrow bed. A sloping wall made it hard to stand straight near the window.

“This is where I sleep,” Becky said.

“It is a room for people who enjoy bumping their heads.”

“I have become very good at it.”

The room held a little tin cup, a wooden box, two folded dresses, and a pair of shoes. A candle stood on the table. Its flame shook whenever the door opened.

Sara looked at the window. The glass was cloudy, but she could see rooftops and a slice of sky.

“You can see the stars here.”

“When the clouds go away.”

“We should ask them to visit more often.”

Becky opened the wooden box. Inside were three buttons, a piece of blue thread, and a picture cut from a newspaper.

“Who is that?” Sara asked.

“I do not know. She is wearing a fine dress.”

“Perhaps she is waiting for a better picture.”

“Perhaps she is a queen.”

Sara placed the picture beside the buttons. “Then your box is already royal.”

Becky looked at Sara’s hands. “You have never been in a room like this.”

“Not exactly. I slept in a tent once.”

“Was it cold?”

“Yes. But my father brought hot tea.”

“That makes a difference.”

“It does.”

Sara did not say that the attic was not a tent and that hot tea would not solve everything. She understood that Becky did not need a speech. She needed someone who could sit in the room without looking away.

They made a small flag from the blue thread and a scrap of paper. Sara drew a crown on it.

“This is the flag of the attic kingdom,” she said.

“What is our first law?”

Sara thought. “No person may be called lazy before she has had enough sleep.”

Becky nodded solemnly. “A very good law.”

“Second law: all cups must be washed before they become lonely.”

“That is not a law. That is work.”

“In our kingdom, work can be a law and a joke.”

Becky hung the flag above the bed. It was crooked. Sara said that a royal flag should lean slightly because straight flags became proud.

### Reading the Labels

Becky could read only a few words. She knew the names of rooms because she had heard them. She could not always read a written message.

Sara brought a simple book to the attic. She did not begin with long stories. She began with labels from the kitchen.

“This says flour,” Sara explained.

“I know the flour by the white dust.”

“The word is another way to know it.”

They wrote the words for flour, bread, coal, water, and plate. Sara drew a little picture beside each word.

Becky copied the letters. Her B leaned sideways. Her P looked like a small flag.

“My letters are running away,” she said.

“They are in a hurry to learn.”

“They will fall off the page.”

Sara drew a line under them. “Now they have a road.”

Becky wrote the words again. She made fewer mistakes.

“Read this one,” Sara said.

Becky looked at the page. “Bread.”

“Yes.”

“That is my favorite word.”

“It is a useful word.”

“It is a beautiful word.”

Sara wrote another: friend.

Becky sounded it out slowly. “Friend.”

“That one is more difficult.”

“It has letters in a strange order.”

“Friends are sometimes strange. The word is practicing.”

Becky read it again. Then she looked at Sara.

“Is it true?”

“What?”

“That we are friends.”

Sara closed the book. “Yes.”

Becky touched the word. She copied it on a clean piece of paper and put the paper in her wooden box.

### The Missing Key

Becky was sent to find a small brass key one afternoon. The key opened a cupboard where extra blankets were kept. She searched the kitchen, the hall, and the schoolroom. Miss Minchin became angry because a cupboard could not be opened without its key, and the cupboard did not seem to understand the problem.

“If I do not find it, I will be punished,” Becky said.

Sara helped her remember the last place she had held it.

“I carried a basket from the office. Then I took coal to the small room. Then I washed the cups.”

“Did you set the key down?”

“Perhaps.”

“Where did you set the basket?”

“Near the cat.”

“The chalk cat?”

“No, a real cat. It lives near the kitchen.”

They found the cat asleep inside a basket. Its tail hung over the side like a piece of rope.

“Did the cat take the key?” Sara asked.

The cat opened one eye and closed it again.

“He refuses to answer,” Becky said.

Sara looked around. The key was not in the basket, but a bright mark showed on the floor. The cat had pushed something under a cupboard.

Becky lay flat and reached beneath it. Her fingers touched the key.

“The cat did take it,” she said.

“Perhaps he wanted to open the cupboard.”

“What would he put inside?”

“More cats.”

Becky laughed so suddenly that the real cat jumped out of the basket and ran away. She held up the key.

“We found it.”

Miss Minchin took the key and said, “You should have found it sooner.”

Sara watched Becky lower her head.

“The cat had hidden it,” Sara said.

Miss Minchin gave her a cold look. “Cats do not use cupboards.”

“This one may be planning a kitchen.”

Miss Minchin turned away. Becky looked at Sara with wide eyes.

“You must not say things like that.”

“I did not say the cat was innocent.”

“You said he was planning.”

“He looked very serious.”

Becky hid a smile in her apron.

### The Torn Apron

On another day, Becky tore her apron on a nail. She tried to hide the tear, but Miss Minchin noticed it.

“Careless girl,” she said. “You will mend it tonight.”

Becky nodded. Her shoulders bent.

Sara found her later with a needle. Becky’s fingers were tired, and the thread kept slipping.

“The hole is winning,” Becky said.

“Then we must surround it.”

Sara showed her how to place small stitches around the tear. Becky copied her. The first line was crooked. The second was better. By the third, the cloth held together.

“It looks like a path,” Becky said.

“A path from one side to the other.”

“A bridge.”

“Exactly.”

Becky stared at the repaired apron. “You always make things into something else.”

“Only when they are sad.”

“The apron is not sad.”

“It has been pulled apart.”

“That does not mean it has feelings.”

“You have never asked it.”

Becky shook her head. “You are a strange girl.”

“My father says that is better than being a boring one.”

Sara gave Becky a small blue thread from her sewing basket. Becky added one stitch in the shape of a tiny star.

“Now it is ours,” Becky said.

The apron was still old. The repair did not make it new. But Becky wore it with a little more pride. A person could mend cloth and also mend the way she felt inside.

### The Princess Rules

Sara and Becky made more rules for their attic kingdom. They wrote them on the back of a food list.

Rule one: A person who is tired may sit for one minute.

Rule two: A person who is hungry may say so.

Rule three: A story may begin in a kitchen and end on the moon.

Rule four: A royal friend must share the last good crumb.

Rule five: No one may call a servant invisible.

Becky read the rules aloud. She stumbled over invisible.

“In-vis-i-ble,” Sara said.

“It is a long word.”

“It is a long problem too.”

“What does it mean?”

“It means people act as if they cannot see you.”

Becky looked at the page. “Miss Minchin sees me when something is dirty.”

“Then she sees the work, but not always the person.”

Becky folded the paper. “You see me.”

Sara answered at once. “Yes.”

They placed the rules beside the crooked flag. The kingdom was small, but its laws were kinder than many laws in large places.

### An Evening Story

One evening, Sara had finished her lessons but did not want to be alone. She carried Emily to Becky’s room. Becky was mending a sock.

“Tell me a story,” Becky said.

“What kind?”

“One with a palace.”

“And a problem?”

“A very large problem.”

Sara began. A princess lived in a palace that had no windows. The people outside thought she had everything, but she could not see the sky. One day, a servant girl found a loose stone in the wall. She and the princess worked together to open a small window.

“What did they see?” Becky asked.

“A roof, a chimney, and a pigeon.”

“That is not a grand view.”

“The pigeon wore a golden ring.”

“Better.”

The pigeon carried messages. It flew to gardens and markets and returned with news. The princess learned that the people outside needed help. She used her palace stores to send bread, blankets, and books through the little window.

“Did the princess leave the palace?” Becky asked.

“Not at first. She had to learn how to open more windows.”

“Who taught her?”

“The servant girl.”

“Then the servant girl was clever.”

“Very clever.”

Becky stopped sewing. “Did the princess call her a servant?”

“She called her friend.”

Becky nodded. “That is the right word.”

The story ended with the palace doors open. Sara did not say that she was thinking about the school. She only knew that stories could build a place where two people were seen clearly.

When Sara returned to her room, Becky stood at the attic window. She held the piece of paper with the word friend.

“Good night, Princess,” Becky called.

Sara turned. “Good night, Royal Friend.”

The words traveled across the narrow hall. For a few minutes, the school seemed less like a place of rules and more like a place that held two small kingdoms.

### Becky’s Five Minutes

Becky wanted to learn more, but the kitchen clock did not leave much time. Sara suggested five minutes after breakfast and five minutes before bed.

“Ten minutes is not a school,” Becky said.

“It is a door into one.”

The first morning, Sara showed her how to read the clock. The long hand pointed to twelve. The short hand pointed to six.

“Six o’clock,” Sara said.

Becky looked at the clock. “It is too early for the clock to be awake.”

“The clock has a very responsible character.”

They practiced five-minute pieces. Becky counted plates. Sara wrote the numbers. Then Becky read a line from the garden book.

“The garden is green,” she said.

“Yes.”

“The garden is not the attic.”

“Also yes.”

“The garden has no coal.”

“It may have flowers instead.”

Becky read the line again. She made a mistake, corrected it, and kept going.

On the second evening, the lesson was about letters. Sara wrote B for Becky, S for Sara, and E for Emily.

“Emily’s letter is easy,” Becky said.

“She will be pleased.”

“She has three lines in her name.”

“That is because she likes decoration.”

Becky copied the letters onto a scrap of paper. She wrote B, then S, then E. At the bottom, she wrote F for friend.

“That letter has a strange shape,” Sara said.

“It keeps falling over.”

“Friends sometimes need support.”

Becky placed the paper in her box. The box now held buttons, the newspaper picture, the paper star, and a word she had written herself.

### The Kitchen Princess’s Choice

A younger girl dropped a plate in the kitchen. The sound made everyone turn.

“I am sorry,” the girl cried.

The cook reached for a cloth. Becky stepped forward.

“Do not move. There may be pieces.”

She swept the sharp pieces into a pan and checked the floor with her hand held safely above it.

“The girl could have cut her foot,” Becky told the cook.

The cook nodded. “Good thinking.”

Becky seemed surprised that someone had praised her.

Sara heard about it later.

“You were a kitchen princess.”

“I was doing my work.”

“A princess can do work and still be a princess.”

“You say that often.”

“Some truths need repeating.”

Becky looked at the paper with the word friend. “I do not think I am clever.”

“Clever is not only knowing lessons. It is also noticing danger, helping someone, and trying again.”

Becky considered this. “Then I may be a little clever.”

“A little clever can grow.”

The kitchen clock rang. Becky had to return to work. Sara watched her go. The five-minute lessons did not change Becky’s position at the school, but they changed what Becky believed she could become.

On the following afternoon, Becky arrived with a question hidden under her apron. She pulled out a small piece of paper.

“I found this in the bin,” she said. “It has a word on it, but I cannot read the middle.”

Sara held it near the window. “It says ‘courage.’”

Becky frowned. “That is a big word for a little piece of paper.”

“Big words can live in little places.”

Becky traced the letters with one finger. “Courage. Is it what a princess has?”

“Sometimes. It is also what a kitchen girl has when she keeps going after a hard day.”

Becky looked at the word again. “Then I will keep it.”

Sara folded the paper neatly. Becky placed it inside her pocket, next to a button and a piece of string. Her pocket now held three useful things and one brave word.

Becky touched the pocket before she went downstairs. “I will read it again tonight,” she promised.

“Then tonight will have a job,” Sara said.

“And tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow can have another one.”

Becky smiled and hurried down the stairs. The brave word stayed safely in her pocket.

## Chapter 6 - The Diamond Mines

Captain Crewe visited Sara whenever he could. He brought books, sweets, and news from India.

One day, he came with a secret smile.

“I have news about a business plan,” he said. “An old friend and I have invested money in diamond mines.”

Sara listened carefully. “Are diamonds pretty?”

“Very pretty.”

“Are the mines safe?”

“We believe they will do well. If the stones are found, the business may make us very rich.”

Sara touched the cover of her book. She did not care much about becoming richer. She cared about the smile on her father’s face.

“Then I hope your friend is happy too,” she said.

“He is a good man,” Captain Crewe answered. “His name is Mr. Carrisford. We have trusted each other for years.”

Sara imagined a hot place in India, with red earth, tall trees, and men looking for shining stones. She imagined her father and Mr. Carrisford opening a box of diamonds.

“If you find a very large one,” she said, “please do not forget Emily.”

Captain Crewe laughed. “Emily shall have the safest diamond in London.”

Miss Minchin heard about the mines from a visitor. Her smile became wider than before.

“Dear Sara,” she said, “you may become one of the richest young ladies in the world.”

Sara did not answer. She had noticed that Miss Minchin never spoke about money when her father was not near.

“I would rather have my father come to dinner,” Sara said.

Miss Minchin blinked. “Of course. But wealth is a wonderful thing.”

Sara knew that wealth could buy books and food. She also knew that it could not make a lonely person feel loved.

The diamond mines seemed to promise a bright future. Nobody at the school guessed that the news would soon bring a terrible change.

### A Map of the Mine

Captain Crewe visited Sara the following Sunday. He brought a small map in a leather folder. Sara spread it on the carpet. The map showed mountains, rivers, roads, and a red mark far from the coast.

“Is the diamond mine here?” she asked.

“Near that mark.”

“How do people get there?”

“By train for part of the journey, then by cart. Some roads are rough.”

Sara traced the road with one finger. “Does Mr. Carrisford go too?”

“He may travel there when the work begins.”

“Does he know the road?”

“He knows India very well.”

Sara imagined the two men bumping over a road in a cart. “Would the diamonds fall out if the cart jumped?”

“They would not be in the cart until they had been found.”

“That is wise. A cart full of diamonds would be too proud to fit through a door.”

Captain Crewe laughed. “You ask practical questions.”

“I ask questions that worry me.”

He folded the map. “The business is a risk. We may lose money.”

Sara looked at him. “Would you still like the work if there were no diamonds?”

Captain Crewe thought. “I like building something with a friend. The money matters, but it is not everything.”

“Then you and Mr. Carrisford are doing two things: looking for stones and making a plan together.”

“Yes.”

“The second thing sounds more important.”

Her father kissed her hair. “You understand more than most adults.”

“I do not understand why adults use so many words when three will do.”

“Which three?”

“Friend, bread, and come home.”

Captain Crewe became quiet. Sara knew she had touched the sadness beneath his smile.

“I will come home soon,” he promised.

“Soon means before dinner or after dinner?”

“After some work.”

“That is not a proper time.”

“I will write.”

“That is better.”

He gave her the map to keep for a week. Sara placed it beside her books. She did not care to own a diamond. She liked knowing where her father was going and why.

### Miss Minchin Makes a Calculation

Miss Minchin learned more about the investment from Captain Crewe’s letters. She began to calculate what Sara might be worth in the future.

She ordered a new dress for Sara. Then she ordered a second one. She told the seamstress to use fine cloth.

“Does Sara need two new dresses?” the seamstress asked.

“Sara is a very important pupil.”

“She has plenty of dresses.”

“A child of her position must appear properly dressed.”

Miss Minchin did not say that she wanted visitors to see the dresses. She did not say that a rich father made the school look successful.

Sara noticed the new clothes.

“Are these for a special day?” she asked.

“You should be grateful,” Miss Minchin said.

“I am grateful for the cloth. Did someone choose the color?”

“I did.”

The dress was a deep purple. Sara liked it, but she did not want to wear it if another girl had no warm coat.

“Could the seamstress make a coat for Jessie too?”

Miss Minchin stared. “This dress is for you.”

“Yes. But the cloth is already here.”

“Do not concern yourself with school clothes.”

Sara did not argue. She only remembered the answer.

Later, she saw Jessie rubbing her hands near the fire. Sara gave her a pair of wool gloves from an older drawer. They were too large, but Jessie folded the fingers and wore them.

“Miss Minchin may not like it,” Ermengarde warned.

“The gloves do not belong to Miss Minchin.”

“Everything in this school seems to belong to Miss Minchin.”

Sara looked at the gloves. “Perhaps not everything.”

### Captain Crewe’s Questions

During his next visit, Captain Crewe asked Sara what she wanted for her birthday. Sara was eleven now, and Miss Minchin had already begun planning a large party.

“Would you like a necklace?” he asked.

“I would like a story.”

“A story can be arranged.”

“And a book about India.”

“That too.”

“And could you stay through tea?”

Captain Crewe looked toward the clock. “I can stay through tea and perhaps a little longer.”

“Then I have everything.”

He tried to hide his worry. His work required travel, and he knew he might soon need to leave. Sara saw the worry and asked about it.

“Are you frightened of the diamond mine?”

“I am careful about it.”

“Careful is a kind of frightened person who carries a notebook.”

He smiled. “Perhaps.”

Sara opened the notebook on his knee. It held numbers and notes.

“What do these mean?”

“Costs, tools, travel, and shares.”

“What is a share?”

“A part of the business.”

“If there are ten shares, does each share get one diamond?”

“Not exactly.”

“Then the diamonds need a better teacher.”

Captain Crewe explained that he and Mr. Carrisford would share both the risk and the result. If the mines failed, they would lose money together. If the mines succeeded, they would gain money together.

“That sounds fair,” Sara said.

“It is meant to be.”

“Then remember that a fair plan must also remember people who cannot pay.”

Captain Crewe looked at her. “Where did you learn that?”

“From watching people.”

He placed the notebook away. “I will remember.”

### Preparing the Birthday

Miss Minchin gave Sara a list of party plans. There would be a table for cakes, a table for gifts, and a game in which the girls searched for small paper stars.

“You must thank the guests warmly,” Miss Minchin said.

“I will.”

“You must look happy.”

“I will try.”

“You must not speak about dull subjects.”

Sara wondered which subjects Miss Minchin considered dull. She wrote a private list: books, India, fathers, maps, French frogs, and whether people were kind.

“What should I speak about?” Sara asked.

“Your dress. Your gifts. Your future.”

“My future has not told me what it wants yet.”

Miss Minchin sighed. “You make everything difficult.”

“I am trying to make it true.”

The school girls helped decorate the room. Ermengarde tied ribbons around chairs. Jessie cut paper stars. Lottie put one star into her pocket and forgot it there.

“Lottie, you have stolen the sky,” Sara said.

Lottie looked alarmed. “I did not.”

Sara pulled the star from the pocket. “Then the sky has followed you.”

Lottie smiled and put it back.

Becky carried plates into the room. Sara noticed that she had a tired look.

“Will you see the party?” Sara asked.

“Only from the kitchen.”

Sara wanted Becky to sit with the guests, but she knew Miss Minchin would not allow it. She saved one paper star and wrote Becky’s name on the back.

“This is for the princess who keeps the palace running,” Sara said.

Becky pressed the star to her apron. “I have never had a party star.”

“Every kingdom needs a first one.”

### A Conversation about Riches

That night, Sara and Ermengarde sat beside the window. The school was quiet.

“If your father becomes very rich, will you change?” Ermengarde asked.

“I do not think so.”

“You could have anything.”

“Not anything.”

“What could you not have?”

“A day that has already passed. A person who does not love me. A mother who is gone.”

Ermengarde was silent.

Sara continued, “Money can buy a warm room. It cannot tell you who will sit in it.”

“I would like a warm room.”

“So would I.”

“And cakes.”

“Yes. A warm room with cakes is a sensible dream.”

Ermengarde thought of the gloves Sara had given Jessie. “Would you give away all your money?”

“No. I would keep enough to live. But if I had more than I needed, I would look for someone who needed it.”

“That sounds like work.”

“Being a good princess is work.”

“What about a bad princess?”

“She may sit on a chair and wait for everyone else to work.”

Ermengarde put on an important face and lifted one hand. “Bring me seven cakes.”

Sara bowed. “The kitchen has declared independence.”

They laughed quietly so Miss Minchin would not hear.

The birthday was close. The party decorations were ready. Captain Crewe’s letter was on its way. Sara thought the future was a room with a door, and she expected her father to open it.

### The Last Visit Before the Party

Captain Crewe came to the school on the afternoon before Sara’s birthday. He looked tired from travel, but he carried a small parcel.

“It is not a diamond,” he said before Sara could ask.

“I was not going to ask.”

“You were looking at the parcel.”

“Emily was looking at it.”

Captain Crewe opened the paper. Inside was a little brass compass with a glass top.

Sara held it in her palm. The needle trembled and turned north.

“It points somewhere,” she said.

“It helps a traveler find a direction.”

“Can it find home?”

“It can help you travel toward home. The rest is up to the traveler.”

Sara placed the compass beside the map of the diamond mine. “Will you take it with you?”

“I bought it for you.”

“Then I will use it to find you.”

He bent down and kissed her forehead. “You will always know where my heart is.”

“That is not on the compass.”

“No. It is in you.”

Sara wanted to ask when he would return. She had asked before, and he had answered as well as he could. This time she asked a different question.

“Are you ready to go?”

“Not quite.”

“What do you need?”

“A good meal, a warm coat, and a daughter who will not worry too much.”

“I can arrange two of those things.”

“Which two?”

“The meal and the daughter. The coat belongs to you.”

He laughed. They went to the dining room. Sara made sure he had tea, bread, and a piece of cake. She told him about Monsieur Dufarge’s French frog and Ermengarde’s king.

Captain Crewe listened to every word. When Sara mentioned the birthday party, he looked at the clock.

“I may not be able to stay tomorrow.”

Sara’s fingers tightened around her cup.

“You have work in India?”

“I may need to leave sooner than I expected.”

“Will the diamonds be lonely without you?”

“They may not notice. Stones are poor company.”

“Then come back quickly.”

“I will come back as soon as I can.”

She did not ask him to promise a day. A promise with no date could still be true, but it could also make waiting sharp.

Before he left, Sara gave him the map she had kept. “Take this. It may help.”

“You are giving it back?”

“You need it more.”

He folded it and put it inside his coat. “I will keep it safe.”

Sara touched the brass compass. “And I will keep this safe.”

They stood together until a servant called that Captain Crewe’s carriage had come. Sara walked with him to the door.

“Tomorrow, even if you are not here, I will have a birthday,” she said.

“You must.”

“And I will think of you.”

“I will think of you too.”

Sara watched the carriage move away. She felt a small worry enter the room, but she did not let it take all the chairs. She put the compass in her pocket and returned to the decorations.

### Paper Stars for Everyone

Sara decided that the birthday game should include Becky, though Becky could not join the party. She cut paper stars and wrote kind words on them.

For Ermengarde, she wrote: You try again.

For Jessie, she wrote: You remember gloves.

For Lottie, she wrote: You can share a cake.

For Becky, she wrote: You keep the palace warm.

For Miss Minchin, she started to write: You have a very large key ring. She thought about it and chose a kinder line: You run a busy school.

“That is not as funny,” Ermengarde said.

“It is safer.”

Ermengarde cut a star for Sara. She wrote: You make hard days less hard.

Sara read the words and felt shy. She placed the star in her book.

Lottie wanted to write something too. She held the pencil upside down and made three dark marks.

“What does it say?” Sara asked.

“It says Sara is a princess.”

“It is very long.”

“Princess is a long word.”

Sara helped her draw a crown. Lottie drew the crown sideways.

“It is falling,” Sara said.

“No. It is dancing.”

Sara had heard that before. She liked how ideas could travel from one child to another and become new.

### Mr. Carrisford’s Name

Miss Minchin told Sara that one of the school’s visitors had asked about the diamond mines. She said the visitor was a business man who knew Captain Crewe.

“What is his name?” Sara asked.

“Mr. Carrisford.”

Sara repeated it. “Carrisford.”

“Have you heard of him?”

“My father spoke about a friend, but I do not know whether it was him.”

Miss Minchin leaned closer. “You must remember such names. Your future may depend on them.”

Sara did not like the sound of that. “My future should not depend on whether I remember a name.”

“You are too young to understand business.”

“Perhaps. But I understand when someone is being kind.”

Miss Minchin looked away. “You should be more interested in your position.”

Sara thought about the compass, the map, and her father’s tired smile. “I am interested in people.”

Miss Minchin did not answer. She left Sara with the paper stars.

That night, Sara wondered why the name Mr. Carrisford felt like a door she had almost seen. She asked Emily whether her father had mentioned it.

“Emily remembers everything,” Sara said. “She simply keeps her information private.”

Emily looked toward the window.

“Yes,” Sara said. “She believes we should wait.”

### The Party Table

The party room was ready by nightfall. Miss Minchin had ordered flowers, ribbons, and a cake with eleven candles. The cake was so tall that Sara thought it needed a small ladder.

“Who will cut it?” Ermengarde asked.

“Perhaps the cake will cut us,” Jessie said.

Sara placed the paper stars under the plates. Each girl would find one during tea.

Becky came in to carry a tray. Sara slipped her star into the pocket of Becky’s apron.

“What is this?” Becky asked.

“A secret.”

Becky touched the paper. “Secrets are dangerous.”

“This one is only a sentence.”

“Sentences can be dangerous too.”

“Then read it when you are alone.”

Becky gave Sara a quick smile and hurried away.

Sara looked at the cake. She wanted her father to see it. She wanted him to laugh at the candles and pretend the cake was too large for the room.

She placed a small chair near the table, although no one needed it. Ermengarde noticed.

“That chair is empty.”

“It is waiting.”

“For Captain Crewe?”

Sara nodded.

Ermengarde did not say that he might not come. She moved a paper flower onto the chair.

“Then it has a decoration,” she said.

Sara smiled. A decorated empty chair was still empty, but it did not have to look forgotten.

The bell rang for bedtime. Sara put the brass compass under her pillow. It pointed north all night, even when she dreamed of India, London, and a road that went through a mountain of shining stones.

The next morning, Captain Crewe found Sara sitting beside a pile of colored stones. She had arranged them from dull gray to bright gold.

“Is that another map?” he asked.

“It is a map of how a plan feels,” Sara said. “Gray means we do not know much. Yellow means we have an idea. Gold means we must be careful, because the idea may be exciting.”

Her father laughed. “That is a map I should carry in my pocket.”

He chose a red stone. “What does red mean?”

“Stop and ask a question.”

“Very useful.”

Sara put the red stone beside the compass. She did not know that the work ahead would need every color. For now, the stones made a bright little road across the table, and her father was still beside her.

Captain Crewe placed the red stone in Sara's hand. “Keep this for the questions,” he said.

Sara closed her fingers around it. A good plan could be exciting, but it also needed patience. She liked that her father trusted her with both the shining stones and the serious ones.

## Chapter 7 - The Diamond Mines Again

Sara’s eleventh birthday came on a cold London day. Miss Minchin planned a grand party because she wanted to impress Captain Crewe and his friends.

There were flowers, cakes, games, and gifts. Sara wore a beautiful dress. Emily sat at the head of a small table, looking calm while the girls made a great deal of noise.

“This is the best birthday ever,” Ermengarde said.

Sara smiled, but she kept looking toward the door. She hoped her father would come in with a birthday kiss.

Instead, a serious lawyer arrived. He asked to speak with Miss Minchin. His face was gray.

Captain Crewe was dead. He had become ill in India, and the illness had taken him quickly. The diamond business had also seemed to fail. Captain Crewe had lost his money, and his friend Mr. Carrisford had disappeared during the trouble.

Sara stood very still. The party sounds seemed to travel far away.

Miss Minchin looked at the lawyer. “What is to become of the child?”

The lawyer explained that Sara had no money that anyone could find. He could not promise payment for her room, clothes, or lessons.

Miss Minchin’s warm smile vanished.

“I have spent a great deal on her,” she said. “I will not spend more.”

Sara heard every word. Her father had died, and now the school wanted to count the cost of keeping her.

Miss Minchin could not send Sara into the street without damaging the school’s name. So she made another choice. Sara would stay, but she would work as a servant.

Her fine dresses were taken away. Her books and toys were locked up. Emily was left with her, but even the doll’s clothes were old and plain.

Sara was sent to a cold attic beside Becky’s room.

“You will earn your food now,” Miss Minchin said.

Sara held Emily close. “I will try.”

That night, she cried into her pillow. Then she sat up and told herself that tears were not the only thing she could give to the dark room.

She had lost her father, her home, her clothes, and her place at the school. She had not yet lost herself.

### The Empty Party Room

For a little while after Sara climbed to the attic, the party room remained full of flowers. The candles had burned down. The cake stood untouched on the table. One paper star had fallen beside the empty chair.

Miss Minchin walked through the room with a notebook. She counted the plates, the candles, the flowers, and the pieces of cake. She wrote each number carefully.

“What are you doing?” the cook asked.

“Making a record.”

“It was a birthday party.”

“It was an expensive birthday party.”

The cook looked at the empty chair. “The child has lost her father.”

“The child has also cost this school a great deal.”

The cook said nothing. She picked up the paper star with Becky’s name on it. She had found it on the floor near the tray.

“That belongs to Becky,” she said.

“Give it to her.”

The cook folded the star and put it in her pocket. She did not understand why Sara had written such a thing for a servant, but she knew Becky would want it.

Upstairs, Sara sat on the narrow attic bed. Becky stood near the door and did not know whether to come in.

“You may enter,” Sara said.

“Are you sure?”

“The door is open.”

Becky came in. She held the paper star.

“You left this.”

Sara read the words on the back. You keep the palace warm.

“It is true,” Sara said.

Becky looked at the bare room. “This palace has a terrible roof.”

“It is being repaired by an invisible builder.”

“He is not working very fast.”

“Builders need supper.”

Becky gave a small smile. Then she saw Sara’s face and sat beside her.

For a few minutes, neither girl spoke. The school below them made ordinary sounds. A plate broke. A door closed. Someone called for a maid.

“I do not know what to say,” Becky whispered.

“You do not have to say anything.”

“I can say that I am sorry.”

“Thank you.”

“I am sorry about Captain Crewe.”

Sara nodded. She held Emily in her lap. “He was kind. He knew how to make a goat leave a room without hurting its feelings.”

Becky did not laugh. Tears came into her eyes.

“You can cry,” Sara told her.

“I am not the one who lost a father.”

“You can still cry.”

Becky wiped her face with her sleeve. “I do not want you to be alone.”

“I am not alone. You are here.”

“I have to work.”

“Then I will be alone while you work, and not alone while you are here.”

Becky looked at the paper star. “I will come when I can.”

“We can make a schedule.”

“Miss Minchin makes schedules.”

“Ours will include talking.”

“And food?”

“If the kingdom has food.”

Becky glanced at the empty table. “It does not.”

Sara looked at Emily. “Then the kingdom has a serious problem.”

### The Things Sara Kept

The next morning, a maid brought Sara’s old clothes from the room downstairs. They were not the dresses Captain Crewe had chosen. They were worn clothes, a pair of plain shoes, and a coat with a missing button.

“Miss Minchin says you may keep only these,” the maid told her.

Sara looked at the small pile. “May I keep my compass?”

“I do not know.”

“May I keep the letter from my father?”

“I do not know.”

“May I keep Emily?”

“She said the doll may stay.”

Sara placed Emily on the bed. Then she searched the pockets of her old coat. She found a marble, a piece of blue thread, a pencil, and the paper star Ermengarde had given her.

“These are mine,” she said.

The maid nodded. “Keep them.”

Sara arranged the objects on the floor. The compass was for direction. The letter was for memory. The marble was round and smooth. The thread was for mending. The pencil was for thinking. The star was for friendship.

“Emily is in charge,” Sara said.

The maid asked, “In charge of what?”

“The things I must remember.”

Sara put the compass under her pillow. She folded the letter inside her book. She tied the thread around the marble so it would not roll beneath the bed. The star went beside Emily.

“You cannot carry all your old life in one room,” the maid said gently.

“No. But I can carry pieces.”

The maid looked at the child. She had seen rich children cry because a ribbon was the wrong color. Sara was crying because her father was gone, but she was also making a place for the pieces that remained.

“What shall I call you now?” the maid asked. “Miss Crewe or Sara?”

“Sara.”

“Miss Minchin says you are to work.”

“Then I will work.”

“She says you are no longer a young lady.”

Sara looked at Emily. “A title can be taken. A person cannot be taken so easily.”

The maid turned away quickly. She did not want Sara to see that her eyes had filled.

### The New List

Miss Minchin gave Sara a list of tasks. It was written on one long sheet of paper.

Carry messages.

Take parcels.

Help the younger girls dress.

Clean the schoolroom.

Dust the library.

Carry coal.

Wait for orders.

Sara read it twice.

“There is no time for lessons,” she said.

“You will learn what you need while you work.”

“Will I still have French?”

“French will not buy your food.”

“It may help me speak to people.”

“You will speak when spoken to.”

Sara folded the list. “Then I will learn from the people who speak.”

Miss Minchin frowned. “Do not make clever remarks.”

“I will make useful ones.”

The first task was to carry a note to the baker. The second was to carry a basket upstairs. The third was to find Lottie, who had hidden under a table because she had heard that Sara was no longer allowed to sit with the girls.

Sara knelt beside the table.

“Lottie, I can see your shoe.”

“I am not here.”

“Then your shoe is very lonely.”

“Where is Sara?”

“She is still Sara.”

Lottie crawled out. “Miss Minchin said you are a servant.”

“I have work to do.”

“Are you not a princess now?”

Sara considered the question. “A princess can carry a basket.”

“Can a princess clean a room?”

“If the room is dirty enough.”

“Can a princess eat porridge?”

“Only if the porridge behaves.”

Lottie touched Sara’s old sleeve. “Will you still tell stories?”

“Yes. Stories do not belong to dresses.”

Lottie followed her to the schoolroom. Sara gave her a picture to color while Sara dusted the shelves. Lottie colored the sun purple.

“The sun is yellow,” Sara said.

“It is purple today.”

“Then I hope it is not feeling ill.”

Lottie smiled. Sara finished the shelf. Work did not become easier because she made a joke, but it became less lonely.

### Ermengarde’s Visit

Ermengarde found Sara at noon. Sara was carrying a stack of books larger than her head.

“Put them down,” Ermengarde cried.

“That is the plan.”

Sara placed the books on the table. One book slid to the floor and opened at a page about kings.

Ermengarde picked it up. “They are making you work.”

“Yes.”

“I will tell my father.”

“What will he do?”

“He will be angry.”

“Being angry may not change Miss Minchin’s mind.”

“Then I will be angry too.”

“You may be angry. But do not let it choose your words.”

Ermengarde looked close to tears. “You helped me. I should help you.”

“You can.”

“How?”

“Remember me when I cannot be in the schoolroom. Tell me what you learn.”

Ermengarde nodded. “I will make a second notebook.”

“A notebook for the attic?”

“A notebook for you.”

She opened the history book and wrote the date. Then she drew a bridge. Under it, she wrote: Sara is still Sara.

Sara touched the page. “That is a good lesson.”

“I have not learned it yet,” Ermengarde said. “I am still angry.”

“You may learn it slowly.”

Before she left, Ermengarde took a small biscuit from her pocket and held it out.

Sara shook her head. “You eat it.”

“I have another.”

Ermengarde broke the biscuit in two. “Then we both have some.”

The pieces were small. Sara ate hers carefully. It was not enough for a meal, but it was enough to remind her that a friend could make a choice too.

### The First Night in the New Room

That night, Sara changed into the plain clothes. The missing button on her coat made the cold air touch her neck.

She tried to sew it, but the thread broke. She tried again and made the button sit crooked.

“It is not a straight button,” she told Emily. “It is a button who likes to look at the floor.”

She placed the compass beside the bed. It pointed north. Her father was far away in another direction, but Sara did not know which way his heart had traveled.

Becky came after her work was finished. She carried a cup of water.

“I saved it,” Becky said.

“Did you save it from a river?”

“From the kitchen.”

“That is more dangerous.”

Becky sat on the floor. She looked at the old coat.

“I can fix the button tomorrow.”

“Your hands must be tired.”

“They know how.”

Sara picked up the paper star. “You can read the back now.”

Becky read the words slowly. “You keep the palace warm.”

“You do.”

“The palace is cold.”

“A palace can be cold and still have a queen.”

“Are you the queen?”

Sara looked at Emily. “Emily believes she is.”

Becky laughed. It was a small laugh, but it filled more of the attic than the cup of water did.

They planned the next day. Sara would carry messages. Becky would find a needle. They would meet for five minutes after dinner. If Miss Minchin asked why, they would say they were discussing the government of the attic kingdom.

“Will she believe us?” Becky asked.

“No. But it is a true answer.”

Becky left. Sara climbed into bed. She cried again, but not as long as before. The tears were still there. So were the compass, the letter, the star, Emily, and the promise of a friend.

The room was poor. It was not empty.

### The First Morning as a Servant

Sara woke before the bell because the attic was cold. She folded the blanket under her chin and listened to the school wake below her.

Becky came with a small candle.

“Miss Minchin says you must carry the coal.”

“I know.”

“She says you must polish the stairs.”

“I know.”

“She says you must not sit with the girls.”

Sara looked at Emily. “Emily may sit wherever she likes.”

Becky smiled. “She has a strong position.”

Sara dressed in the old coat. The crooked button leaned toward the floor.

“Your button is still looking down,” Becky said.

“It is shy.”

They went downstairs. Miss Minchin gave Sara a cloth and a list. The list was longer than the one from the night before.

“Begin with the front hall,” she ordered.

Sara looked at the wide floor. “Does the hall know it is first?”

“Do not be foolish.”

Sara began to polish. Her knees hurt, and the cloth became dark with dust. A girl crossed the hall without seeing her.

Sara whispered, “Good morning.”

The girl stopped. “Are you cleaning?”

“The floor and I are becoming better acquainted.”

The girl looked uncomfortable. “I am sorry.”

“You did not make the floor dirty.”

“No, but I did not say anything yesterday.”

Sara kept polishing. “You are saying something now.”

The girl’s name was Jessie. She helped carry the empty bucket to the kitchen. It was a small help, but it made the bucket lighter.

At breakfast, Sara was not given a place at the pupil’s table. She ate a small piece of bread near the kitchen door. Becky stood beside her.

“You may have my butter,” Becky said.

“You need it.”

“I have a plan.”

“Your plan is to share everything.”

“It is not a bad plan.”

Sara broke the bread in half. They shared it.

Lottie appeared in the doorway. “Why are you there?”

“I have a new job.”

“I do not like your new job.”

“Neither does the floor.”

Lottie looked at the cloth. “Can I help?”

Miss Minchin called from the hall, “Lottie, return to your lesson!”

Lottie stepped back. Sara gave her a smile.

“You can help me by remembering the story of the princess.”

“She is not in the attic now.”

“She is still there.”

Lottie touched her chest. “Inside?”

“Inside.”

Lottie nodded and went to her lesson.

Sara finished the hall. It did not look like a palace. It looked like a clean hall. That was enough for the morning.

### Emily’s New Place

Sara carried Emily with her while she worked. Miss Minchin complained that the doll was in the way.

“Put her on the shelf.”

Sara placed Emily on a high shelf. The doll looked down at everyone.

“She is watching,” Sara said.

“She is a toy.”

“She may be both.”

During the afternoon, Sara returned to the attic. Emily was not on the shelf. A younger girl had moved her to a chair and put a paper hat on her head.

“Emily has been promoted,” Becky said.

Sara took off the hat. “She may prefer her own clothes.”

“She did not complain.”

“She is polite.”

Sara made a small pocket inside her coat for Emily’s ribbon. She could not carry the whole doll everywhere, but she could carry a part of her.

That evening, Sara placed Emily on the bed. The doll’s blue dress was now old and faded.

“We have lost many things,” Sara told her. “But we have not lost our rules.”

Becky asked, “What are the rules today?”

Sara counted them. “Do not hurt people because you are hurt. Share when you can. Rest when your body asks. Remember that a floor is not a person.”

Becky laughed at the last rule.

“Why is it there?”

“Because Miss Minchin speaks to the floor more kindly than she speaks to us.”

They sat beside the little window. London was dark. Sara could hear carts in the street.

“Will your father come back?” Becky asked.

Sara held the compass. “I do not know.”

“What will you do if he does not?”

Sara looked at Emily, then at the wall, then at Becky.

“I will live through the next day. After that, I will live through another.”

Becky took her hand. “I will be there.”

Sara nodded. The promise was small, but it stood firmly in the cold room.

At dawn, Becky woke before the bell. She found Sara's old blanket folded at the foot of the bed and shook it out. One corner had come loose.

“I can mend this,” Becky said.

“You can?”

“I can mend a blanket. I can also mend a sleeve, a pocket, and a very bad-looking sock.”

Sara watched her thread the needle. The stitches were uneven, but they held. Becky bit the thread, tied a knot, and held up the blanket like a flag.

“The flag of the attic,” she announced.

“What does it stand for?” Sara asked.

Becky thought. “Warmth. Work. And not giving up before breakfast.”

Sara laughed so loudly that Emily stirred in her sleep. The morning was still cold, but the attic had gained a flag and a rule. That felt like a beginning.

When the bell finally rang, Sara and Becky hurried downstairs. They were still poor in many ways, but they carried the attic flag in their minds. It was not made of silk. It was made of a mended blanket, a promise, and two people who had decided to keep going.

## Chapter 8 - In the Attic

Sara’s work began before the sun rose. She carried messages, cleaned rooms, helped the younger girls, and walked through the streets on errands.

Her shoes became thin. Her dress became short at the sleeves. Some days, she was so hungry that she could think only about bread.

The attic room was near the roof. Wind moved under the door, and rain tapped on the small window. Becky slept in the room next to hers, so the two girls could whisper at night.

“I am cold,” Becky said one evening.

“Let us pretend we are explorers,” Sara answered.

“Explorers are cold too.”

“Yes, but they have a map.”

Sara drew a map of the attic with a bit of pencil. The bed became a mountain. The washstand became a river. A loose board became a secret cave.

“And what are we searching for?” Becky asked.

“A warm kingdom.”

“Does it have supper?”

“Naturally. It has soup, bread, potatoes, and a very large pudding.”

Becky sighed happily.

Sara also pretended that she was a prisoner in an old stone prison. She imagined that an invisible friend had put her there to test her courage.

“A princess may be in rags,” Sara told Emily, “but she is still a princess inside.”

The words did not make the attic warm. They did not make Miss Minchin kind. They did not bring Captain Crewe back.

But imagination gave Sara a small space that Miss Minchin could not control. In that space, Sara could choose her thoughts. She could turn a dirty wall into a castle wall and a hard day into one part of a longer story.

She did not pretend that suffering was pleasant. She simply refused to let suffering tell her what kind of person she must become.

### The Attic Schedule

Sara learned the sounds of the school before she learned its new schedule. The first bell meant wake up. The second bell meant wash. The third bell meant carry the coal. A short bell meant someone had forgotten a book.

“There are too many bells,” Becky said.

“Perhaps the school is afraid that one bell will feel lonely.”

Sara made a schedule on a scrap of paper. She drew a bell beside each job. Under the last bell, she drew a moon.

“What is the moon for?” Becky asked.

“Sleeping.”

“There is no bell for sleeping.”

“That is because sleep does not need to shout.”

The schedule helped Sara move through the day. She cleaned the schoolroom before the younger girls arrived. She brushed dust from the shelves. She carried messages to the front door. She took parcels to the kitchen and returned with empty baskets.

Miss Minchin added tasks whenever Sara finished one.

“Take these books upstairs.”

“Yes, Miss Minchin.”

“Then polish the brass.”

“Yes, Miss Minchin.”

“Then find Lottie’s blue ribbon.”

“Where did she lose it?”

“If you knew, you would not need to find it.”

Sara looked under chairs, behind curtains, and inside a flower pot. The ribbon was tied around a table leg. Lottie had decided the table was a horse.

“Your horse has a blue tail,” Sara said.

“He is a very fine horse.”

“He is also holding up the table.”

Lottie untied the ribbon. The table remained standing, although it looked less proud.

### The Errand in the Rain

One day, Miss Minchin sent Sara to the station with a note. Rain fell hard, and the street shone like black glass. Sara held her coat closed with one hand and the note with the other.

At the station, she saw people hurry beneath umbrellas. A man carried a cage of birds. The birds were silent, perhaps because the rain had made them forget their song.

Sara delivered the note. On the way home, she stopped beneath a shop roof. Her shoes were wet through.

“Are you waiting for someone?” asked a woman near the door.

“I am waiting for the rain to decide what it wants.”

“It wants to fall.”

“Then it is very certain.”

The woman looked at Sara’s thin coat. “You should go home.”

“I am trying. The rain is walking with me.”

The woman gave her a piece of brown paper. Sara wrapped it around one shoe. The paper came off after three steps, but those three steps were dry.

“It was a short friendship,” Sara said.

“Most paper friendships are.”

Sara thanked the woman and continued. At the school door, Miss Minchin asked why she was late.

“The rain stopped me.”

“Rain does not stop people.”

Sara looked at the water dripping from her sleeves. “It stopped this person.”

Miss Minchin sent her to change. There was no warm coat, but Becky had dried a towel near the kitchen fire.

“You are dripping on the floor,” Becky said.

“I am adding a little river.”

“The school has enough water.”

“Then I must make the river interesting.”

Sara used a stick to draw a winding line in the drops. Lottie saw it and placed a paper boat on the floor.

“Do not make a mess,” Miss Minchin called.

Sara lifted the boat. “The river has been closed.”

Lottie looked disappointed. Sara promised that the paper boat could sail in a bowl later.

### The Princess Prison

When the attic felt cold, Sara imagined that she was in an old prison. She gave the prison a name: The Tower of Uncomfortable Blankets.

“It has one guard,” she told Becky.

“Who?”

“Emily.”

“She is a doll.”

“That is why she is good at guarding. She never falls asleep.”

“She also never helps.”

“She watches very seriously.”

Sara made rules for the prison. A prisoner could read one page before breakfast. A prisoner could speak to a rat. A prisoner could remember a happy place. A prisoner could plan what to do when the prison door opened.

“What will you do first?” Becky asked.

“I will ask for tea.”

“That is all?”

“Then I will ask for two teas.”

They laughed. Sara knew the game could not change the room. The game helped her choose what she would think about next.

Sometimes she imagined a palace instead. The bed became a throne. The cracked basin became a silver bowl. The loose board became a secret door.

“Where does the door lead?” Becky asked.

“To a library.”

“Is there food in the library?”

“There is a librarian who keeps biscuits in her desk.”

“Then the library is safe.”

“It is the safest place in the kingdom.”

Sara described shelves that reached the ceiling. She described books about ships, gardens, stars, cooking, and brave girls. She could not see those books, but she had read enough books to know how a library might feel.

### The Morning Without a Coat

One cold morning, Sara had to take a message outside. Her coat was too thin, and the missing button left a gap at her neck.

Ermengarde saw her in the hall.

“You cannot go out like that.”

“I have gone out like this before.”

“That does not make it wise.”

Ermengarde ran to her room and returned with a scarf. It was not new. One end had a small tear.

“Take it.”

Sara shook her head. “You need it.”

“I have a coat.”

“You may be cold.”

“Then I will walk quickly. The cold cannot catch me if I am faster.”

Sara tied the scarf around her neck. It smelled faintly of lavender.

“I will return it.”

“Not until your neck is warm.”

“That may take all day.”

“Then keep it all day.”

Sara delivered the message and returned with the scarf tucked under her chin. Miss Minchin saw it.

“Where did you get that?”

“Ermengarde gave it to me.”

“You must not take things from other pupils.”

“She offered it.”

“You should have refused.”

Sara looked at the scarf. “Why should I refuse warmth when it is freely given?”

Miss Minchin had no answer she wanted to say. She told Sara to return to work.

Ermengarde waited near the stair.

“Did she take it?”

“No.”

“Then the scarf won.”

“It has a strong character.”

Ermengarde smiled. “Keep it until dinner.”

Sara kept it until dinner, then folded it carefully and returned it. The tear was still there, but the scarf had done its work.

### A Lesson in the Attic

Sara did not want the attic to become a place where learning stopped. She began a small evening lesson for Becky. There was no desk, so they used an upside-down box.

The lesson had three parts: reading, numbers, and a story.

Becky read the labels they had practiced. She counted spoons, buttons, and pieces of thread. Then Sara told a story in which a servant girl became the keeper of a secret library.

“Why does she keep the library?” Becky asked.

“Because she knows where every book belongs.”

“What if a book is in the wrong place?”

“She carries it back.”

“That sounds like my work.”

“Then you are already prepared.”

Becky counted the buttons in her wooden box. She had five. She added a sixth button from an old coat.

“How many now?” Sara asked.

“Six.”

“If I take two?”

“I shall ask why.”

“Very good. If I return one?”

“I shall ask why you changed your mind.”

“You are learning numbers and questions.”

“Questions are more useful.”

The lesson ended when the candle grew small. Sara placed the box near the wall so it would not fall.

### A Letter Sara Could Not Send

Sara wrote a second letter to her father. She told him about the rain, the scarf, the attic prison, and Becky’s buttons. She wrote, I am doing my best. I am tired, but I am still here.

She folded the letter. Then she remembered that she did not know where to send it. The school might refuse to post it. She placed it under the compass instead.

“The compass knows where north is,” she told Emily. “Perhaps it knows where Papa is too.”

She knew this was not true. Still, the thought gave her a little comfort.

Becky came to the door.

“Are you writing?”

“A letter.”

“To your father?”

“Yes.”

“Will he read it?”

“I do not know.”

Becky leaned against the door. “You can read it to me.”

Sara read the letter. She did not hide the line about being tired.

“You should tell him the funny part,” Becky said.

“Which part?”

“The scarf.”

Sara read that the scarf had a strong character and had won a battle against Miss Minchin.

Becky laughed.

“Now he will know you are safe,” Becky said.

Sara folded the letter again. “Perhaps.”

“And if he does not read it?”

“Then I have said the words.”

Becky nodded. Some words needed a listener. Others needed only to leave the heart.

### A Small Victory at the Schoolroom

The younger girls sometimes left their books in untidy piles. Sara was told to put them away. She made labels for the shelves so the girls could help.

“Stories,” she wrote on one shelf. “History,” on another. “Maps,” on a third.

Lottie carried a book to the map shelf. It was about cooking.

“This is not a map,” Sara said.

“It has a picture of a pie.”

“A pie is not a country.”

“It could be.”

Sara considered the picture. “Then the pie country has a round border.”

Lottie put the book on the story shelf.

Ermengarde helped sort the rest. The girls made mistakes, but they corrected them. The schoolroom became easier to use.

Miss Minchin saw the labels. “Who gave you permission?”

“No one.”

“Then remove them.”

The younger girls looked at Sara. She could remove the labels, but she thought of the books being lost again.

“The shelves are easier now,” she said.

Miss Minchin looked at the room. It was true. The books were in order. The girls knew where to find them.

“Keep them,” she said at last. “But do not waste paper.”

Sara nodded. “We will use the backs of old lists.”

It was a tiny victory. No one clapped. No music played. The schoolroom simply worked better because Sara had noticed a problem and made a useful choice.

That night, she added one line to her schedule: Make one small thing better.

The line did not promise that the whole world would change. It only promised that Sara would look for one corner where her hands could help.

### A Day of Small Tasks

On Wednesday, Sara was sent to wake a girl who had slept through the bell. The girl pulled the blanket over her head.

“The bell is calling you,” Sara said.

“Tell it I am not home.”

“It can see your blanket.”

“The blanket is home.”

Sara sat beside the bed. “If you rise now, you will have time to tie your shoes.”

“I can tie them in bed.”

“That is a difficult skill.”

The girl sat up. One shoe was under the bed. The other was on the pillow.

“Your shoes have chosen different rooms,” Sara said.

The girl found them and dressed. Sara did not drag her from bed. She gave her a reason to move.

Later, Sara cleaned a window. The glass was so dirty that she could not see the garden.

“There is a world hiding here,” she told Becky.

“The world should wash itself.”

“It may be busy.”

They cleaned the lower part of the window. A small patch of garden appeared. A bird stood on the wall.

“We found a country,” Sara said.

“It has one bird.”

“Countries begin small.”

Miss Minchin came to inspect the work. “You have missed a corner.”

Sara looked at the corner. It was true.

“The corner was hiding,” she said.

“Clean it.”

Sara cleaned it. The whole window shone a little more.

### A Map on the Wall

In the attic, Sara drew a map of the school. She marked the kitchen, schoolroom, stairs, roof door, and her bed.

“Why is the kitchen larger than the schoolroom?” Becky asked.

“Because the kitchen has food.”

“That is not how maps work.”

“It is how hungry maps work.”

Sara marked safe places with a star. The corner beside the fire was safe when no one was angry. The library was safe when a teacher was not looking for a lost book. The garden was safe when the door was open.

“The attic is not safe,” Becky said.

Sara placed a star near the bed. “It is safe when we are together.”

“That is not a real map rule.”

“It is the best kind.”

They added secret paths. A broom became a bridge. A laundry basket became a boat. The stairs became a mountain that could be climbed only by brave explorers.

Lottie came in and added a purple circle.

“What is that?” Sara asked.

“My mother’s garden.”

Sara did not ask whether it was real. She drew a path to the circle.

“Now it has a way in.”

Ermengarde added a box for lessons. Becky added the kitchen clock. The map became a picture of the lives they carried through the school.

### A Little Rest

Sara learned to rest when she could. She sat on the bottom stair for one minute. She leaned against a wall while waiting for a message. She closed her eyes beside the coal bucket.

“You are sleeping,” Miss Minchin said once.

“I am practicing being still.”

“Servants do not practice being still.”

“Then I am practicing before I become a very good servant.”

Miss Minchin sent her away.

Sara did not win the argument, but she had rested for one minute. Sometimes a small victory was enough to carry a large day.

At night, she and Becky read three pages. They stopped before the candle became too small.

“What happens next?” Becky asked.

“Tomorrow will tell us.”

“I do not like waiting.”

“Neither do I.”

“Then why do you say it so calmly?”

“Because tomorrow cannot hear me worry.”

Becky laughed. Sara closed the book. The attic schedule had not made her life easy, but it had helped her keep pieces of herself: time to think, time to laugh, time to read, and time to notice someone who needed help.

That evening, Sara tested the schedule with a game. She wrote four words on scraps of paper: think, laugh, read, notice. Becky mixed them in a hat.

“Draw one,” Sara said.

Becky pulled out notice. At once, they looked around the attic. They noticed a loose nail in the floor, a cup without water, and Emily's shoe lying under the bed.

“That word is hard work,” Becky said.

“Draw again.”

The next word was laugh. Becky placed the shoe on her hand and made it bow. Emily woke, saw the shoe, and giggled.

Then came read. Sara read a page about a queen who lost her crown and found a kind friend. Finally came think. They sat quietly and wondered what kind thing they could do tomorrow.

The game did not repair the roof or fill the empty cupboard. It did something smaller. It reminded them that a day was made of many pieces, and even a difficult day might contain one piece they could choose.

Becky put the four scraps back in the hat. “We should play again tomorrow.”

“Of course.”

“Can we add sleep?”

Sara considered this very seriously. “Yes. But only after we finish the reading.”

Becky groaned like an actor in a tragedy. Emily threw a pillow at her. The pillow missed Becky and landed in the wash basket. They all agreed that it was a successful ending.

Sara wrote the four words on the wall beside their bed. The paper curled at the corners, but the letters stayed clear.

“If we forget,” she said, “the wall will remind us.”

Becky nodded. “And if the wall forgets?”

“We will remind the wall.”

Emily reached up and tapped the word laugh. “This one is my favorite.”

“That is because you are very good at it,” Sara said.

The attic was still a hard place to live. Yet the girls had begun to make small tools for living there. A schedule, a game, and a crooked paper list could not change every problem. They could help the girls meet each problem together.

## Chapter 9 - Melchisedec

The attic had other residents. At night, Sara heard tiny feet moving behind the wall.

One evening, a large rat looked out from a hole near the floor. He had bright eyes and long whiskers. He seemed to be waiting for dinner.

Sara stared at him.

“You are a very brave gentleman,” she said. “Or perhaps you are only very hungry.”

The rat twitched his nose.

Sara named him Melchisedec. The name was much too grand for a rat, which was exactly why she liked it.

She began to leave small pieces of food for him. There was not much food to spare. Sometimes she saved a crumb from breakfast or a little bit of cheese from the kitchen.

Becky watched from the doorway. “You are feeding a rat?”

“He is my neighbor.”

“He has a long tail.”

“Many important gentlemen do.”

Melchisedec came closer each night. He still ran away if Sara moved too quickly, but he no longer hid as soon as she entered the room.

Sara made up a history for him. Melchisedec had once been the mayor of a great underground city. He had lost his way while traveling and now lived in the attic until he could return home.

“He must have a family,” Sara said. “Perhaps all those little feet belong to his cousins.”

The rats did not solve Sara’s problems. They could not bring her a blanket or stop Miss Minchin’s angry voice.

Still, they made the attic feel less empty. Sara had someone to greet in the morning. She had a small life to protect. When she had food, she shared it.

One rainy night, Melchisedec sat near her shoe while she read. Sara looked down at him.

“You are not beautiful,” she said. “But neither am I after a day of cleaning.”

The rat washed his face with his paws.

“That is a very rude answer.”

Sara smiled. Even in a poor attic, a little friendship and a little humor could make the darkness easier to bear.

### The Rat Council

After Sara named Melchisedec, she noticed that he was not alone. Three small rats appeared near the wall. One had a white mark on its nose. One had a bent ear. The third moved so quickly that Sara could see only a gray blur.

“You have brought your family,” Sara said.

Melchisedec sat beside the hole.

“Are they your cousins?”

The white-nosed rat sniffed the floor.

“Or your ministers?”

Becky stood in the doorway. “You are speaking to rats again.”

“This is a council meeting.”

“What are they deciding?”

“Whether the attic should have more cheese.”

“That is a good decision.”

Sara broke a small piece of cheese into four pieces. She gave one to each rat and kept the last piece for herself.

“The council is fair,” Becky said.

“A council cannot eat its own floor.”

“These rats might.”

The rats carried the pieces away. The fast gray blur pulled its piece beneath the bed. The white-nosed rat sat on top of a box and ate slowly. Melchisedec remained near Sara.

“He is the chairman,” Sara explained.

“What is the bent-ear rat?”

“The secretary.”

“How can he write?”

“He remembers everything.”

Becky shook her head. “You give them too many jobs.”

“The attic has many jobs.”

### A Rat Map

Sara wanted to know where the rats went when they disappeared. She sprinkled a tiny line of flour near the wall. In the morning, the flour had become a map of tracks.

“Look,” she said to Becky. “Melchisedec traveled to the corner.”

“You put flour on the floor.”

“The flour helped us see.”

“It also made the floor dirty.”

“The floor was already having a difficult day.”

They followed the tracks. The rats had traveled under a loose board, behind a box, and toward a warm pipe. Sara found a small space beneath the roof where dry grass and bits of cloth made a nest.

“They built a house,” Becky said.

“A house inside a house.”

“It is not very clean.”

“Neither is the school kitchen after supper.”

Becky crouched beside the opening. A tiny rat looked out.

“It has a button,” she whispered.

One of the rats had carried a blue button into the nest.

“That is from my old coat,” Becky said.

Sara did not take it back. “It is a gift.”

“You gave a button to a rat?”

“No. The rat found it. He may believe the button is a moon.”

“It is too small to be the moon.”

“The attic has a small sky.”

They placed a little piece of cloth near the nest. The rats pulled it inside. Sara watched them work. They moved with purpose. Each one carried a piece. None of them asked whether the others deserved it.

### A Narrow Escape

One morning, the white-nosed rat became trapped inside an empty tin. Sara heard scratching.

“Melchisedec has a problem,” she said.

Becky listened. “That is not Melchisedec. It is the small one.”

“The council has a problem.”

The tin had been left near the wall. The rat had climbed inside to smell a little food, then slipped into the narrow bottom. He could not climb out.

Sara tried to move the tin, but it was wedged under a board. Becky brought a spoon. They pushed the board up a little. Sara placed a book beneath it.

“The book is holding the roof,” Becky said.

“It is a very useful book.”

“What is it about?”

Sara looked at the cover. “Arithmetic.”

“Then it has finally found a job.”

They lifted the board. Becky tipped the tin. The rat ran out and disappeared behind the box.

Sara held the tin. “No more tin journeys without a map.”

The rat poked his head out.

“He agrees,” Becky said.

“Or he wants another piece of cheese.”

“Both can be true.”

Sara put the tin outside the nest. She washed it and used it as a cup for the rat family’s water.

“They have a water room now,” she said.

“They will move in next week.”

“They are welcome if they pay rent.”

“What will they pay with?”

“Stories.”

Becky looked at the rats. “They are not good at stories.”

“You have not heard the one about the brave tin.”

### Melchisedec’s Birthday

Sara decided that Melchisedec needed a birthday. She did not know the date of his birth, so she chose the first day he had come near her.

“We shall celebrate the day you became a gentleman,” she announced.

Becky found a crumb of cake. Sara placed it on a saucer. She drew a paper crown and set it near the saucer.

Melchisedec came out. He sniffed the cake.

“You must wear the crown before you eat,” Sara said.

The rat walked around it.

“He does not like hats,” Becky said.

“Perhaps he is a hat critic.”

Sara put the crown on a small stone. “Then the stone can be king.”

Becky sat beside her. “What shall we sing?”

Sara made up a song. It had four lines, but the last line did not rhyme.

Becky added two lines. The song became longer and less musical.

“It is not a good song,” Becky said.

“It is a brave song.”

They sang softly. Melchisedec ate the cake during the second line. The white-nosed rat carried away the paper crown. The bent-ear rat sat on the saucer.

“The party is a success,” Sara said.

“The guests have eaten everything.”

“That is usually the sign.”

Sara wrote the date on a scrap of paper and placed it beside the rat nest. She knew she was making a game out of a small thing. That was the point. A poor room still had room for a birthday.

### The Rat Palace

Sara made a longer story about the rats. In the story, Melchisedec was the mayor of an underground city. His city had streets made of roots and bridges made of old boards. The white-nosed rat was the map keeper. The bent-ear rat was the keeper of lost things. The fast gray rat was the runner.

“What does he run?” Becky asked.

“News.”

“What news?”

“A boot is coming. A cat is sleeping. There is cheese near the kitchen.”

“Those are important news.”

“Very important.”

In the story, the city had one problem. A great flood had blocked the road to the bread room. The rats needed a bridge. Melchisedec called a meeting.

Sara used a ruler as the bridge. The rats, of course, did not cross it. She moved a button across the ruler and called the button a messenger.

“The messenger says the bridge is too high,” Becky said.

“Then the builders must dig.”

“The builders are rats.”

“Rats can dig.”

“Can they move a ruler?”

“They can move smaller things.”

Sara used a strip of cloth as a road. She placed crumbs along it. Melchisedec followed the crumbs until they ended near her shoe.

“He has crossed the bridge,” Sara said.

“He has crossed the floor.”

“A story must begin somewhere.”

Becky smiled. She liked correcting Sara, and Sara liked that Becky had begun to add her own ideas.

### A Little Food to Share

Food was not always easy to find. Some mornings, Sara had only a piece of bread. She ate half and kept half for later.

One day, she broke off a piece for Melchisedec. Becky watched.

“You need it.”

“He needs it too.”

“If you keep sharing, you will have nothing.”

Sara looked at the bread. “I do not want him to have nothing while I have something.”

“But you may become hungry.”

“I already am.”

Becky sat down. She had a small crust in her pocket. She broke it in two and gave one piece to Sara.

“Then we share with him and with each other.”

Sara accepted the piece. Together they placed crumbs near the wall. The rats came slowly.

“There is less,” Becky said.

“There is also company.”

Becky thought about this. “Company is not food.”

“No. But loneliness can make hunger feel larger.”

The girls ate their pieces. The rats ate theirs. Nobody had a full meal, but nobody ate alone.

### A Letter to the Council

Sara wrote a letter to the rat council. She could not read it to them because they moved whenever she spoke. She left the paper near the nest.

Dear Council,

Thank you for visiting the attic. I am sorry that the floor is cold. I hope you like the cloth. Please do not eat my book. It is the book about the library, and the library is important.

Your friend,

Sara

In the morning, the letter had a tiny tooth mark in one corner.

“They have answered,” Sara said.

“What did they say?” Becky asked.

“They say the library needs more cheese.”

“That sounds like a request, not an answer.”

“A good council knows how to ask.”

Sara kept the letter. She liked the mark. It was proof that something else had noticed her words.

### A Quiet Watch

One night, Sara could not sleep. The wind shook the roof, and the school below made strange sounds. She sat by the window with Emily.

Melchisedec came out. He sat near her foot.

“Are you awake too?” Sara asked.

The rat washed his face.

“Perhaps the council is watching the night.”

She imagined that the attic had two guards. Emily guarded the bed. Melchisedec guarded the floor. Sara guarded the window.

They watched together. A cloud moved away from the moon. The roofs shone silver. A chimney sent smoke into the sky.

“The world is very large,” Sara whispered.

Melchisedec’s whiskers moved.

“And we are very small.”

The rat continued washing his face.

“You are right. Small does not mean unimportant.”

Sara thought about Becky, Ermengarde, Lottie, and the girls who were often overlooked. She thought about the bird in the garden and the rat beneath the floor.

She took a crumb from her pocket and placed it near Melchisedec. He carried it away.

“Good night, Mayor,” she said.

The rat disappeared into the wall. Sara returned to bed. The attic was still cold, but it held a family, a story, and a tiny council that knew her footsteps.

### The Night Signal

Sara began to use a soft tap on the floor as a signal. One tap meant that she had come home. Two taps meant that she had found food. Three taps meant that Becky should bring a story.

Melchisedec learned the signals faster than Becky.

“He is a very good pupil,” Sara said.

“He is listening for food.”

“That is also a good reason to learn.”

Sara tapped once. Melchisedec appeared. She tapped twice. The white-nosed rat appeared behind him.

“The council has arrived.”

Becky tapped three times. The bent-ear rat came out.

“He wants a story,” Sara said.

“Or he wants your shoe.”

“He has good taste.”

Sara told the rats about a ship that sailed through a sea of soup. The captain was a girl with a compass. The crew included a spoon, a button, and four rats who knew every secret tunnel.

“Does the ship sink?” Becky asked.

“No. The soup is thick.”

“Does the spoon help?”

“It acts as an oar.”

“Spoons are not good oars.”

“This spoon has training.”

The story reached a storm. Sara tapped the floor while she described the waves. Melchisedec ran in a circle, perhaps because he liked the drama or perhaps because he had discovered another crumb.

When the story ended, Becky asked, “What does the captain do when she reaches land?”

“She builds a warm house.”

“With a kitchen?”

“With two kitchens.”

“Why two?”

“One for soup and one for cake.”

Becky approved.

### The Lost Button

One morning, Becky found that a button was missing from her coat. She searched the bed and the floor.

“The council has taken it,” she said.

Sara asked the rats. Melchisedec looked innocent. The white-nosed rat hid behind a board. The bent-ear rat carried something shiny toward the nest.

“We have found the keeper of lost things,” Sara said.

Becky reached for the button, but Sara stopped her.

“Ask first.”

She placed a crumb near the nest. The bent-ear rat dropped the button and took the crumb.

“A fair trade,” Becky said.

Sara sewed the button back on. The stitch was neat.

“He may have thought it was a moon,” Sara said.

“It is not round enough.”

“The attic moon is small.”

Becky looked at the rat nest. “We should give them something that belongs to them.”

She found a piece of old cloth. Sara cut it into strips. The rats carried the strips into the nest.

“Now they have blankets,” Becky said.

“And your button is safe.”

“The council has learned to trade.”

“It has learned from people.”

### A Warning from the Wall

The rats became alert one afternoon. Melchisedec stopped near the hole and lifted his head. The white-nosed rat ran back into the nest.

“What happened?” Becky asked.

Sara listened. A cat moved somewhere below. Its claws made a soft scratch against wood.

“The council has sent a warning.”

“About the cat?”

Melchisedec disappeared.

Becky lifted the food from the floor and placed it on the table. Sara closed the small opening near the wall with a piece of cloth, but she did not block it completely.

“They need a way out,” she said.

“Why help rats that might eat our food?”

“Because danger is danger.”

They waited. A gray cat appeared near the stairs. It looked at the attic door and sniffed the air.

Sara stood between the cat and the wall.

“This room is occupied,” she said.

The cat looked at her.

“There is no dinner here.”

The cat sat down.

“It does not believe you,” Becky whispered.

Sara picked up a broom. “You may leave politely.”

The cat moved closer. Sara tapped the floor. Melchisedec ran through a narrow gap behind the box. The other rats followed.

Sara lifted the broom and guided the cat toward the door. She did not strike it. She only made the path away from the wall clear.

The cat slipped out.

Becky closed the door. “You sent away a cat with a broom.”

“The broom has experience.”

Melchisedec returned. He carried a small piece of thread.

“He is thanking us,” Sara said.

“That is not food.”

“It is a royal gift.”

The thread joined the rat family’s nest.

### The Council Meeting

Sara decided that the rat council needed a meeting place. She put a flat box near the wall and placed a small stone in the middle.

“The stone is the table,” she explained.

Becky sat beside her. “What will they discuss?”

“The cat, the food, and the future.”

The rats came out one by one. Melchisedec sat beside the stone. The white-nosed rat moved around it. The bent-ear rat sniffed the corner. The fast gray rat ran to the door and back.

“The secretary has a report,” Sara said.

“What is it?”

“The door is still a door.”

“Very useful.”

“The cat has gone downstairs.”

“Excellent.”

“The cheese is finished.”

“A serious problem.”

Sara broke a crust into pieces. The council listened while she made a plan. Food would be shared. The rat family would keep away from the girls’ clothes. The cat would not be allowed to enter the nest.

“Can the rats agree?” Becky asked.

Melchisedec lifted one paw.

“He agrees,” Sara said.

“He may be scratching.”

“A scratch can be a vote.”

The meeting ended when the rats carried away the crust.

### A Story for the Rats

Sara told the council about India. She described the hot sun, the gardens, and the monkeys that climbed across roofs. She described her father reading under a tree.

“Did he know about rats?” Becky asked.

“He knew they were clever.”

“Did he feed them?”

“He preferred to feed me.”

“A wise man.”

Sara described a night in India when rain fell so hard that every roof sounded like a drum. She and her father had sat indoors. He had made up names for the clouds.

“What were they called?” Becky asked.

“The Grandmother Cloud, the Sleepy Cloud, and the Cloud Who Had Forgotten Her Umbrella.”

“Did the cloud find it?”

“No. It borrowed the moon.”

Becky laughed. Sara looked at Melchisedec.

“Papa would have liked you,” she said.

The rat sat near her foot. For a moment, the attic held a memory of India and the warmth of a father’s voice.

### The Rat Garden

The attic had no real garden, so Sara made one on paper. She drew roots, tunnels, flowers, and a small underground palace. The rats lived beneath the palace. Their doors were round. Their lamps were bits of shining glass.

Becky colored the flowers blue.

“Flowers are not blue,” Sara said.

“Some are.”

“Then these are.”

Lottie saw the picture and added a cake tree. Its branches held buns.

“The rats will eat it,” Becky said.

“That is why it grows more.”

Ermengarde added a road with signs. She wrote the signs carefully.

Sara placed the drawing beside the nest. The rats ignored it, but that did not matter. The picture made the room bright.

### A Small Promise

Sara knew the rats could not be her only friends. She also knew that a small life deserved care.

“I will not feed you when I have no food,” she told Melchisedec. “But when I have enough to share, you will have some.”

Melchisedec washed his face.

“That is a promise, not a command.”

Becky said, “He may not understand.”

“Perhaps he understands the food.”

“Most people understand food.”

“And some understand promises.”

Sara placed the last crumb near the wall. Melchisedec took it. The rat family disappeared together.

The attic was quiet after they left. Sara did not feel as if the room were empty. She knew the wall held a small world. She knew Becky would return. She knew that tomorrow might bring a new task, a new cold, or a new surprise.

She also knew that even a rat could make a lonely place feel less lonely.

## Chapter 10 - The Indian Gentleman

A sick man moved into the large house next door to the school. He had returned from India and needed a quiet place to rest.

Sara saw servants carry boxes into the house. She saw an Indian servant in a bright coat and heard a monkey making loud, unhappy sounds.

“The Indian Gentleman has arrived,” Sara told Becky.

She did not know the man’s real name was Mr. Carrisford.

Mr. Carrisford had once been Captain Crewe’s business partner. He had been very ill when the diamond mine news first arrived. He believed that the mines had failed and that Captain Crewe had died in sadness because of the lost money.

But the mines had not failed. They became successful after Captain Crewe’s death. Their diamonds made a great fortune.

Mr. Carrisford felt terrible when he learned the truth. He wanted to find Captain Crewe’s daughter and give her everything that belonged to her.

“Her name is Sara Crewe,” he told his helper. “She was sent to a school in Europe. Find the school. Find the child.”

His helper searched letters, ships, hotels, and schools. The search took a long time because nobody knew exactly where Sara had gone.

Mr. Carrisford often sat by the window and thought about his old friend. He imagined Sara as a little girl waiting for news that never came.

At the same time, Sara lived only a short distance away. She crossed the school yard every day, but she never saw the man who was looking for her.

The great house and the cold attic stood side by side. A wall separated them. To Sara, the house was a mysterious place with warm rooms and an Indian monkey.

To Mr. Carrisford, the school was one more place that might hide Captain Crewe’s child.

Neither of them knew how close the answer was.

### The Quiet House

Mr. Carrisford’s house was large, but it did not feel cheerful. Rooms stood ready with chairs, tables, and lamps, yet few people used them. Mr. Carrisford spent most of his time in one room near the fire.

His illness had left him weak. He tired after walking across the hall. Sometimes he tried to read and discovered that he had been looking at the same line for ten minutes.

Ram Dass brought him tea.

“You must rest,” Ram Dass said.

“I have rested for months.”

“Then rest in a different chair.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at him. “Is that Indian medicine?”

“It is house medicine. The chair by the window is less gloomy.”

Ram Dass moved the chair. Mr. Carrisford sat in it and saw the school next door.

“That building makes too much noise,” he said.

“Schools make noise because children have many feet.”

“I used to like noise.”

“You may like it again.”

Mr. Carrisford watched girls cross the yard. He did not know Sara yet, but he knew the sight of a school made him think of his friend Captain Crewe.

Years earlier, he and Captain Crewe had shared a tent in India. They had eaten hard biscuits, argued about maps, and laughed when a monkey stole a button from Captain Crewe’s coat.

“The monkey had good taste,” Mr. Carrisford said.

Ram Dass looked toward the monkey, who was trying to open a drawer.

“He still has good taste.”

“He has terrible manners.”

“Manners and taste are different skills.”

The monkey pulled out a handkerchief and wore it on his head.

Mr. Carrisford smiled for the first time that morning.

### The Old Partnership

Mr. Carrisford kept Captain Crewe’s letters in a wooden box. He opened the box one rainy afternoon. The letters smelled faintly of dust and travel.

In one letter, Captain Crewe had written about Sara. He said she loved stories, asked difficult questions, and believed a doll could have strong opinions.

Mr. Carrisford read the line twice.

“She must have been a happy child,” he said.

Ram Dass stood near the door. “She may still be.”

“If she knows what happened?”

“Perhaps she knows only that her father loved her.”

Mr. Carrisford closed his eyes. He remembered the day he had sent news about the diamond mines. The first report had been bad. He had been ill and unable to travel. Captain Crewe had received the news alone.

“He believed the money was gone,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“The mines were not ready then.”

“I should have written again.”

“You were very ill.”

“That does not change the result.”

Mr. Carrisford took out another letter. It contained a later report. The mines had produced diamonds. The business had become rich.

“If I had known sooner, I could have found Captain Crewe.”

Ram Dass did not correct him. Some regrets needed to be spoken before they could become smaller.

“Find his daughter now,” Ram Dass said.

Mr. Carrisford nodded. “Yes. Whatever it takes.”

### The Search Book

Ram Dass made a search book. On the first page, he wrote Sara Crewe. Under the name, he wrote every fact they knew.

Her father: Captain Crewe.

Her age when sent away: seven.

Her home before England: India.

Her school: a private school somewhere in Europe.

Her doll: Emily.

“Why write the doll?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“A doll may help someone remember a child.”

“A doll may also be lost.”

“Then we will ask about both.”

Ram Dass wrote letters to schools. He asked whether they had a pupil named Sara Crewe. Some schools answered no. Some did not answer. One sent back a note about a Sara who had been sixteen and played the violin.

“Not our Sara,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“Perhaps she has grown quickly.”

“Not that quickly.”

Another letter described a Sara who lived in a village. She had two brothers and no doll.

“Not our Sara,” Ram Dass said this time.

They wrote to ship agents, hotels, and people who knew families from India. The search filled pages. Names crossed out crowded the margins.

Mr. Carrisford began to fear that the search book would become a graveyard of answers.

“We need one good clue,” he said.

Ram Dass closed the book. “A clue may be standing in a window.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at him. “What do you mean?”

“I have seen a girl in the school attic.”

“A girl?”

“She looks like a pupil, but she wears old clothes and carries heavy things.”

“Could she be the child?”

“I do not know.”

Mr. Carrisford felt hope and fear at the same time. “Ask about her.”

### Sara at the Window

Sara sometimes stood at the attic window when her work was finished. She could see part of the next house. A curtain moved in one upstairs room. A lamp shone behind it.

“Someone lives there,” she told Becky.

“Of course. It is a house.”

“A house may have no one interesting inside.”

“The monkey is interesting.”

“We do not know the house belongs to him.”

“He acts as if it does.”

Sara liked the monkey because he reminded her of India. She remembered how monkeys jumped between roofs and stole fruit from open windows.

One day, the monkey climbed onto a ledge and looked at Sara. He held a silver spoon.

Sara lifted her hand.

The monkey lifted the spoon.

Sara touched her nose.

The monkey touched the spoon to his nose.

“He is copying you,” Becky said.

“Or I am copying him.”

The monkey dropped the spoon and ran away.

“He has ended the conversation,” Sara said.

Ram Dass saw the exchange. He did not hear the words, but he saw Sara’s smile. The girl looked tired, yet she found joy in a monkey and a spoon.

### The Name in the Hall

Mr. Carrisford asked Ram Dass to learn the girl’s name. Ram Dass spoke to a driver who sometimes waited outside the school.

“The girl is called Sara,” the driver said. “Her father was Captain Crewe.”

Ram Dass returned quickly.

“Sir,” he said, “the girl’s name is Sara. They say her father was Captain Crewe.”

Mr. Carrisford gripped the arm of his chair. “What does she look like?”

“Small. Thin. Dark hair. Bright eyes.”

“Her age?”

“Perhaps eleven.”

The room became silent.

“It could be her,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“It could.”

“Why did no one tell me?”

“Perhaps no one knew where you were looking.”

Mr. Carrisford wanted to rush to the school. His legs were not strong, and fear held him back. What if this was not Sara? What if it was Sara, but he frightened her? What if she believed that he had failed her father?

“I should speak with her,” he said.

Ram Dass nodded. “First we must know whether she is safe.”

They watched the attic window. They saw Sara carry a basket. They saw her return with wet shoes. They saw her sit beside a younger child. They saw her give food to a servant girl.

Mr. Carrisford’s hope changed into anger. “They are treating her badly.”

“Yes.”

“And she may be Captain Crewe’s daughter.”

“Yes.”

Mr. Carrisford stood, but he became dizzy. Ram Dass helped him back into the chair.

“You must not fall,” Ram Dass said. “A rescue needs a rescuer who can stand.”

“Then help me stand.”

### A Plan with Warm Things

Ram Dass suggested beginning with food and blankets. Mr. Carrisford wanted to send money, but Ram Dass shook his head.

“If we send money, the school may take it.”

“Then send it secretly.”

“The child may be frightened by a stranger’s money.”

“She is already frightened by hunger.”

“That is why we must bring comfort first.”

They listed things a cold attic needed. A fire. A thick blanket. A chair. Clean clothes. Books. Food that could be eaten without a knife.

“Why no knife?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“A child who is surprised may drop it.”

Mr. Carrisford added a small lamp.

“The attic has a lamp,” Ram Dass said. “It is poor.”

“Then give it better oil.”

They decided that Ram Dass would enter from the roof when the school was asleep. He would leave no note at first. The child could accept the gifts without being forced to answer questions.

“What if she thinks it is magic?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“Perhaps she needs to think so.”

Mr. Carrisford looked toward the window. “Captain Crewe believed in her imagination.”

“Then we should not break it.”

The plan was small and careful. It did not solve the mystery of the name. It did not restore the years that had passed. It could make one cold night kinder.

Across the wall, Sara looked at the moon. She did not know that the quiet house had begun to move toward her.

### The Letter He Wrote

Mr. Carrisford wrote a letter to Captain Crewe even though he knew his friend would never read it.

Dear Crewe,

I know now that the mines became successful. I know that your daughter may be near. I am sorry that the truth came too late for you. I will find her. I will tell her that you were loved and that your fortune was not lost forever.

He stopped before signing his name. The words felt too small. He folded the letter and placed it in the wooden box.

Ram Dass brought fresh tea.

“You wrote another letter.”

“It was for an old friend.”

“Will you send it?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps it is for you.”

Mr. Carrisford considered this. “Perhaps.”

He asked Ram Dass to tell him more about Sara. Ram Dass described her habit of talking to Emily, her care for Becky, and the way she made jokes about coal, spoons, and proud coats.

“She does not sound like a child who has lost everything,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“She has not lost everything.”

“What does she have?”

“Her memory. Her imagination. Her friends.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the papers on his desk. He had wealth, a large house, servants, and a business that had become successful. Yet he would have traded all of it for one more day with Captain Crewe.

“Money is a strange thing,” he said.

“It can buy a house,” Ram Dass answered. “It cannot fill an empty chair.”

### The House Looks Next Door

Mr. Carrisford asked a carpenter to repair the windows facing the school. He said the work was needed because of the cold. In truth, he wanted to see the attic clearly.

The carpenter cleaned the glass. The school roof came into view. The attic window was small, but it was not hidden anymore.

“There is a child there,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“Yes.”

“She stands by the window every evening.”

“She likes the monkey.”

“Does the monkey like her?”

“He likes anyone who watches him.”

Sita climbed onto the sill and waved a red ribbon. Sara saw it and waved back.

Mr. Carrisford watched from behind the curtain. He did not want Sara to think she was being observed by a stranger.

“She has no idea,” he said.

“No.”

“The child may be afraid if we tell her suddenly.”

“Then we will make the truth gentle.”

### A Box of Questions

Mr. Carrisford made a second list. It was a list of questions he might ask Sara when they met.

What was your father’s full name?

Where did you live in India?

What did your father call his old friend?

Did he speak about a business partner?

What was the name of your doll?

What did the doll wear?

Ram Dass read the list.

“This sounds like an examination.”

“I need to know whether she is the child.”

“You can ask questions without making a test.”

Mr. Carrisford crossed out the first question. Then the second. He crossed out almost all of them.

“What should I ask?”

“Ask whether she would like tea.”

“That will not prove her name.”

“It may prove whether she feels safe.”

Mr. Carrisford wrote one new question: What would make this moment easier?

He looked at it for a long time.

“This is not a question for a business meeting.”

“It is a good question for a child.”

### The Memory of the Goat

Mr. Carrisford remembered the goat. Captain Crewe had once brought Sara to a camp where a goat walked into the tent and ate a corner of a map.

“Sara asked the goat to leave politely,” Mr. Carrisford told Ram Dass.

“Did it leave?”

“It ate the map first.”

“The goat was not polite.”

“No. Sara offered it a leaf.”

“Did the leaf work?”

“For a moment.”

Mr. Carrisford smiled at the memory. Sara had been small, but she had spoken to the goat as if it were a visitor who had made a mistake.

“Captain Crewe said she believed every creature had a reason,” he said.

“Even the goat?”

“Especially the goat.”

Ram Dass looked at Sita, who was trying to open a box with a spoon.

“Then the monkey has a reason.”

“What is it?”

“He wants the box.”

“That is not a deep reason.”

“Not every reason is deep.”

Mr. Carrisford laughed. The memory of Captain Crewe was painful, but it could also make the room warmer.

### A Promise to a Dead Friend

Mr. Carrisford placed Captain Crewe’s photograph beside the window.

“I will find her,” he said.

Ram Dass bowed. “You will.”

“I will give her the fortune.”

“Yes.”

“I will not ask her to forgive me at once.”

Ram Dass looked pleased. “That is wise.”

“I will not tell her that money can fix the past.”

“Also wise.”

“I will tell her that her father loved her.”

“She already knows.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the photograph. “Then I will tell her again.”

Across the wall, Sara closed her curtain. The attic became dark. Mr. Carrisford could not see her, but he knew she was there.

The next step would be careful help. A basket. A fire. A blanket. A book. The house next door had many things, but the first gift would be the one a cold child could accept without fear.

Mr. Carrisford wrote the plan on a clean card. He wrote slowly so that each item stood alone.

“First, warmth,” he said. “Second, food. Third, a little company. No grand entrance.”

Ram Dass nodded. “A grand entrance would frighten her.”

“It might frighten me too.”

They placed a basket on the table and tested its handle. It held. They wrapped a small loaf in cloth, then added oranges, a jar of honey, and a book with a bright red cover.

“What if she thinks it is a trick?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“Then we give her time to decide,” Ram Dass said.

Mr. Carrisford looked toward the wall. He wanted to hurry through it. But Sara had already waited through many things. The first kindness had to be patient enough to wait for her.

He added a pair of warm socks. They were too large, but he folded them tightly.

“No child should have cold feet while reading,” he said.

Ram Dass smiled. “That is a good rule.”

“Write it on the card.”

Ram Dass wrote it beneath the other rules.

They left the card beside the basket so no one would forget the order. When Mr. Carrisford looked at it again, he saw that the list was not grand. It was simply kind. That made it harder to ruin with pride.

He placed the card inside his coat. “We will follow it exactly.”

Ram Dass bowed. “And if we make a mistake?”

“We will notice, repair it, and try again.”

The two men looked at the basket. It was ready, but the right moment mattered more than speed.

Mr. Carrisford added a small bell to the card. “If the child is asleep, we must leave quietly.”

Ram Dass nodded. “If she is awake, we must still leave quietly.”

“That is an excellent rule.”

The monkey rang the bell once.

“Not you,” Ram Dass said.

The monkey rang it twice and hid behind the curtain. Mr. Carrisford laughed for the first time that morning. Even a careful plan could begin with a little noise. They waited, listened, and chose kindness over hurry.

## Chapter 11 - Ram Dass

Ram Dass worked for Mr. Carrisford. He had come from India and cared for the monkey, who was clever, fast, and fond of trouble.

Ram Dass sometimes looked toward the school roof. From there, he could see the small attic window.

One day, he noticed Sara standing near the glass. She was wearing an old dress. Her face looked thin, but her eyes were bright as she watched the monkey play.

The monkey jumped from a chair to a table. Then he stole a piece of fruit and ran under a curtain.

Sara laughed. The sound reached Ram Dass through the open window.

“She remembers India,” he said.

Sara watched him too. She liked the way he spoke and moved. His clothes reminded her of home. She wanted to ask him about the warm streets, bright cloth, and gardens she remembered.

She lifted one hand in greeting. Ram Dass bowed.

Miss Minchin called from below, “Sara! Come here at once!”

Sara lowered her hand and hurried away.

Later, Ram Dass asked a school servant about the girl in the attic.

“That is Sara, Captain Crewe’s daughter,” the servant answered. “She used to be rich. Now she works like a servant.”

Ram Dass was surprised. He began to watch more carefully. He saw Sara carry heavy baskets. He saw her stand outside in the cold while other girls walked past her.

He also saw her stop to help Lottie, even when Sara was tired. He saw her give part of her dinner to Becky. He saw that Sara still tried to smile.

Ram Dass told Mr. Carrisford what he had learned.

“The poor child lives in the attic?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“Yes, sir. She has almost nothing.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the wall between the houses. He had spent months searching across Europe, yet the child might be on the other side of that wall.

Ram Dass began to wonder whether he could reach her.

### Ram Dass and the Monkey

The monkey’s name was Sita. She had a long tail, quick fingers, and a love of bright things. She had learned that doors could be opened by pushing, pulling, lifting, or waiting until someone else opened them.

Ram Dass had cared for her since she was small.

“You must not take the master’s watch,” he told her one morning.

Sita held the watch behind her back.

“I can see it.”

She moved it behind her other back.

“You have only two backs.”

Sita looked offended. She dropped the watch into a flower pot.

Ram Dass found it before Mr. Carrisford did. He dried it and placed it on the mantel.

“You need a new game,” he said.

Sita took a spoon from the breakfast tray.

“Not a new game. A new object.”

She tapped the spoon against the table. The sound traveled through the house.

From the other side of the wall came a faint laugh.

Ram Dass looked toward the school. He knew the girl in the attic had laughed at Sita before.

Sita tapped again.

“Are you speaking to her?” Ram Dass asked.

Sita placed the spoon on her head.

“That is not a language I understand.”

The monkey looked through the window. Ram Dass followed her gaze.

Sara stood at the attic glass. She had one hand on the frame and a book under her arm. When she saw the spoon, she smiled.

Ram Dass bowed. Sara bowed back.

Sita bowed too, but she fell forward and caught herself on the sill.

Sara laughed. Ram Dass laughed with her.

The small exchange became a habit. Sita showed Sara a spoon, a ribbon, a flower, or a button. Sara answered with a wave, a smile, or a curious gesture. Neither needed many words.

### A Servant’s Story

Ram Dass had come to England from India many years before. He missed the heat, the bright clothes, the smell of cooking, and the sound of many languages in one street.

Mr. Carrisford had treated him with respect. That was why Ram Dass stayed with him, even when the house felt sad.

One evening, Sara saw Ram Dass carrying a tray. She wanted to ask about India, but Miss Minchin called her from the stair.

“Bring the coal at once!”

Sara hurried away.

Ram Dass watched her. Later, he asked the driver about her work.

“She carries coal?”

“Coal, books, parcels, anything they tell her to carry.”

“She is a pupil.”

“She was. Her father died, and her money went with him.”

“A child cannot lose her father and her place in the same day.”

“This child did.”

Ram Dass thought of his own childhood. He had worked early too, but he had known which house was his and which people would feed him. Sara seemed to have no safe corner.

He asked what people said about her.

“Some call her proud because she speaks well. Some call her strange because she talks to a doll.”

“Does she hurt anyone?”

“No.”

“Does she help anyone?”

The driver looked toward the school. “She helps the younger girls. She helps Becky too.”

“Who is Becky?”

“The kitchen girl.”

Ram Dass made a note in his mind. The girl had a name. The servant girl had a name. Names made people visible.

### The Roof Window

Ram Dass kept watch from an upstairs room. He did not stare at Sara all day. He knew that a person deserved privacy. He looked only when he needed to understand whether she was safe.

He saw her read by the window. He saw her clean a shelf. He saw her speak to a small child who was crying.

Once, a book fell from the attic sill. It landed on the roof below. Sara looked down but could not reach it.

Ram Dass climbed out and picked it up. He held it where she could see.

Sara pressed her hands together in thanks.

He placed the book near the wall where she could reach it with a long broom.

Sara tried. The broom moved the book farther away.

Ram Dass covered his mouth to hide a smile.

Sara tried again. This time, the book came close. She caught it and held it up like a prize.

Ram Dass bowed.

Sara bowed to the broom.

The broom stood still, proud of its work.

Sita watched from behind Ram Dass. She took the broom and ran away with it.

Sara laughed so hard that she had to sit down.

Ram Dass chased the monkey across the hall. He recovered the broom, but not before Sita had placed it beside the piano.

“You have made a new friend,” Ram Dass told the broom.

### Questions Without Answers

Ram Dass wanted to ask Sara who she was. He could not shout across the wall, and he did not want to frighten her by appearing at the attic window.

He asked the school cook instead.

“The girl in the attic,” he said. “Where did she come from?”

The cook was busy cutting bread. “India, I think.”

“Her father?”

“Captain Crewe. He was rich.”

“Was?”

“He is dead.”

The cook lowered her knife. “She lost everything.”

“Does Miss Minchin know what she is doing?”

The cook gave him a tired look. “Miss Minchin knows exactly what she is doing.”

Ram Dass felt anger rise in him. He kept his voice calm.

“Does the child eat enough?”

“Not always.”

“Does she have a warm room?”

“No.”

“A coat?”

“An old one.”

The cook put down the knife. “Why do you ask?”

“Because I have seen her.”

“Many people see her.”

“Do they look?”

The cook was silent. She knew the difference.

### The Monkey’s Lesson

Sita escaped again and climbed onto the school wall. Ram Dass followed. The monkey stopped near the attic window.

Sara was there with Emily. Sita reached toward the doll.

Sara lifted Emily away.

“No,” she said. “You may look, but you may not take.”

Sita reached again.

Sara shook her head.

The monkey sat down. She looked from Sara to Emily and back.

“You understand more than you pretend,” Ram Dass said.

Sara smiled. “Perhaps she has been listening.”

Sita touched her own chest, then pointed toward Emily.

Sara did not understand. She held Emily close.

The monkey made a soft sound. Ram Dass guessed that Sita wanted Sara to show the doll.

Sara held Emily near the window. Sita leaned forward and sniffed. Then she gave a happy chirp.

“You may say hello,” Sara told her.

Sita bowed. Emily remained serious.

“Emily says hello too,” Sara added.

Ram Dass wondered how Sara could make a doll speak without moving its mouth. Then he understood. She was giving a lonely room more than one voice.

### The Name on the Paper

Ram Dass returned to Mr. Carrisford and wrote the name he had learned.

“Sara Crewe,” he said. “The school says her father was Captain Crewe.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the page. “Are you certain?”

“The cook said it.”

“Does the child know about me?”

“No.”

“Does she know about the diamonds?”

“No.”

Mr. Carrisford took the paper. His hand shook.

“She may be the child we seek.”

“She may.”

“How can we learn the truth?”

“Carefully.”

Ram Dass told him about Sara and the monkey. He told him about the book, the broom, the food, and the way Sara watched the house next door.

“She remembers India,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“Her eyes change when she sees the monkey.”

“I must speak with her.”

“Soon, but not suddenly.”

Mr. Carrisford nodded. He knew that a frightened child might run from help as quickly as from danger.

Ram Dass folded the paper. “Let me watch one more night.”

That night, he saw Sara close the attic window. She lifted Emily to the bed. Then she placed one small crumb near the wall.

Melchisedec came out and took it.

Ram Dass did not know the rat’s name. He only saw Sara share her food with a creature that could give her nothing in return.

“She may be the child,” he said to himself. “But she is also the person her father hoped she would be.”

### A Closer Look

Ram Dass learned Sara’s daily path. He did not follow her. He simply remembered where she appeared: the schoolroom door in the morning, the kitchen stairs at noon, and the attic window at night.

He saw that Sara carried books even when nobody had asked her to. She tucked one under her arm before she carried coal. She put a page marker between the pages so the book would not lose its place.

“A person who saves a place in a book may know how to save a place in a heart,” Ram Dass told Sita.

Sita took the page marker and ran behind a curtain.

“Return that.”

The monkey held it between her teeth.

Ram Dass offered a banana. Sita dropped the marker into a flower pot and took the banana.

“You understand trade,” he said.

Sita peeled the banana and placed the peel on the page marker.

Ram Dass sighed. “You understand only the first half.”

He rescued the marker and washed it. Then he looked toward Sara’s window again.

### The School Servant

Ram Dass spoke with the young driver a second time. The driver was called Thomas. He had worked near the school for several years and knew which doors opened, which bells stuck, and which girls tried to hide biscuits in their pockets.

“Tell me about Sara,” Ram Dass said.

Thomas frowned. “Why?”

“My master may know her father.”

“Then you should ask Miss Minchin.”

“I am asking you first.”

Thomas looked at the school door. “She was treated like a little lady at first. She had a private room and fine clothes. Miss Minchin liked her father’s money.”

“And after Captain Crewe died?”

“Everything changed.”

“Did Sara complain?”

“Not much. She worked. She helped the little ones.”

“Did she ever run away?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Thomas shrugged. “Where would she go?”

Ram Dass looked down. “She should not have had to answer that question.”

Thomas agreed. “People at the school call her proud because she does not beg. I think she is simply trying to stand up.”

Ram Dass remembered those words. Trying to stand up was not the same as having a comfortable place to stand.

### A Question about Emily

Ram Dass asked whether Sara still had her doll.

Thomas smiled. “Emily? Yes. She carries that doll like a queen carries a crown.”

“What does the doll look like?”

“Tall. Dark hair. A serious face.”

“Does Sara speak to her?”

“All the time.”

“What does she say?”

“Things about palaces, prisons, and rats.”

Ram Dass almost laughed. “Rats?”

“There are rats in the attic.”

“Are they large?”

“One is.”

“Does Sara feed it?”

“When she has food.”

Ram Dass’s expression became serious. “She gives food to a rat when she is hungry herself?”

“She gives food to Becky too.”

“Who is Becky?”

“The kitchen girl.”

Thomas told him about Becky’s long work and Sara’s kindness. He said that Sara had once given Becky cake and had hidden her when she was asleep.

“The child sees people,” Ram Dass said.

“Yes.”

“Then we must make sure someone sees her.”

### The Word from the Window

Ram Dass wanted to speak to Sara without shouting. He wrote a word on a small card: India.

He held it near the window. Sara saw it. She leaned closer.

Ram Dass pointed to the card, then to himself.

Sara placed her hand over her heart. Then she pointed toward the sky, the school, and the far distance.

Ram Dass did not understand every gesture, but he understood that she missed home.

He drew a sun on the back of the card. Sara smiled.

Sita came to the window and took the card.

“No,” Ram Dass said.

The monkey folded it and hid it beneath her arm.

Sara laughed.

Ram Dass held out a second card. It had a drawing of a banana.

Sita returned the first card at once.

“You learn very quickly when fruit is involved,” Ram Dass told her.

Sara saw the trade and gave a solemn nod. She held the card with the word India against her chest.

### Ram Dass Remembers

That night, Ram Dass told Mr. Carrisford about his own childhood. He had lived in a house with a courtyard and a neem tree. He had played with a wooden cart and learned to count by sorting stones.

“I left because I wanted to see the world,” he said. “I did not know that the world could be so cold.”

Mr. Carrisford listened.

“Do you want to return?”

“Some days. Other days, I remember that people can make a home in many places.”

“Can a child make one?”

“A child can begin one. Adults must help keep it safe.”

Mr. Carrisford looked toward the school. “Sara may be the child we seek.”

“She may.”

“I keep saying that.”

“Because you want certainty.”

“Yes.”

“Certainty will come. Kindness cannot wait for it.”

Mr. Carrisford nodded. He told Ram Dass to prepare whatever was needed for the attic.

### The Small Basket

Ram Dass made a list: bread, fruit, milk, a blanket, a lamp, a chair, books, warm clothes, and food for the rats.

Sita watched him pack the basket. She removed the book three times.

“That must stay.”

Sita returned it and took the blanket.

“The blanket must also stay.”

She tried to carry the blanket under the table.

Ram Dass picked it up. “You may carry the ribbon.”

Sita accepted a red ribbon and tied it to the basket handle.

“Now you are part of the plan.”

Sita chirped.

“But you are not coming to the attic.”

Sita chirped louder.

“You will frighten the child.”

She sat on the basket.

Ram Dass looked at Mr. Carrisford. “The monkey has given her answer.”

“Perhaps she should come,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“Perhaps.”

“You have learned the word from me.”

### A Quiet Visitor

Ram Dass crossed the roof with an empty basket to test the path. The night was clear. He reached the attic door, opened it, and stood inside for only a moment.

The room smelled of dust, cold cloth, and a small animal. He saw the drawings on the wall. He saw the compass under the bed. He saw the paper star near Emily.

He did not touch anything. He only learned where the gifts could go.

When he returned, Mr. Carrisford asked, “What did you see?”

“A child’s whole life in a few objects.”

“Was she asleep?”

“Yes.”

“Did she look peaceful?”

Ram Dass thought. “She looked tired. But her hand was near the doll.”

Mr. Carrisford closed his eyes. “Then tomorrow night, we help her.”

### A Promise between Servants

The school cook knew that Ram Dass was asking questions. She found him near the kitchen door.

“If you are helping the child,” she said, “do it carefully.”

“I will.”

“Miss Minchin watches money. She watches food. She may watch the attic when she smells a secret.”

“We will leave no secret she can use.”

The cook gave him a small piece of cloth. It was clean and warm from the kitchen.

“For the girl.”

Ram Dass accepted it. “Thank you.”

“Do not thank me. Make sure she eats.”

“I will.”

The cook returned inside. Ram Dass folded the cloth into the basket.

The plan now belonged to more than one household. Sara did not know it, but several people had begun to choose her safety.

Before he left the kitchen, Ram Dass asked the cook for one more thing: a clean cup with a blue flower on it.

“Why that cup?” the cook asked.

“Because it looks cheerful.”

The cook opened the cupboard. “Cheerful cups do not warm a room.”

“No, but they may help a child believe the room can become warm.”

The cook handed it over. Ram Dass carried the cup beside the basket. He felt foolish and hopeful at the same time. The two feelings walked together, like two children sharing one umbrella.

Ram Dass tested the roof door once more. It opened without a squeak. He put oil on the hinge anyway.

“A quiet door is a helpful door,” he said.

The monkey watched him and copied the motion with one finger. Then it tried to oil its own ear. Ram Dass caught it just in time.

“Not every door is a hinge,” he told the monkey.

The monkey looked disappointed, then stole an orange peel from the basket. Even careful plans had to leave room for monkey trouble.

## Chapter 12 - The Other Side of the Wall

Mr. Carrisford continued the search for Captain Crewe’s daughter. He wrote more letters and asked more questions.

“We know she was called Sara,” he said. “We know her father placed her in a school. But which school?”

He did not yet understand that the answer stood beside his house.

Ram Dass understood more each day. He saw Sara through the attic window. He saw that she had no warm coat. He saw her hold herself straight when Miss Minchin spoke harshly.

One evening, snow began to fall. Sara came back from an errand with wet shoes. She had not eaten enough since breakfast.

Ram Dass watched her climb the stairs. He felt angry, but he knew anger alone would not help her.

He studied the roof. The houses were close. A careful person could cross from Mr. Carrisford’s side to the school’s side. From there, a small door might lead to the attic.

Ram Dass told his master about the path.

“Can you reach her room?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“I believe so. But we must be quiet. If the school sees me, the child may be punished.”

Mr. Carrisford nodded. “Help her without frightening her.”

The plan began with simple things: a warm blanket, a good meal, a chair, and a few books. Mr. Carrisford wanted Sara to feel safe before he asked questions.

Across the wall, Sara knew nothing of this plan. She sat beside Becky and tried to make a fire from a few poor pieces of coal.

“The attic needs a magic spell,” Becky said.

“We shall imagine one,” Sara replied.

Sara and the people searching for her were separated by only a wall. The truth was very near, but it still had not crossed the distance.

### More Letters

Mr. Carrisford sent letters to three more schools. He wrote Sara’s name in large, careful letters. He described Captain Crewe, India, and a doll called Emily.

The first school answered, “We have no child by that name.”

The second school answered, “We once had a Sara, but she left years ago.”

The third school sent no answer at all.

Mr. Carrisford placed the letters in the search book. The book had become heavy with paper.

“Perhaps the school next door has forgotten her,” he said.

“People forget many things when money changes,” Ram Dass replied.

“They should not forget a child.”

“Then we will remember for them.”

Mr. Carrisford wrote the school’s name on a new page. He had passed it many times, but he had never asked a direct question. He feared that Miss Minchin might hide the truth if she knew he was looking.

“If she is Sara, why has no letter reached us?” he asked.

“Perhaps no one sent one.”

“Why would a school hide a child?”

“People hide things when they want to keep what belongs to them.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the diamond reports. The money belonged to Captain Crewe’s daughter. If the school knew this, it might not want to lose the child’s fees and good name.

“We need proof,” he said.

“We need care first.”

Ram Dass was right. A question asked too loudly could close a door.

### The Roof Path

Ram Dass walked through Mr. Carrisford’s house and studied the roof windows. He measured the distance to the school with a piece of string.

“You cannot measure a roof with string,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“I can if the string is patient.”

“Is it?”

“It has not complained.”

Ram Dass tied one end of the string to a railing. He carried the other end along the roof. The wind lifted it, and it wrapped around his ankle.

“The string has changed its mind,” he said.

He untangled it and tried again. A narrow roof led toward the school. A low wall separated the houses. Beyond the wall, a small door stood near the attic.

“That door may be used by the chimney workers,” Ram Dass explained.

“Can it be opened?”

“I do not know.”

“Find out.”

Ram Dass looked at the snow. “Not during a storm.”

“Why not?”

“Because roofs become rivers, and rivers are poor places for walking.”

They waited for a clear night. Ram Dass carried no heavy basket at first. He crossed part of the path, tested each stone, and returned.

“The way is possible,” he said.

Mr. Carrisford was relieved. “Then you can reach her.”

“Possibly. The door is locked.”

“Can it be opened?”

“Possibly.”

“You use the word possibly very often.”

“It is a safe word.”

### Sara’s Day of Snow

On the same clear night, Sara returned from an errand with snow on her hair. She had carried a parcel to the far side of the city. Her fingers felt stiff.

Becky rubbed them between her own hands.

“They are cold.”

“They are thinking about summer.”

“Your fingers are not thinkers.”

“You have not asked them.”

Becky placed a small cup of warm water near the fire. Sara held her hands above it.

“Miss Minchin should give you a coat,” Becky said.

“She says the old one is enough.”

“It is not enough.”

“Then we must make it believe it is enough.”

Becky shook her head. “You cannot pretend your coat is warm.”

“I can pretend it is a proud coat. Proud coats do not admit weakness.”

“That is foolish.”

“It is also sometimes useful.”

The snow made the attic window bright. Sara looked across the wall toward the next house. A lamp glowed behind the curtains.

“Do you think the Indian Gentleman is awake?” she asked.

“Who?”

“The man next door.”

“Why do you call him that?”

“He came from India. I have seen his servant.”

“Have you spoken to him?”

“No. I do not know his name.”

Sara drew a picture of the house. She added a monkey, a lamp, and a door.

“There are too many doors,” Becky said.

“A house should have choices.”

“The school has doors, but they do not lead to choices.”

Sara looked at the drawing. “Then we shall draw a better house.”

She added a wide door between the two buildings. The door had a brass handle.

“It is not real,” Becky said.

“Not yet.”

### The Wall Between Houses

Sara became interested in the wall. She tapped it with one finger.

“What are you doing?” Becky asked.

“Listening.”

“To what?”

“A house.”

“Houses do not speak.”

“They creak, breathe, and hold secrets. That is a kind of speaking.”

She pressed her ear to the wall. At first, she heard nothing. Then a faint sound reached her: a footstep, a door, and the soft chitter of a monkey.

“The house is busy,” she said.

Becky listened too. “I hear the monkey.”

“He may be telling a story.”

“He sounds as if he is asking for a banana.”

“That may be the beginning of the story.”

Sara imagined the monkey moving through the other house. He crossed rooms that she could not see. He knew the warm places. He probably knew where the food was kept.

“If you were a monkey,” Sara asked, “would you like a cold attic?”

“No.”

“Would you visit it?”

“Only if someone had a banana.”

“Then we must keep a banana.”

“We do not have one.”

“That makes the plan difficult.”

They laughed quietly. The wall did not answer, but the sound of the monkey continued on the other side.

### A Secret Question

Ram Dass asked a school servant whether Sara had ever lived in India.

“She says she did,” the servant answered.

“Do you believe her?”

“Why would she lie?”

“Some people tell stories.”

“That child tells stories because she has no one else to talk to.”

Ram Dass lowered his voice. “What was her father’s name?”

“Captain Crewe.”

“Was he a friend of Mr. Carrisford?”

The servant looked around the hall. “I do not know.”

“Does Miss Minchin know?”

“Miss Minchin knows many things.”

Ram Dass thanked her and left. He did not repeat the question to anyone else. He had learned enough to know that the school was not a safe place for questions.

That evening, he told Mr. Carrisford everything.

“The name fits,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“The age fits.”

“India fits.”

“The doll?”

“We must ask about Emily.”

Mr. Carrisford rose. “I will go now.”

Ram Dass placed a hand on his arm. “You are not strong enough to cross the roof.”

“Then take me by the stairs.”

“The school may not let you in.”

“I own the house next door. I can knock.”

“If you knock before we know the truth, Miss Minchin may move the child.”

Mr. Carrisford sat again. He understood the danger. The child was close, but closeness did not make the rescue simple.

### A Room of Pictures

Sara and Becky made pictures of the warm room they wished for. Sara drew a fire. Becky drew a table with bread. Sara drew a chair. Becky drew a thick blanket.

“What will be on the wall?” Sara asked.

“A picture of a garden.”

“With flowers?”

“And a cat.”

“A real cat?”

“A picture cat. Real cats steal food.”

Sara added books. Becky added a small shelf for her buttons. Sara added a window looking over a garden.

“What is outside the window?” Becky asked.

“The sky.”

“That is not enough.”

“A tree.”

“And a bird.”

“A bird with a message.”

“What message?”

Sara thought of her father. “The message says, Keep going.”

Becky drew the bird. It had very large feet.

“It will not fly,” Sara said.

“It can fly if it wants to.”

“That is a good rule for birds.”

They put the pictures on the wall. The attic looked no warmer, but it held a plan made by two people.

### The Quiet Decision

Ram Dass returned to the roof after midnight. He tested the attic door again. The lock was old. He could open it with a key from the house, but the door squeaked.

He oiled the hinge and closed it gently. The squeak became a small whisper.

“Better,” he said.

He would need a basket. He would need cloth to cover the things inside. He would need a lamp that did not show from the street.

Mr. Carrisford wanted to send a servant with him.

“No,” Ram Dass said. “Too many feet make too much noise.”

“Then take the monkey.”

“The monkey makes more noise than ten feet.”

Sita climbed onto the table and looked offended.

“She can stay here,” Ram Dass said.

Sita pulled a ribbon from his pocket.

“She has already decided to come.”

Mr. Carrisford smiled. “Perhaps she knows the child.”

“Perhaps she knows where the food is.”

“Both may be true.”

They prepared a first basket and put it near the door. The basket held a blanket, a loaf of bread, fruit, a small lamp, and a book.

### The Door in the Dream

In the morning, Sara told Becky about the dream. “There was a door between the houses,” she said.

“Did you open it?”

“I tried.”

“Was it locked?”

“No. I woke up first.”

Becky looked toward the wall. “Dream doors are not real.”

“Some real doors begin as dreams.”

They drew the dream door on a fresh sheet. Sara gave it a brass handle. Becky gave it a window.

“Doors do not have windows,” Becky said.

“This one wants to see who is coming.”

Lottie found the drawing and added a cat on top of the door.

“The cat will fall,” Sara said.

“It has nine lives.”

“That is not a reason to waste one.”

Ermengarde added a sign: Please knock kindly.

Sara liked the sign. A door could be opened by a key, but a home should also be opened by kindness.

### Mr. Carrisford Waits

Mr. Carrisford wanted to cross the wall at once. He sat near the window and watched the attic. Sara came to the glass with the borrowed shawl around her shoulders.

He lifted his hand. Sara lifted hers.

He wanted to call her name. He did not.

Ram Dass brought tea. “You have been sitting here for an hour.”

“I am thinking.”

“You are also holding the cup without drinking.”

Mr. Carrisford took a sip. It had gone cold.

“The tea has waited too long.”

“Then it understands your problem.”

Mr. Carrisford looked toward the school. “If she is Sara, she has already waited years.”

“One more night will not change that.”

“It may feel like another year.”

“Then make the night useful.”

Mr. Carrisford opened the basket list. He checked each item. The blanket was thick. The bread was fresh. The lamp had oil. The books were suitable for a child.

“What if she refuses the gifts?”

“Then we leave them and try again.”

“What if Miss Minchin finds them?”

“Then we learn that the path is not safe.”

“What if Sara thinks we are playing a cruel game?”

Ram Dass was quiet. “Then we must be ready to explain.”

Mr. Carrisford nodded. He had wanted to fix the story with one grand act. Ram Dass kept reminding him that trust grew by small steps.

### A Snowy Errand

Sara was sent outside during a snowfall. Miss Minchin gave her a parcel and told her to return quickly.

“The streets are slippery,” Becky said.

“I will walk slowly.”

“Miss Minchin said quickly.”

“Then I will walk quickly and slowly at the same time.”

“That is not possible.”

“It is a new skill.”

Sara wrapped the parcel in her coat. Snow gathered on her hair. At the corner, she saw a man drop a bundle of papers.

The papers spread across the wet street.

Sara stopped. She was already late, but she helped gather them. The man thanked her.

“You will be punished for stopping,” he said.

“Perhaps. But the papers were becoming wet.”

She reached the address and delivered the parcel. On the way back, her shoes were soaked.

At the school door, Miss Minchin looked at the clock.

“You are late.”

“I stopped to help a man.”

“You should have continued.”

“The papers would have been ruined.”

“That is not your concern.”

Sara removed the snow from her sleeve. “It became my concern when I saw it.”

Miss Minchin sent her to the attic without supper.

Becky had saved a small crust. “You were right to stop.”

“I was also very cold.”

“Both can be true.”

Sara ate the crust. She heard the wall creak.

“The house is speaking,” she said.

“What does it say?”

“It says the night is not finished.”

### The Hidden Key

Ram Dass returned to the roof with the key to the attic door. He had tied it to a thin cord around his wrist. The wind pulled at his coat.

He reached the low wall and tested the tile near the mark. It held. He stepped to the attic door and turned the key.

The door opened a finger’s width. Cold air came from inside.

Ram Dass looked through the gap. He saw the empty room, the paper pictures, and the edge of Sara’s bed.

He did not enter yet. He wanted to know whether the girls were awake.

From inside came Becky’s voice. “The house is speaking.”

Sara answered, “It says the night is not finished.”

Ram Dass smiled. He closed the door and returned.

“They are awake,” he told Mr. Carrisford.

“Then wait.”

“Until they sleep.”

“Yes.”

### A Plan for Trust

Mr. Carrisford made a new plan. He would not put all the gifts in the room at once. Ram Dass would place the first things quietly. If Sara accepted them, more could follow.

“What is first?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“Fire and food.”

“The blanket?”

“After the fire.”

“The books?”

“After she is warm.”

“The truth?”

“After she knows we mean no harm.”

Mr. Carrisford wrote the order on a card. Fire. Food. Warmth. Books. Truth.

Sita took the card and ran.

“She has taken the plan.”

“She may know a better order.”

They found the monkey under a table. She had placed the card beside a banana.

“She wants food first,” Ram Dass said.

“The monkey agrees with the child.”

“The monkey agrees with the banana.”

They recovered the card. Mr. Carrisford added a note: Do not frighten her.

### The Last Look Across the Wall

Before midnight, Sara went to the attic window. She saw the dark roof and the faint light in the neighboring house.

“Good night, Indian Gentleman,” she whispered.

She did not know Mr. Carrisford was standing near the same window.

“Good night, Sara,” he whispered.

The wall held both voices, though neither child nor man heard the other.

Sara closed the curtain and lay down. Becky slept in the next room. Melchisedec moved behind the wall. The compass rested beneath Sara’s pillow.

On the other side, Ram Dass lifted the basket. He tied the rope. He covered the lamp. He checked the key.

Mr. Carrisford sat by the fire and waited.

The two houses were ready. The truth was ready. Sara was tired enough to sleep.

The first quiet step toward the Magic was about to cross the roof.

In the morning, Mr. Carrisford checked the roof from his own window. The tiles were wet. A small branch lay across the path between the houses.

“Not today,” Ram Dass said.

Mr. Carrisford sighed. “The roof has made its decision.”

“The roof is older than both of us. We should listen.”

They waited until the rain stopped. While they waited, Ram Dass found a paper star on the floor near the window. It had blown from the other side. On one point, Sara had written the word “hope.”

Mr. Carrisford held it carefully. “She made this.”

“Then she has sent a message without knowing it.”

“We must answer without making noise.”

Ram Dass placed the star beside the basket. The visit was delayed, but the plan had gained a sign. They would cross only when the path was safe, and they would carry hope with them.

“Will that be enough?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“Enough for one night.”

“And after that?”

“We will learn more.”

Across the wall, Sara fell asleep beside her drawings. She dreamed of a door with a brass handle. She did not know who stood on the other side. She only knew that the door was waiting.

## Chapter 13 - One of the Populace

The cold grew stronger. Sara walked through a street covered with dirty snow. Her shoes hurt, and her stomach felt empty.

Then she saw something shining near the curb. It was a coin.

Sara picked it up and looked at the bakery window. Warm bread sat behind the glass. The smell came through the door whenever someone entered.

She went inside and used the coin to buy several hot buns.

The first bun warmed her hands. She was about to take a bite when she saw a little girl outside.

The girl was thin and dirty. She stood near the wall with her arms around herself. Her name was Anne, though Sara did not know it yet.

Sara stepped outside.

“Are you hungry?” she asked.

The girl nodded.

Sara looked at the buns. She was very hungry too. For one moment, she wanted to run away and eat every one.

Then she remembered the girl’s face.

“Here,” Sara said. “Take this.”

Anne ate the bun quickly.

Sara gave her another, and another. The bakery woman watched from the doorway.

“You should keep some for yourself,” the woman said.

“I will keep one,” Sara answered.

But after Anne finished, Sara gave her the last bun too. Her hands were empty again.

The bakery woman stared at Sara’s worn clothes. “You have hardly anything.”

Sara brushed snow from her sleeve. “I still have more than she has.”

The woman’s face changed. She brought Sara a warm drink and another small piece of bread.

“Please take this,” she said.

Sara thanked her. She did not feel like a grand lady giving gifts. She felt like a hungry child who had found another hungry child.

As Sara walked back to the school, she thought about the word “poor.” A person could have little money and still have something to give. A kind choice did not need a full purse.

The bakery woman remembered Sara. She also remembered Anne.

### The Bakery Door

The bakery stood on a busy corner. Its windows were steamed by warmth from the ovens. Loaves sat in rows behind the glass. Some were round, some long, and some so large that Sara thought they might need names.

The woman who worked there was called Mrs. Brown. She had flour on her sleeves and a red mark on one cheek from the heat of the oven.

When Sara first found the coin, Mrs. Brown had watched her count the buns.

“You may have a small loaf instead,” Mrs. Brown had said.

“The buns are easier to share.”

“You are sharing them?”

Sara had nodded toward Anne.

Mrs. Brown had looked at Anne, then at Sara’s worn coat. She understood that the child before her was hungry too.

“Keep one,” she had said.

Sara had tried. Then she had given that one away.

Now, as Sara returned to the bakery, she saw the same red-cheeked woman at the counter.

“I remember you,” Mrs. Brown said.

“I remember you too.”

“You were the girl with the coin.”

“Yes.”

“And the buns.”

“Yes.”

“You gave them to Anne.”

Sara looked toward the back room. “Is she here?”

“She is helping.”

Mrs. Brown wiped her hands on her apron. “Come in. Becky may wait by the door.”

Becky stepped inside. Warm air touched her face. She closed her eyes.

“The bakery smells like a birthday,” she said.

“It smells like bread,” Sara replied.

“Bread is sometimes a birthday.”

Mrs. Brown smiled. “You may sit for a moment.”

Becky sat on a stool. Sara looked at the shelves and the baskets. She remembered being outside with empty hands. The memory had not become smaller just because she was now rich.

### Anne’s Story

Anne came from the back room carrying a small tray. She was still thin, but her eyes looked brighter. Her dress had been mended at the sleeves.

“Sara,” she said.

“Hello, Anne.”

Anne looked at Becky. “Is she the girl who was with you?”

“She is my friend.”

“I am Becky,” Becky said.

Anne put down the tray. “Mrs. Brown lets me sleep in the room upstairs.”

“Do you have work?” Sara asked.

“I carry bread, sweep the floor, and help count the loaves.”

“Counting is important,” Becky said.

“It is not hard.”

“Wait until the loaves begin to hide.”

Anne smiled. “They do not hide.”

Mrs. Brown pointed at a basket. “One did this morning.”

Anne told Sara that she had lived with other poor children near the market. She had sometimes slept under a stair. A woman had promised her work and then left without paying her. She had been too hungry to search for another place.

“Mrs. Brown found me near the shop,” Anne said. “She gave me soup.”

“And a bed,” Mrs. Brown added.

“And a bed.”

Sara looked at Mrs. Brown. “You helped her.”

“She needed help.”

“You did not know her.”

“I knew that she was cold.”

Sara remembered the day when Mrs. Brown had given her bread after Sara gave away the buns.

“You helped me too,” Sara said.

Mrs. Brown waved one hand. “You had given away your food. Someone had to make sure you did not fall over.”

“It mattered.”

“Small things matter when a person is hungry.”

Anne looked at Sara. “Why did you give me the buns?”

Sara did not give a grand answer. “I saw you.”

Anne nodded. “Most people walked past.”

“I know.”

The bakery grew quiet around them. The oven made a low, steady sound. A loaf cracked as it cooled.

“Did you ever feel angry?” Anne asked.

“Yes.”

“At me?”

“No. At the cold. At the hunger. At people who could see and still walk away.”

Anne touched the tray. “I was angry too.”

“You were allowed.”

“Mrs. Brown says I may be angry, but I must not throw bread.”

“That is a good rule.”

“Once I threw a potato.”

“Did it deserve it?” Becky asked.

Anne thought. “It was a very hard potato.”

Becky nodded. “Then perhaps it had started the fight.”

Mrs. Brown laughed. The bakery became warm in a new way.

### Sara’s Plan

Sara asked Mrs. Brown how many children came to the shop each day.

“Some days, ten. Some days, more.”

“How much bread could you give them?”

Mrs. Brown looked uncertain. “Bread costs money.”

“I can pay for it.”

“You should not spend your money on every hungry child in London.”

“I cannot help every child. I can help the ones who come here.”

Mrs. Brown folded her arms. “Why do you want to do this?”

Sara looked at the door. Outside, the street was cold. “Because I know what it feels like to have no food. Because Anne was hungry. Because you gave me more when I had nothing.”

“You are not poor now.”

“That is why I can help.”

Mrs. Brown did not answer quickly. She understood that Sara was not trying to buy praise. The child had remembered a kindness and wanted it to travel onward.

“What will you pay for?” Mrs. Brown asked.

“A loaf or a bun for each child who needs one. Soup if you can make it. Milk for the smallest children.”

“And who decides who needs it?”

“You do. You know the neighborhood.”

“What if someone lies?”

“Then we will still have fed a child.”

Mrs. Brown looked at Sara carefully. “That could become expensive.”

“Then we will make a clear plan.”

Sara took out a small notebook. She wrote columns: name, food, date, and note.

Becky leaned over the page. “You are making another list.”

“Lists help food find people.”

Anne pointed to the first line. “Can I help write?”

“Yes. Your letters are good.”

Anne looked proud. She wrote her name at the top.

“That is not a child who needs bread,” Becky said.

“She needs a job,” Sara replied. “That belongs in another list.”

Mrs. Brown brought a clean slate. “The first day will be tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow is soon,” Becky said.

“Hungry children do not need a long meeting,” Sara answered.

### The First Food Basket

The next morning, Sara returned with a basket. She had asked Mr. Carrisford’s cook for bread, fruit, and a pot of soup. Becky carried spoons. Anne carried cups. Mrs. Brown set up a table near the wall.

The first child to arrive was a boy with a cap too large for his head. He stood outside and would not enter.

“You may come in,” Sara told him.

“Do I have to pay?”

“No.”

“Do I have to sing?”

“Only if the soup asks.”

The boy came in. He ate slowly at first, then quickly.

Another child arrived with two sisters. A little girl held a broken wooden horse.

“Does the horse eat soup?” Becky asked.

“No,” the girl said. “He guards it.”

“Then he may guard the bowls.”

The children sat at the table. Anne poured milk. She spilled a little and looked frightened.

“A milk river,” Sara said. “It is traveling toward the cloth.”

Anne wiped it up. The girl with the wooden horse helped.

Mrs. Brown watched the children eat. The shop became busy, but it did not feel troubled. Each child received food and a place to sit.

One boy took two buns and tried to hide one in his coat.

Sara saw him. She did not shout.

“Would you like to save it for later?”

The boy froze.

“Yes.”

“Then wrap it in paper. Bread deserves a better pocket.”

The boy wrapped the bun. He had expected punishment. Instead, he learned that he could ask.

### A Fair Rule

Sara and Mrs. Brown made rules for the food table. Children had to wash their hands. They had to wait for the smallest child to receive a cup. Nobody could push. Anyone who needed more could ask.

“What if someone asks for a third bun?” Anne asked.

“We ask why,” Sara said.

“What if the reason is hunger?”

“Then we give the bun.”

“What if the reason is that buns are delicious?”

“That is a true reason, but hunger comes first.”

Becky wrote the rules. Her letters were still slow, but she could read each line.

“You should add no throwing potatoes,” Anne said.

Mrs. Brown pointed at her. “That rule is for you.”

Anne added it at the bottom.

The food table continued through the week. Some children came once. Some came every day. Sara did not ask them to tell sad stories before receiving food. Hunger did not need to perform.

She listened when a child wanted to speak. She also allowed children to eat in peace.

### The Bakery’s New Helper

Anne became good at the work. She knew which loaves were ready, which shelves were clean, and which children needed the smallest cups.

“You are becoming the keeper of the bread,” Sara said.

“I am becoming the keeper of the cups.”

“A cup is a small bowl.”

“It still needs a keeper.”

Anne had a place to sleep, work to do, and food to eat. She was not rich, but she was safe. Sara thought that safety was a kind of wealth too.

One afternoon, Anne found the old coin that Sara had used to buy the buns. Mrs. Brown had kept it in a little box.

“It is not worth much,” Mrs. Brown said.

“It bought a great deal,” Sara answered.

“Only a few buns.”

“It bought a choice.”

The coin had helped Sara decide to share. Mrs. Brown’s kindness had helped the choice continue. Now the bakery fed many children. The coin was small, but its story had grown.

### Sara Remembers

When Sara returned home, she found a warm room and a full table. She ate with Becky, Ermengarde, and Lottie. She did not hide the food, but she noticed how quickly she remembered hiding crumbs in the attic.

Lottie asked about the bakery.

“Did the children have cake?”

“Not today.”

“Why not?”

“Cake is for special days.”

“Food is special every day.”

Sara smiled. “That is a wise rule.”

Ermengarde asked whether Sara was happy now that she could give money away.

“I am happy that the children have food. I am not happy that they needed to be hungry first.”

Becky put down her spoon. “You remember too much.”

“I remember enough.”

“Does the memory hurt?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then why keep it?”

Sara looked at the table. “Because it helps me notice other people.”

She did not want her hard months to become a story about herself alone. If she remembered hunger, she could see hunger. If she remembered loneliness, she could look for lonely people. Her fortune could buy food, but her memory told her where to take it.

Outside, London remained cold. Inside the bakery, the ovens were warm. Children came through the door. Anne welcomed them. Mrs. Brown filled bowls. Sara paid the bill.

The first choice had been one coin and a few buns. The next choices were larger, but they came from the same place: a child who had almost nothing decided that another child should not have less.

### The Rule about Asking

Sara noticed that some children stood outside the bakery because they were afraid to ask. She made a card with three words: Come in. Sit down. Ask.

Anne placed it beside the door.

“What if they cannot read?” Anne asked.

“Then we will say the words.”

The boy with the large cap read them aloud. His little sister repeated them.

“Come in,” she said.

“Sit down,” Anne answered.

“Ask,” Becky finished.

The three children laughed. The words became a small welcome.

One rainy day, a girl stood outside with a baby wrapped in a thin cloth. She would not enter.

Sara went to the door. “Come in.”

The girl shook her head. “I only need bread for my brother.”

“Then come in for the bread.”

“I have no money.”

“You do not need money.”

“I cannot stay.”

“You may take the bread outside. But the baby is wet.”

The girl looked down. Anne brought a dry cloth. Becky brought warm milk. Mrs. Brown moved a chair near the oven.

The girl entered. She did not sit until Sara pulled out the chair.

“You may sit,” Sara said. “The chair is strong.”

The baby opened one eye.

“He is checking the shop,” Becky said.

The girl smiled for the first time. She sat until the baby was warm. Then she took bread for her brother.

The next day, she returned and asked for work. Mrs. Brown gave her a few hours of sweeping and paid her fairly. The food table had helped one child eat, but the open door had helped a family find a safer path.

Sara wrote a new line in the ledger: Food first. Questions later. Help where possible.

The words were simple. The work of following them was not always simple. That was why the bakery needed many hands.

On Saturday, Mrs. Brown set a second table near the back door. It was only a narrow board on two wooden boxes, but Anne polished it until it shone.

“This is for bread?” Anne asked.

“Bread, apples, and questions that can wait,” Mrs. Brown said.

Becky carried a basket from the kitchen. Sara carried the ledger. A small boy named Pip came through the door with his baby sister on his hip. He looked at the table, then at Mrs. Brown.

“Is this for everyone?” he asked.

“It is for anyone who is hungry,” Mrs. Brown said.

Pip stood very straight. “I am not begging.”

“Good,” said Becky. “We are not making you beg. We are helping you eat.”

Pip looked relieved, but his baby sister reached for a roll. He broke it in half and gave her the softer piece.

“You may take one for yourself too,” Sara said.

“What if someone else needs it?”

“Then we will see what we can do. Sharing is not the same as disappearing.”

Pip took a roll. Anne wrote his name in the ledger, then crossed it out and wrote it again more neatly.

“I am practicing,” she explained.

Soon three more children arrived. One needed food for a grandmother. One needed a quiet place to sit. One needed directions to a safe lodging house. The bakery became busy, but it did not become unkind.

Mrs. Brown made a rule about the table: no pushing, no laughing at worn clothes, and no taking more than a family could carry. When the last loaf was gone, everyone helped sweep the floor.

Pip returned the next week with a drawing of the bakery. In his picture, the door was enormous. Above it he had written, “Come in.” Sara pinned the drawing beside the food table.

The shop still sold bread to customers. It also became a place where a hungry person could stand without shame. That was a new kind of business, and it needed care every day.

At closing time, Anne counted the empty baskets. She was tired, but she was not sad.

“Tomorrow we need more bread,” she said.

Mrs. Brown nodded. “Tomorrow we will bake more.”

Sara added a fresh line to the ledger: “A small table can grow if people bring their hands.”

Pip read the line over her shoulder. “I can bring hands,” he said.

“You can bring your own two,” Becky told him. “That is enough to start.”

Pip held up both hands. Flour dust covered his fingers. The bakery door closed on a busy day, but its welcome remained ready for the morning.

Anne swept the flour from his fingers with a clean cloth. “You may help tomorrow,” she said.

Pip nodded. His baby sister reached for the cloth, and everyone laughed when she tried to wear it as a hat. The bakery had found another pair of hands, and the pair came with a very small, very determined helper.

Mrs. Brown gave Pip a wooden spoon. “You may stir when you are tall enough.”

Pip stood on his toes. “I am almost tall enough.”

“Almost is a useful place to begin.”

He held the spoon with both hands. The baby clapped, the spoon wobbled, and one puff of flour flew into the air. It landed on Becky's nose.

“A baker's mark,” Becky said.

Pip grinned. The work was not perfect, but everyone had a place in it.

## Chapter 14 - What Melchisedec Heard and Saw

Sara grew weaker as the winter days passed. She worked from morning until night. Sometimes she climbed the stairs and had to stop halfway because the room moved around her.

Becky worried about her.

“You must eat,” Becky said.

“I know.”

“Knowing is not the same as eating.”

“That is a very wise thing to say for someone who once tried to eat a candle.”

“It looked like a sweet.”

Sara smiled, but she could not laugh for long.

She tried to make the attic beautiful in her mind. The bed became a golden bed. The cracked wall became a painted wall. Melchisedec became the guard of the palace.

“The guard is very small,” Becky said.

“He is small because he moves through secret places.”

Melchisedec listened from his hole. The rat saw Sara lie down without supper. He saw her hands shake as she turned the pages of a book.

Across the roof, Ram Dass saw the same things. He told Mr. Carrisford that Sara was in danger from cold and hunger.

Mr. Carrisford looked troubled. “We must act now.”

Ram Dass studied the roof again. He found a way to climb from the next house to the school attic. He could carry things in a basket and leave without being seen.

“I will go tonight,” he promised.

Mr. Carrisford ordered warm food, blankets, a small fire, a chair, and books. He wanted to send more, but Ram Dass warned him that too much at once might frighten Sara.

“She has been alone for a long time,” Ram Dass said. “Kindness must enter quietly.”

In the attic, Sara whispered to Emily. “If the princess cannot be saved today, she must save her courage until tomorrow.”

Melchisedec came out and sat near her hand. He could not explain the secret plan. He could only stay close.

Unseen help was moving over the roof.

### A Day That Would Not End

Sara’s day began with a coal scuttle. She carried it to the schoolroom, then to the parlor, then to a bedroom where the fire had gone out. The scuttle cut into her fingers.

“Put it down,” Becky said.

“The coal will not walk by itself.”

“It should learn.”

“It is not a very bright pupil.”

Becky took one side of the scuttle. Together they carried it upstairs. At the landing, Sara stopped.

“The stairs are moving.”

“The stairs are still.”

“Then I am moving.”

Becky helped her sit. Sara pressed her hands to her eyes. Black dots floated in front of them.

“You must tell Miss Minchin,” Becky said.

“She will say I am making excuses.”

“You are not making excuses. You are falling.”

Sara looked at the coal. “It has not fallen.”

“You are more important than the coal.”

Sara wanted to believe that. She stood again and carried the scuttle the last few steps.

In the room, Miss Minchin inspected the fire.

“It is not hot enough.”

Sara looked at the small pile of coal. “It needs more.”

“Then bring more.”

Becky’s hands closed into fists. Sara touched her sleeve.

“We will bring it,” Sara said.

They returned to the kitchen. Sara carried the next scuttle alone. She moved slowly, one stair at a time.

### The Missed Meal

At noon, Sara was sent to deliver a parcel. She returned after the meal had ended. The kitchen door was closed, and nobody had saved her plate.

She looked at the empty table.

“There is no food,” Becky said.

“Perhaps the food has gone for a walk.”

“It should have left a note.”

Sara drank water. It made her stomach feel full for a moment.

Becky searched a cupboard and found half a carrot.

“This is not enough.”

“It is a beginning.”

They cut the carrot into two pieces. Sara gave Becky the larger piece.

“You need it.”

“I have a plan.”

“Your plan is to give away the carrot.”

“It is a very small plan.”

Becky gave back half. “A plan can be shared.”

Sara ate the piece slowly. She thought about the bakery, but it was far away and she had no coin.

Her hands went to her coat pocket. She found a bit of thread and a smooth marble. Neither was food.

“The marble cannot help,” Becky said.

“It can remind me that not every round thing is an apple.”

“That is not helpful either.”

Sara smiled weakly. “It is accurate.”

### Melchisedec Keeps Watch

Melchisedec had learned Sara’s footsteps. He knew the quick steps meant an errand. He knew the slow steps meant she was tired. He knew the pause near the door meant that she was listening for Miss Minchin.

He came out when Sara entered the attic.

“Good evening, Mayor,” she said.

Melchisedec looked at her empty hands.

Sara had one small crumb in her pocket. She placed it near him.

The rat did not take it at once. He moved closer to Sara’s shoe.

“You may eat it,” Sara said. “It has no other plans.”

Melchisedec sniffed the crumb, then carried it toward the nest.

“You are taking it home.”

The white-nosed rat appeared. Melchisedec gave the crumb to him.

Sara watched. “You are a generous mayor.”

Becky sat on the bed. “He is a rat.”

“A rat can have good manners.”

“He lives in a wall.”

“Some important people live in large houses and still have bad manners.”

Becky smiled, but her eyes were worried.

Sara leaned against the wall. “I am tired.”

“I know.”

“If I sleep, I may miss a job.”

“You need sleep more than the job needs you.”

Sara closed her eyes. “That sounds like one of our rules.”

“Rule one.”

“A tired person may sit for one minute.”

“You have sat for three.”

“Then I am a criminal.”

“I will hide you from the law.”

Sara laughed softly. The laugh became a yawn.

She slept for twenty minutes. Becky watched the door. Melchisedec watched the floor. The attic guards took their jobs seriously.

### Ram Dass Studies the Roof

On the other side of the wall, Ram Dass waited until the house was quiet. He carried a small lamp and went upstairs.

The roof path was dangerous in the dark. Tiles were wet. Snow sat in the corners. The wind pushed at his clothes.

He tied a rope around a strong post before he stepped outside. The rope was not long enough to reach the school attic. It was only for the first part of the path, where the roof sloped sharply.

Mr. Carrisford stood at the window.

“Be careful,” he called.

“I will.”

“Take the basket.”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

“I must learn the path with empty hands.”

Ram Dass crossed to the low wall. He tested each tile with his foot. A loose tile moved, so he marked it with a piece of chalk and stepped around it.

The attic door stood beyond the wall. He reached it and touched the handle.

The door did not open.

He tried the key. The lock turned, but the hinge made a sharp sound.

Ram Dass stopped. He listened.

No one came.

He opened the door a little more. The hinge whispered instead of squeaking. He closed it and returned by the same path.

“The path is possible,” he told Mr. Carrisford.

“And the door?”

“Possible.”

Mr. Carrisford smiled. “You have found a safe word.”

“A safe word is useful on a roof.”

### Choosing the Gifts

Mr. Carrisford wanted to fill the basket with everything a child might want. Ram Dass made him choose carefully.

“One blanket,” Ram Dass said.

“Two.”

“One.”

“Two blankets are warmer.”

“Two blankets make a larger basket.”

“A larger basket carries more warmth.”

Ram Dass raised one eyebrow.

“Fine,” Mr. Carrisford said. “One blanket.”

They chose warm clothes, bread, fruit, milk, a lamp, and a book. Mr. Carrisford added a little box of sweets.

“Sweets are not necessary,” Ram Dass said.

“They are necessary for courage.”

“Food is necessary. Sweets are a happy argument.”

“Then take a small argument.”

Ram Dass placed the box in the basket.

They wrapped each object in cloth so it would not make noise. The bread went at the top. The lamp was covered. The blanket filled the empty spaces.

“What if she asks who sent it?” Mr. Carrisford asked.

“You will tell her the truth when the time is right.”

“And tonight?”

“Tonight, the truth is that someone saw a cold child and decided to help.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the basket. “It does not feel like enough.”

“Enough for one night is a beginning.”

The words reminded him of a small carrot. He did not know why.

### Sara’s Last Strength

Sara woke when Miss Minchin called her name from the hall. She stood too quickly and struck her knee on the bed.

“Bring my shawl,” Miss Minchin ordered.

Sara found it and carried it down. Then she was sent to close a window, fetch a book, and carry a message.

At the end of the tasks, she returned to the attic. Her head hurt. Her feet were sore. She held Emily and sat near the wall.

“I have done everything,” she whispered.

Becky came in. “Not everything. You have not eaten.”

“That was not on the list.”

“It should be.”

Becky searched again and found a dry crust. She placed it in Sara’s hand.

Sara wanted to give it to Melchisedec. Becky closed Sara’s fingers around it.

“This time, you eat first.”

Sara obeyed. She broke off a tiny piece for the rat afterward.

Melchisedec took it and disappeared.

“He will tell the council,” Sara said.

“Tell it what?”

“That the princess is still working.”

“Tell it she should stop.”

Sara leaned back. “The princess may rest when she has finished the next page.”

“There is no next page.”

“Then she must write one.”

Sara found her pencil. On a scrap of paper, she wrote: Tomorrow I will find one good thing.

She did not know what the thing would be. She did not know whether tomorrow would be kind. She wrote the sentence because it gave tomorrow a place to begin.

### The Plan Waits

Late that night, Ram Dass tied the rope to a strong post and lifted the basket. He stepped toward the roof door, but the wind pushed hard against it.

“The tiles are wet,” he said.

Mr. Carrisford looked through the window. “Can you cross safely?”

Ram Dass tested the first step. The tile moved under his foot.

“Not yet.”

He carried the basket back to the table. The bread was wrapped. The lamp was covered. The blanket filled the empty spaces. Everything was ready, but ready was not the same as safe.

Mr. Carrisford sat near the fire. “I want to go myself.”

“You can help by waiting,” Ram Dass said.

“Waiting feels like doing nothing.”

“It is doing something when the roof is dangerous.”

The monkey reached for the rope. Ram Dass moved it out of reach.

“You must wait too,” he told her.

The monkey folded her arms and looked offended.

Ram Dass marked the loose tile with chalk. He checked the latch, the rope, and the basket again. Then he placed the basket beside the door where it would be ready at the quietest hour.

In the attic, Sara slept beside the cold wall. Across the houses, three helpers waited: a sick man, a careful servant, and a monkey who believed every plan needed her opinion.

The roof was not ready. The kindness was.

### The Body’s Warning

The next morning, Sara woke with a sore throat. She tried to stand, but the floor tilted under her feet.

“I am fine,” she said.

Becky looked at her. “That sentence is not true.”

“It is a polite sentence.”

“The body does not care about politeness.”

Sara put on her coat. She had three errands and a room to clean.

“You should tell someone,” Becky said.

“Miss Minchin will say I am avoiding work.”

“Let her say it.”

Sara stopped at the door. “You sound like Ermengarde.”

“I have been taking lessons.”

Becky went to the kitchen and told the cook. The cook gave Sara warm water and sent a message to Miss Minchin.

Miss Minchin came to the attic. “You are needed downstairs.”

Sara stood straight, though her knees shook. “I am ill.”

“You look well enough.”

Becky stepped forward. “She almost fell.”

Miss Minchin looked at her. “Do not interrupt.”

Sara spoke before Becky could answer. “She is not interrupting. She is telling you what happened.”

Miss Minchin’s face tightened. “You will do the morning work. Then you may rest.”

Sara knew that this was not kindness. It was a smaller order than usual, but it was still an order.

She carried one message. Then she sat on the stair.

“The princess is resting,” Becky said.

“The princess is making trouble,” Miss Minchin answered.

Sara closed her eyes. “The princess is listening to her body.”

Miss Minchin left. Becky sat beside Sara.

“You must ask for help before you fall.”

“I am learning.”

“Learn faster.”

Sara drank the warm water. She did not feel strong, but she felt less alone.

### The Attic Inventory

While Sara rested, Becky made a list of everything in the attic. One bed. One blanket. Two cups. One candle. Three books. Four pictures. Five buttons. One doll. One rat family.

“You counted the rats?” Sara asked.

“I counted the sounds.”

“That is not the same.”

“It is close enough for a list.”

Sara added the compass and the paper star.

“What are we doing?” Becky asked.

“Learning what we have.”

“We have very little.”

“Very little is still a beginning.”

They made a second list of what they needed: warmer clothes, more food, oil for the lamp, and a better way to close the window.

“We cannot order these things,” Becky said.

“No. But we can know them.”

“Knowing does not make them appear.”

“It helps us recognize them when they come.”

Becky put the list beside the door. Melchisedec came out and pulled at the paper.

“Do not eat the needs,” Sara told him.

He let go.

“He understands,” Becky said.

“He understands paper.”

### The Room Next Door

Ram Dass stood in the house next door and heard a cough through the wall. He looked at the basket. He wanted to cross at once.

Mr. Carrisford held up a hand. “Wait until the school is quiet.”

“The child is ill.”

“We must not be seen.”

“If we wait too long, secrecy will matter less than her health.”

Mr. Carrisford nodded. “Prepare the warm water first.”

Ram Dass packed a small jar, a cup, and a cloth. He added bread and fruit.

“The basket is getting heavy,” he said.

“Take out the book.”

“The child likes books.”

“Then take out the lamp.”

“She needs light.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the basket. “Take out the fruit.”

“She needs food.”

“Then I will carry it.”

He lifted the basket. It was too heavy for him. Ram Dass took it back.

“You need to stand,” Ram Dass said.

“I know.”

“The rescue still needs a rescuer who can stand.”

Mr. Carrisford sat near the fire and watched the roof. The cough did not come again for several minutes.

### A Different Kind of Courage

Sara woke from a short sleep. She felt embarrassed that Becky had told the cook.

“I should have kept working,” she said.

“You were ill.”

“A princess should be strong.”

“A princess should know when she needs a blanket.”

“That is not a grand rule.”

“It is a useful one.”

Sara looked at the thin blanket. “I do not want anyone to think I am weak.”

Becky sat beside her. “Asking for help is not the same as giving up.”

“How do you know?”

“I asked you for help with reading.”

“You did.”

“I am still here.”

Sara thought about Ermengarde’s list and her own words to Lottie. A person could feel afraid and still act. A person could be tired and still be brave. Courage did not always look like a knight. Sometimes it looked like a girl drinking warm water instead of carrying a coal scuttle.

“Then I will rest for ten minutes,” Sara said.

“Twenty.”

“Fifteen.”

“Agreed.”

Becky watched the clock. Sara closed her eyes.

### The Basket at Midnight

When the school had grown quiet, Ram Dass tied the rope and crossed the roof. He carried the basket close to his chest.

The wind tugged at him. He stepped around the marked tile. The attic door opened without a squeak.

Sara and Becky were asleep. Sara’s hand rested near Emily. Becky had placed the inventory list on the table.

Ram Dass read it in the dim light. One bed. One blanket. Two cups. Three books. He looked at the list again.

“Not enough,” he whispered.

He placed the cup and warm water beside the bed. He added bread, fruit, the blanket, and the lamp. He left a small piece of cloth near the wall for the rats.

On the back of the inventory list, he wrote one word: Welcome.

He did not wake the girls. He crossed back over the roof and returned the key.

### What the Rat Saw

Melchisedec saw the door open. He saw the dark shape of a person. He smelled warmth and bread.

He could have made a sound. He did not.

He watched the stranger place the blanket on the bed. He watched the lamp appear. He watched the stranger bow to Emily.

Melchisedec decided that the visitor had good manners.

When the visitor left, the rat carried the little piece of cloth to the nest. The white-nosed rat followed him. The cloth was soft and warm.

The council had received its first royal gift.

### The Quiet Before Morning

Ram Dass stepped away from the attic door. Sara and Becky were still asleep. The room remained dark, but a careful visitor had left a change behind.

In the morning, Sara would discover it. For now, the roof was quiet, the basket was hidden in shadow, and the secret was safe with the night.

## Chapter 15 - The Magic

That evening, the wind pushed hard against the attic window. Sara and Becky sat close together.

“Let us pretend the room is warm,” Sara said.

“And full of food.”

“There is a fire in the grate.”

“A very big fire.”

“There are soft blankets.”

“And a chair that does not have a broken leg.”

They built the room in their minds. Sara described bright lamps, clean sheets, books, fruit, and hot chocolate. For a few minutes, the attic almost felt warm.

Then Sara fell asleep.

While she slept, Ram Dass crossed the roof. He opened the small door near the attic and carried in a basket. He placed a blanket on the bed, made a fire, and set food on the table. He brought a comfortable chair, warm clothes, and books.

He worked quietly. Melchisedec watched from his hole. The rat did not make a sound, which was the most helpful thing he had ever done.

In the morning, Sara woke and opened her eyes.

For a moment, she saw only the old attic. Then she felt warmth around her.

She sat up. A fire burned in the grate. A thick blanket covered the bed. A table stood near the wall with bread, fruit, and milk. Books waited on a clean shelf. The chair looked soft enough to swallow a tired person.

Sara touched the blanket.

“Becky,” she whispered. “Wake up.”

Becky opened one eye. Then she opened both.

“Where are we?”

Sara looked around the room. “Perhaps the princess has found the Magic.”

They ate. They warmed their hands. They laughed and cried at the same time.

Sara did not know who had helped them. Each morning, another kind thing appeared. The Magic brought food, clothes, blankets, and small surprises.

The gifts could not erase the hard months. But they told Sara that someone in the world had seen her suffering and had chosen to answer it.

### The First Morning

Sara stood beside the fire for a long time. Becky touched the blanket, then pulled her hand away.

“It may belong to someone,” Becky whispered.

“It belongs to the room now.”

“What if Miss Minchin comes?”

“We will tell her the Magic brought it.”

“She will not believe in Magic.”

“Then she will have to argue with the blanket.”

Becky picked up a warm coat. It was plain but strong. She held it against herself.

“This is too good.”

“A coat cannot be too good for a cold girl.”

Sara poured milk into two cups. She was afraid to drink too quickly. The food looked like a picture of breakfast.

“Perhaps we should ask permission,” Becky said.

“From whom?”

“The room.”

Sara looked around. “Room, may we eat?”

The fire crackled.

“It says yes,” Sara decided.

They ate bread, fruit, eggs, and a small cake. Becky tried to save half the cake.

“For later,” she said.

Sara placed it in a covered dish. “Later has been invited.”

Melchisedec came out. He sniffed the new food.

“You cannot have the whole cake,” Sara told him.

The rat looked offended.

“You may have a crumb. The kingdom must have rules.”

She gave him a tiny piece. He took it and carried it toward the wall.

“He approves,” Becky said.

“He is the palace mayor.”

Becky touched the soft chair. “I have never sat in one like this.”

“Sit.”

“It might break.”

“It looks stronger than the table.”

Becky sat. The chair did not break. It gave a small comfortable sound.

“It has spoken,” Sara said.

“What did it say?”

“Sit down.”

They took turns in the chair. Sara read the new book aloud. Becky listened while eating the last piece of cake. For one morning, the attic was not a punishment. It was a room where two friends could breathe.

### The Second Morning

The next morning, the fire was already lit. A new book sat beside the first one. There were oranges on the table and a small box of pencils.

“The Magic has learned our habits,” Becky said.

“It knows that books need friends.”

Sara opened the box. The pencils were different colors. She chose a green one and drew a leaf.

“What will you draw?” she asked.

“A kitchen.”

“Why a kitchen?”

“Because it has food.”

Becky drew a large oven. It had a smiling door. Sara drew a table. Becky added a soup pot.

“The pot is bigger than the room,” Sara said.

“It is a very important pot.”

They made a picture of their palace. The attic had a golden wall, a red carpet, and a window looking over trees. A bird carried a message. Melchisedec wore a crown.

“He will not wear it,” Becky said.

“He wore it on his birthday.”

“He stood beside it.”

“A mayor knows how to keep his dignity.”

Sara found a small mirror among the gifts. She held it up to Becky.

“You look different.”

“I have washed.”

“That is not what I mean.”

Becky looked at her reflection. Warm clothes and a full meal had brought color to her face.

“I look like a person in a picture,” she said.

“You are a person outside the picture too.”

Becky put down the mirror. “The Magic sees us.”

Sara thought about that. She did not know who the Magic was, but she felt grateful to be seen.

### The Third Morning

On the third morning, the gifts changed. There was a small table for Becky’s room, a tin of tea, and a pair of thick socks.

“The Magic knows about your feet,” Sara said.

“The Magic has excellent eyes.”

There was also a little basket for Melchisedec. It held dry cloth and a piece of cheese.

“He has his own basket,” Becky said.

Melchisedec climbed inside, turned around, and came out again.

“He does not like it.”

“Perhaps he wants a larger basket.”

“He is a rat, not a horse.”

Sara moved the basket near the wall. Later, the dry cloth disappeared. Melchisedec had taken it to the nest.

“He has accepted the gift,” Sara said.

“He has stolen it.”

“The royal word is accepted.”

Sara noticed a folded paper beneath the cheese. It had no name. On it was a simple drawing of a sun.

“Who made this?” Becky asked.

“The Magic.”

“Does the Magic draw?”

“Perhaps it has a pencil.”

The drawing made Sara think of India. She remembered bright mornings, warm walls, and the smell of flowers after rain.

She placed the paper beside Emily.

### Asking the Magic

Sara wanted to thank the person who had helped them. She did not know how to speak to a hidden visitor, so she wrote a note.

Thank you for the fire.

Thank you for the food.

Thank you for seeing Becky.

Thank you for seeing me.

She placed the note near the lamp. The next morning, it was gone. In its place was a small red ribbon.

“The Magic answered,” Becky said.

“It may have taken the note.”

“That is an answer.”

Sara tied the ribbon around Emily’s dress. It was a little long, so she made a bow.

“Emily looks pleased.”

“She always looks serious.”

“Serious pleasure.”

Becky found a new note under the chair. It contained one word: Hope.

“Can you read it?” Sara asked.

Becky sounded it out. “Hope.”

Sara nodded. “A small word.”

“A big meaning.”

They placed the note on the wall. It joined the pictures of the fire, the garden, the bird, and the palace.

### Miss Minchin Notices

Miss Minchin soon noticed that the attic was no longer empty. She saw the light under the door. She smelled toast in the hall.

“Where did you get food?” she demanded.

Sara stood beside the table. “It appeared.”

“Nothing appears.”

“The food did.”

Miss Minchin pushed open the door. She saw the blanket, the chair, the books, and the fire.

“Who has been here?”

“We were sleeping.”

“Someone entered this room.”

“The door was closed.”

“Then someone has a key.”

Sara looked at Emily. “Perhaps the doll has a key.”

Miss Minchin did not find this funny.

“You will tell me where these things came from.”

“I do not know.”

“You expect me to believe that?”

“It is true.”

Miss Minchin picked up one of the books. It had no name inside. She looked at the fire and the food. She could not take everything without admitting that someone outside the school cared about Sara.

“Do not become proud,” she said.

Sara touched the blanket. “Warmth does not make a person proud.”

Miss Minchin left. Becky waited until her footsteps faded.

“I thought she would take it.”

“She may try again.”

“What will we do?”

Sara looked at the door. “We will remember that the Magic came quietly. We must also be quiet.”

### The Secret Marks

Sara began to look for clues. The chair had a small scratch on one leg. The blanket smelled faintly of cedar. The bread was from a bakery she did not know.

Becky searched the window.

“There are footprints on the roof.”

Sara leaned close. The prints were small and partly covered by snow.

“A bird?”

“A person.”

“A very large bird.”

“The Magic may have boots.”

They found a thread caught on the attic door. It was dark red, like the cloth worn by the Indian servant next door.

Sara held it. “I have seen this color before.”

“From India?”

“Perhaps.”

She did not guess the truth. She only knew that the Magic might be a person who had come from the house beside them.

That night, she put a crumb near the door. In the morning, the crumb was gone. The fire had been made. The bread was fresh.

“Melchisedec may know,” Becky said.

“He is a good secret keeper.”

“He eats secrets.”

“Only paper ones.”

Sara watched the rat. He had heard someone enter. He had seen the basket. But Melchisedec gave no report.

### The Magic Becomes a Habit

The gifts continued. A warm shawl appeared. A little rug covered the worst part of the floor. A new cup sat near the fire. There were apples, soup, bread, and books.

Sara did not stop being careful. She shared everything with Becky and saved food for the rats. She also saved one apple for a bird in the garden, though the bird did not come when she called.

“The bird is late,” Sara said.

“Perhaps it is wearing its best feathers.”

“That takes time.”

One morning, a small packet held a comb and a blue ribbon. Sara gave the ribbon to Becky.

“You should wear it.”

“It is too pretty.”

“Pretty things do not belong only to rich rooms.”

Becky tied it around her hair. She looked in the mirror and stood straight.

“I look like a kitchen princess.”

“The highest kind.”

“Do kitchen princesses carry coal?”

“Only when the coal is behaving badly.”

Becky laughed. The sound was no longer strange. It belonged in the room.

Sara understood that the Magic was not just a pile of objects. It was a message. Someone was saying, You matter. You are worth warmth. You are worth time.

She wanted to know who had sent the message. She also wanted to protect it. If the Magic was a secret, she would keep it until the secret was ready to open.

### The Magic List

Sara began a list of the gifts. She wrote them in her notebook so she would not forget.

One fire.

Two blankets.

Bread, fruit, milk, and cake.

Books.

Pencils.

A chair.

Warm clothes.

A red ribbon.

“You forgot the lamp,” Becky said.

Sara added it.

“You forgot the rug.”

Sara added that too.

“You forgot the food for Melchisedec.”

“The rat is not a gift.”

“He believes he is.”

Sara wrote: cheese for the council.

The list grew longer. Sara did not want the gifts to become ordinary. She wanted to remember how the first warm morning had felt.

### Gifts with Names

One morning, the Magic brought three small things. There was a ribbon for Lottie, a purple pencil for Ermengarde, and a clean apron for Becky.

“These are not for me,” Sara said.

Becky touched the apron. “How do you know?”

“The Magic knows who needs what.”

“Perhaps you asked for them.”

“I did not.”

Sara carried the gifts to her friends. Lottie tied the ribbon around her memory box. Ermengarde wrote a date with the purple pencil. Becky put on the apron and looked at herself in the mirror.

“It has no tear,” Becky said.

“It may become lonely.”

“I will give it work.”

Miss Minchin saw the gifts and became suspicious.

“Where did these come from?”

Lottie pointed at Sara. “The Magic.”

Miss Minchin looked at Sara.

Sara said, “That is what we call it.”

“You are encouraging foolishness.”

Ermengarde held up the purple pencil. “It writes very sensibly.”

Miss Minchin sent the girls away. But she did not take the gifts. Perhaps she feared that the Magic might leave a complaint in her own room.

### A Feast in a Small Room

The food grew better for a few days. Sara and Becky set the table carefully. They placed the cups in a row and cut the cake into equal pieces.

“The princess should take the largest piece,” Becky said.

“The princess should take the piece she needs.”

“What if she needs the largest?”

“Then she may explain why.”

Sara chose a middle piece. Becky chose another. They saved one for Lottie, although she was not there.

Melchisedec appeared. He looked at the cake.

“You may have a crumb,” Sara told him.

The rat climbed toward the table.

“Not the whole piece.”

He stopped and sat down.

“He is waiting politely,” Becky said.

“The Magic has improved his manners.”

“Or the cake is too large to carry.”

Sara placed a crumb near the wall. Melchisedec took it. The girls ate their cake.

The room was still small. It now held enough warmth for two girls, one doll, a rat family, and many stories.

### The Gift That Was Not an Object

One morning, Sara found nothing new on the table. There was no book, blanket, food, or ribbon.

“The Magic has stopped,” Becky said.

Sara looked around. The fire was burning. The room was warm. The chair was comfortable. The gifts from earlier days were still there.

“Perhaps the Magic has brought us a quiet morning.”

“Can we eat a quiet morning?”

“No.”

“Then it is less useful than bread.”

Sara laughed. They sat by the fire and read. Nobody hurried them. No one shouted from the hall.

Becky finished a page without help. Sara read a story about a girl who found a garden behind a wall.

“This is a good book,” Becky said.

“Why?”

“The girl finds the door herself.”

Sara closed the book. “Yes. That is important.”

The quiet morning was a gift too. It gave the girls time to notice how far they had come.

### A Secret Thank-You

Sara wanted to leave a thank-you where the visitor would find it. She and Becky made a small package. It held a drawing of the attic, a paper star, and a note.

The drawing showed the room with a fire, a chair, a table, books, Emily, Becky, Sara, and a rat wearing a crown.

“Why is the rat larger than the chair?” Becky asked.

“He is important.”

“He is still a rat.”

“Important rats can be large.”

Sara placed the package near the attic door. In the morning, it was gone. In its place lay a small silver bell.

“The Magic has a bell,” Becky said.

“Perhaps it wants us to call.”

Sara rang it once. The sound was soft. No one came.

She rang it again. From the neighboring house came a faint monkey call.

Sara and Becky looked at each other.

“The bell has an answer,” Becky whispered.

Sara walked to the window. Across the roof, the dark red thread she had seen before moved near the door.

“Someone is there,” she said.

“Will you call?”

Sara almost did. Then she remembered that the gifts had come quietly. She did not want to turn kindness into a demand.

“Not yet,” she answered. “When the visitor is ready, we will meet.”

### The Magic’s Promise

The gifts continued after that. A warm scarf appeared. A small clock appeared. A box of oranges appeared. One day, a tiny flowerpot appeared with a green shoot inside.

Sara carried it to Lottie. Lottie placed it beside the bean seed in the memory garden.

“They are sisters,” Lottie said.

“They may be cousins.”

“They are both trees.”

“Then they have a family.”

Lottie watered them carefully. This time, she did not make a river.

“I am learning,” she said.

Sara looked at the green leaves. The Magic had brought comfort, but it had also brought chances for the girls to give comfort to one another.

Sara still did not know who had crossed the roof. She did know one thing. A person who sent warmth to a child in secret was thinking about more than money.

She held Emily close.

“The secret can wait one more day,” Sara said.

Sara placed the red ribbon on the table after Emily fell asleep. It was only a ribbon, but it made her think about the person who had sent it. A kind stranger could be near and still remain unknown.

“Do you think the visitor is lonely?” Sara whispered.

Becky looked at the dark window. “Perhaps. People who help in secret may need a friend too.”

Sara folded the ribbon beside the compass. “Then I hope the visitor finds one.”

Outside the attic window, the monkey jumped onto the roof. The next visit would not be planned by adults.

The monkey carried a red ribbon in its teeth. It dropped the ribbon on the sill, bowed, and waited.

Sara picked it up. “Is this for Emily?”

Emily sat up. “A present?”

Sara tied the ribbon around the paper crown. It slipped sideways, so Emily wore it at an angle.

“Now you look like a queen who has just escaped a very small storm,” Sara said.

Emily lifted her chin. “I order the storm to go away.”

The monkey clapped its hands.

Sara laughed. The gift was small, and no one had explained it. Yet the ribbon had crossed the roof and found the right head. Sometimes a secret kindness did not need a name before it could make a child smile.

## Chapter 16 - The Visitor

The monkey next door was not pleased by quiet plans. He liked open doors, shiny objects, and any place where he was not allowed to go.

One night, he escaped from his room. He ran along the roof and climbed through the attic window.

Sara woke to a noise near her books.

“Who is there?”

The monkey pulled a book from the shelf. It fell with a loud thump.

Sara sat up. In the moonlight, she saw the small animal sitting on her chair.

“You are the Indian Gentleman’s monkey!” she said.

The monkey answered by putting the book on his head.

Sara laughed. “That is not how people read.”

The monkey jumped to the bed and tried to hide under the blanket. Sara caught him gently before he could tear it.

Becky came in. “What will we do?”

“We must return him. He may be frightened.”

Sara wrapped the monkey in a shawl. He held one of her books and refused to let it go.

“You may keep the book until we reach home,” Sara told him. “Then you must give it back.”

She carried him across the roof and down to Mr. Carrisford’s house. A servant opened the door. Ram Dass hurried forward.

“The monkey has been in the attic,” Sara explained. “He came through the window.”

Ram Dass looked at her old dress and thin face. Mr. Carrisford came into the room.

“Please come inside,” he said. “You must be cold.”

Sara stepped into the warm hall. It looked strange and beautiful. She did not know why the rooms felt familiar.

The monkey climbed onto Ram Dass’s shoulder. Sara returned the book.

“Thank you,” Mr. Carrisford said. “What is your name?”

“Sara.”

He studied her face. Something about her eyes made his heart beat faster.

The escaped monkey had brought Sara to the house next door. Neither Sara nor Mr. Carrisford yet understood the meaning of the meeting.

### The Monkey’s Apology

The monkey sat on Ram Dass’s shoulder and held Sara’s book against his chest. He had no plan to give it back.

“He is sorry,” Ram Dass said.

Sara looked at the monkey. “He does not look sorry.”

The monkey hid his face in Ram Dass’s coat.

“He is pretending.”

“That may be his kind of sorry.”

Mr. Carrisford smiled. “What is the book about?”

“A garden.”

“Does the monkey like gardens?”

The monkey pulled a leaf from a vase.

“He likes gardens very much,” Sara said.

The monkey began to eat the leaf.

“He likes gardens in a practical way.”

Ram Dass took the leaf away. The monkey made an unhappy sound.

“You may have one leaf,” Sara told him. “But not the whole vase.”

The monkey looked at Mr. Carrisford.

“Do not ask me,” Mr. Carrisford said. “She is right.”

The monkey sighed. He gave the book to Ram Dass. Ram Dass returned it to Sara.

“Thank you,” Sara said.

The monkey reached for it again.

“You have already said goodbye,” Sara told him.

He put both hands over his eyes.

Sara laughed. Mr. Carrisford watched her carefully. She laughed like a child who had forgotten for one minute that the world could be hard.

### Tea in the Warm Room

Mr. Carrisford asked Sara to stay for tea. Sara hesitated near the door.

“I should return to the school.”

“You may return after tea.”

“Miss Minchin may be looking for me.”

“Then she will find you here.”

Sara looked down at her old shoes. “I do not want Becky to be punished.”

“Who is Becky?”

“She works at the school. She is my friend.”

“Does she know where you are?”

“Not yet.”

Mr. Carrisford told Ram Dass to send a message that Sara was safe. Ram Dass went to the school door. He returned with news that the school knew Sara had taken the monkey home.

“Becky will worry,” Sara said.

“We will send word to her too.”

A servant brought tea, bread, fruit, and warm milk. Sara sat at the table. The cup felt fine in her hands.

“You may eat,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“I am not certain whether the food belongs to me.”

“It belongs to the table until someone eats it.”

Sara took a piece of bread. She ate slowly. The monkey watched her and then reached for the bread.

“You have your own plate,” Sara told him.

The monkey looked at his plate. It held a banana.

He turned his back and reached for Sara’s bread.

“He believes your bread is better.”

“It may be.”

Ram Dass moved the banana closer to the monkey. The monkey took it and peeled it with one hand.

“He is very clever,” Sara said.

“He is clever when he is not being wicked,” Ram Dass answered.

“That sounds like many people.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at her with interest. “You speak as if you know many people.”

“I know some people. I watch others.”

“What have you learned?”

Sara held her cup. “People can look rich and still be lonely. People can look poor and still be kind. A monkey can look innocent while holding a book.”

Ram Dass laughed. Mr. Carrisford smiled.

### Questions about India

Mr. Carrisford asked Sara about India. He did not ask directly about her father yet.

“What did you like there?”

“The colors. The heat. The gardens. The sound of the streets.”

“Did you have a favorite place?”

“A garden near our house. There was a tree with a low branch. I could sit there and read.”

“What did you read?”

“Stories about girls who traveled, ships that crossed storms, and people who found hidden rooms.”

“Did you have friends?”

“My father was my best friend.”

Mr. Carrisford’s face changed when she said this.

“He was kind?”

“Very kind. He did not laugh when I asked odd questions.”

“What odd questions?”

“Whether a diamond can be lonely. Whether a map knows when it is wrong. Whether a goat can understand an invitation.”

“Can a goat?”

“No. But it can refuse one.”

Mr. Carrisford turned toward the fire. Sara noticed that his hand shook.

“Do you know my father?” she asked.

He looked at her. “I knew a Captain Crewe in India.”

Sara sat very still.

“Was he your friend?”

“Yes.”

“What was he like?”

Mr. Carrisford told her that Captain Crewe loved his daughter, trusted his friends, and laughed easily. He did not mention the last letters or the illness.

Sara listened. She had heard these things before, but it was comforting to hear them from a new voice.

“You knew the same Captain Crewe,” she said.

“I believe so.”

“Then he chose good friends.”

Mr. Carrisford lowered his eyes. “I hope he did.”

### A Walk to the Window

Mr. Carrisford could not walk far, but he showed Sara the room that faced the school. It had tall windows and a desk covered with papers.

“These papers are about the diamond mines,” he said.

Sara looked at the maps. The lines reminded her of her father’s map.

“Are they successful?”

Mr. Carrisford hesitated. “They are now.”

“Now?”

“The business changed after your father died.”

Sara did not understand. “Did my father lose money?”

“He believed he had.”

“That sounds sad.”

“It was.”

Sara studied the maps. “But if the mines are successful now, someone must know.”

“Someone does.”

“Does that person know about Captain Crewe’s daughter?”

Mr. Carrisford looked at Ram Dass. Ram Dass looked at the window.

“We are trying to learn,” Mr. Carrisford said.

Sara noticed that he had not answered. She did not press him. She knew that grown people sometimes carried questions they were not ready to open.

At the window, she saw the attic. The roof was only a few steps away. Her room looked small from the warm house.

“I live there,” she said.

Mr. Carrisford followed her gaze. “In the attic?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“It is my room.”

“It is not a room for a child.”

Sara looked at him. “I am not allowed to choose.”

Mr. Carrisford’s face became angry, but he kept his voice calm. “Do they treat you well?”

Sara thought about the question.

“Some people do. Becky does. Ermengarde does. Lottie does when she is not throwing pillows.”

“And Miss Minchin?”

Sara did not answer at once. “Miss Minchin gives orders.”

“That was not my question.”

“She gives many orders.”

Mr. Carrisford understood. He did not ask more.

### A Message for Becky

Ram Dass returned from the school. He had spoken to the cook and told her Sara was safe. Becky had been looking for Sara.

“Can I send a message?” Sara asked.

Ram Dass gave her paper.

Sara wrote: I returned the monkey. I am safe. Please do not worry. The house is warm, and the monkey is a thief.

She gave the paper to Ram Dass.

“Will Becky receive it?”

“I will make certain.”

“Thank you.”

“You are welcome.”

Sara looked at him carefully. “You remind me of India.”

Ram Dass smiled. “And you remind me of a child who asks too many questions.”

“That is because I am one.”

“It is a good kind of child.”

The message reached Becky. She read it in the kitchen and pressed it to her apron. She was still worried, but she knew Sara had eaten.

### A Gift from the House

When Sara prepared to leave, Mr. Carrisford asked whether she needed anything.

“No, thank you.”

“You came in the cold.”

“I have a coat.”

“It is a thin coat.”

Sara looked toward the door. She did not want to accept gifts from a stranger.

Mr. Carrisford called for a warm shawl. He held it out, but he did not force it into her hands.

“You may borrow this,” he said. “Return it if you do not want to keep it.”

Sara touched the cloth. “Borrowing is easier.”

“Then borrow it.”

She wrapped it around her shoulders. It smelled like cedar and soap.

“It is warm.”

“Good.”

The monkey reached for the shawl. Sara moved away.

“You may not borrow this,” she told him.

The monkey sat down with a sad face.

“He has a very strong acting skill,” Sara said.

Ram Dass opened the door. The hall seemed less strange now. Sara knew where the fire was and where the monkey liked to hide.

“May I visit again?” she asked.

Mr. Carrisford was surprised. “You may.”

“I would like to see the garden.”

“You will.”

“And the map?”

“We can look at it.”

“And perhaps the monkey can learn not to steal books.”

“That may be the hardest lesson.”

Sara returned to the school with the shawl around her shoulders. She did not know that Mr. Carrisford watched from the window until she disappeared through the door.

He turned to Ram Dass.

“She has Captain Crewe’s eyes,” he said.

“And his questions.”

“Is she the child?”

“We must find out.”

The answer was close. Sara carried the borrowed warmth back across the wall, still unaware that her life was about to open.

### The Second Visit

Sara visited the house again two days later. This time, she brought Becky. The monkey saw them through the window and began to jump.

“She remembers you,” Ram Dass said.

“She remembers the book,” Sara answered.

Sita ran to a shelf and pulled down three books. She placed them on the floor and sat on the largest one.

“She is choosing a chair,” Becky said.

“That is not a chair.”

The monkey looked at Becky and patted the book.

“She disagrees,” Sara said.

Ram Dass brought another chair. Sita moved the book onto it and climbed on top.

“Now she has both.”

Becky laughed. Mr. Carrisford came into the room.

“I am glad you returned,” he said.

“I wanted Becky to see the monkey.”

“The monkey is pleased to see Becky.”

Sita reached for Becky’s blue ribbon.

“She has poor manners,” Becky said.

“She is practicing,” Sara replied.

Becky removed the ribbon before the monkey could take it. Sita looked surprised.

“You must ask,” Becky told her.

Sita made a soft sound.

“She says she forgot.”

“She says no such thing.”

“You do not speak monkey.”

“Neither do you.”

“I am learning.”

### A Book about Gardens

Mr. Carrisford asked Sara to choose a book from the shelf. She selected one about gardens. Becky chose a book with pictures of animals.

“This book has a tiger,” Becky said.

“Does it have a monkey?”

“A small picture.”

Sita leaned over and tried to turn the pages.

“She wants the tiger,” Sara said.

Ram Dass held the book. “The tiger is not for eating.”

Sita looked disappointed.

Sara read a short part of the garden book. It described a tree that grew beside a wall. The tree had roots on one side and branches on the other.

“A tree can cross a wall without walking,” Sara said.

Mr. Carrisford looked at her. “Do you like walls?”

“Some are useful. Some are only in the way.”

“Which kind is the wall between the houses?”

Sara thought. “It keeps rain away from the roofs. It also keeps people from meeting.”

“Perhaps a wall can do both.”

“Then it needs a door.”

Mr. Carrisford was quiet.

Sara noticed that he looked tired. “Are you ill?”

“I am getting better.”

“That is good.”

“You speak as if you know what it is like to be ill.”

“I know what it is like to be tired and told to work.”

Becky glanced at her. Sara had not told Mr. Carrisford about every hard day. She did not want to make the warm room heavy.

### The Drawing on the Table

Sara found paper and drew the garden from the book. She added the wall and a door. Becky drew a path from the attic to the garden.

Mr. Carrisford watched them.

“You draw a door in every house,” he said.

“Doors make houses kinder.”

“Do you think the school has a door for you?”

Sara looked at the drawing. “It has many doors. They do not all open.”

“What would you put behind a door that opened?”

“A warm room. A table. Books. A place for Becky to sit.”

“And you?”

“I would sit too.”

Mr. Carrisford felt his throat tighten. He wanted to tell her that such a room already waited. He was not yet ready to say why.

Sita took the pencil. She drew a line across the paper.

“The monkey has added a road,” Sara said.

“She has added a scratch,” Becky replied.

“A scratch is a very short road.”

Sita drew another line, then dropped the pencil.

“She has finished.”

### A Question about the Doll

Mr. Carrisford looked at Emily. “Does your doll have a name?”

“Emily.”

“How long have you had her?”

“Since I came to London.”

“Did your father give her to you?”

“Yes. He said she would be my friend.”

“Was she always dressed in blue?”

“At first, her dress was new. Now it is old.”

Sara touched the worn cloth. “She has lived through many days.”

“Does she speak?”

“Only when people listen properly.”

Mr. Carrisford smiled. “What does she say now?”

Sara looked at Emily. “She says the monkey should not sit on books.”

Sita jumped off the chair.

“Emily has great power,” Becky said.

“She has good timing.”

Mr. Carrisford studied the doll’s face. Captain Crewe had written about Emily. The name was another piece of proof.

“Did your father call you a little princess?” he asked.

Sara’s eyes became bright. “Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because he wanted me to remember that I could be kind. He did not mean that I was better than other people.”

Mr. Carrisford looked down. This was exactly how Captain Crewe had described her.

### Ram Dass Brings the Shawl

Ram Dass brought back the shawl Sara had borrowed. He had cleaned it and repaired a small loose thread.

“You may keep it,” he said.

Sara shook her head. “It belongs here.”

“There are other shawls.”

“Then I can borrow it again.”

Ram Dass smiled. “You may borrow it as long as you need.”

Becky touched the cloth. “It is warmer than my coat.”

“You may borrow one too,” Mr. Carrisford said.

Becky looked startled. “For me?”

“For you.”

She held the shawl around her shoulders. “I do not know what to say.”

“You may say thank you.”

“Thank you.”

Sara looked at Mr. Carrisford. “You are kind to people you do not know.”

Mr. Carrisford answered carefully. “Sometimes a person is not a stranger for long.”

Sara wondered what he meant. Before she could ask, Sita stole the end of the shawl and ran.

Becky chased her around the chair. Ram Dass caught the monkey and returned the cloth.

“She is practicing running,” Sara said.

“She has already passed the test,” Ram Dass replied.

The room filled with laughter.

### The Questions Continue

When the laughter ended, Mr. Carrisford asked Sara about her father’s home in India. She described a house with wide windows, a garden, and a tree where birds gathered.

“Was there a river?”

“Not near the house. There was a well.”

“Did Captain Crewe have a friend named Carrisford?”

Sara looked at him.

“He spoke of an old friend. I do not remember the name.”

Mr. Carrisford’s hand tightened on the chair.

“Did he ever write about the diamond mines?”

“Yes. He showed me a map.”

“Did the map have a red mark?”

“Yes.”

“Did he carry a brass compass?”

Sara reached into her coat. “He gave me one.”

She placed it on the table. The needle moved toward north.

Mr. Carrisford stared at it. He remembered buying the compass with Captain Crewe in a market. He had chosen it because it was strong.

“He gave you this?”

“Before he left.”

Mr. Carrisford knew now. The girl was Captain Crewe’s daughter. He knew it in his heart, but he still needed to tell her gently.

### A Visit That Ends

Sara saw that Mr. Carrisford had become tired. She stood.

“We should go.”

“You may stay.”

“You need rest.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at her with surprise. She had noticed his need before he spoke.

“Will you come again?” he asked.

“Yes, if Miss Minchin permits it.”

“I will speak with her.”

“Please do not make trouble for Becky.”

“I will not.”

Sara returned the garden book to the shelf. Sita tried to hide the animal book under a cushion.

“She is not ready to return it,” Sara said.

“She may keep it until tomorrow,” Ram Dass answered.

Sita hugged the book.

“She has a very strong friendship with it.”

Sara and Becky left the warm house. Ram Dass watched them cross the roof path with care. Mr. Carrisford stood by the window.

“It is her,” he said.

“Yes,” Ram Dass answered.

“I must tell her.”

“Tell her when she is ready to hear.”

Sara returned to the school with the borrowed shawl. She did not know that the man next door had found the child he had promised to find.

## Chapter 17 - “It Is the Child!”

Mr. Carrisford asked Sara to sit near the fire. He wanted to know about the attic and the strange gifts that had appeared there.

“Who lives at the school?” he asked.

“Miss Minchin, the teachers, and the girls.”

“And your father?”

Sara looked down. “My father is dead.”

Mr. Carrisford became very still.

“What was his name?”

“Captain Crewe.”

The room seemed to change. Mr. Carrisford stared at her.

“Your full name?”

“Sara Crewe.”

Ram Dass looked at his master. Mr. Carrisford rose too quickly and had to hold the table.

“It is the child!” he cried. “It is Captain Crewe’s daughter!”

Sara watched him with surprise. “You knew my father?”

“I was his friend and business partner. I have searched for you for a long time.”

He told Sara about the diamond mines. At first, everyone had believed that the investment was lost. Later, the mines had become successful. The fortune now belonged to Sara.

Sara listened. She understood that she was rich again, but the news did not seem real.

“Does this mean I can have my books?” she asked.

Mr. Carrisford smiled through tears. “You can have every book you wish.”

“And Becky?”

“She must be safe too.”

Sara looked at the fire. “I do not want to leave her alone.”

Mr. Carrisford promised that Becky would come with her. He promised a warm home, good food, lessons, and people who would care for her.

Sara began to cry. These were not only tears of happiness. They were tears for her father, for the lonely months, and for the little girl she had been when everything changed.

Money had returned to her, but money was not the greatest gift. Sara had found a home. She had found people who believed her and wanted her near.

For the first time since her father’s death, she felt that she could rest.

### The Proof in the Box

Mr. Carrisford went to a locked cabinet and brought out a wooden box. His hands shook as he opened it.

Inside were letters, a small photograph, and a silver paper knife. Sara saw her father’s name on one envelope.

“May I see it?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She touched the handwriting. It was the same handwriting that had appeared on her letters from India.

“Papa wrote this.”

“He wrote it to me.”

Sara read the first line. It mentioned a journey, a business plan, and a little girl who had asked whether diamonds needed a home.

“I said that,” Sara whispered.

Mr. Carrisford nodded. “You were very small.”

“I asked many questions.”

“Your father told me about all of them.”

He showed her the photograph. It was old and a little pale. Captain Crewe stood beside Mr. Carrisford in India. Both men wore travel clothes. Behind them was a tent and a tree.

Sara took the photograph in both hands.

“You were younger.”

“Much younger.”

“And Papa’s coat was dusty.”

“It had met a very dusty road.”

“Did the goat live near the road?”

Mr. Carrisford stared. “You know about the goat?”

“Papa told me.”

The two men looked at each other in the photograph. Sara felt as if her father had left a small window open in the past.

### The Story of the Diamonds

Mr. Carrisford explained the diamond business. He used simple words and stopped when Sara looked worried.

“Your father and I put money into the mines together. At first, the workers found nothing. The reports were bad.”

“And Papa became ill?”

“Yes. I was ill too. I believed the business had failed, and I feared your father had died thinking the money was gone.”

“But the mines became successful later.”

“Yes. The stones were there. We simply had not reached them.”

Sara looked at the map. “So the diamonds were not lost. They were waiting.”

“That is one way to say it.”

“Papa did not know.”

“No.”

“You did not know where I was.”

“No.”

“And I was next door.”

“Yes.”

Sara thought about the wall. It had stood between them while she carried coal and he searched for her.

“The wall has been very unhelpful,” she said.

Ram Dass nodded. “It has kept too many secrets.”

Mr. Carrisford promised that every part of the fortune would be placed in Sara’s name. He would not make decisions without explaining them. He would not treat her as a child who could be moved from room to room without a voice.

“I do not know much about money,” Sara said.

“You will learn.”

“I know about bread.”

“That may be more important.”

### A Home for Becky

Sara’s first question after the fortune was settled was about Becky.

“Can Becky come here tonight?”

Mr. Carrisford looked toward Ram Dass. “If the school permits it.”

“If it does not?”

“Then I will speak with Miss Minchin.”

Sara shook her head. “Please do not make Becky wait because adults are arguing.”

Ram Dass sent a message to the school. Becky came to the house with a small bundle of clothes. She stood in the hall and looked at the carpet.

“Do not step on the flowers,” she whispered.

“They are painted,” Sara said.

“They may still be important.”

Sara took her hand. “You have a room here.”

Becky looked at Mr. Carrisford. “A room for me?”

“Yes.”

“Do I have to clean it?”

“You may, if you wish. You do not have to earn the bed.”

Becky’s mouth opened. No sound came out.

Sara led her upstairs. The room had a bed, a window, a table, and a small shelf. A blue cover lay across the bed.

Becky touched the cover. “It is not cold.”

“That is the usual plan for a bed.”

“The attic had other plans.”

The room also held a little box for buttons. Becky put her old wooden box inside it.

“We can make a palace rule,” Sara said. “No servant girl sleeps in a room where she cannot stand straight.”

“What about a kitchen princess?”

“She gets the best chair.”

Becky sat in the chair. It did not break.

### Returning to the School

Sara did not want to leave Ermengarde and Lottie without saying goodbye. The next day, she returned to the school with Becky and Ram Dass.

Miss Minchin met them in the hall. Her face was full of surprise and worry.

“Sara, I was about to send for you.”

“Why?”

“There are matters to discuss.”

“I have come to see my friends.”

Miss Minchin looked at Becky. “The servant has no reason to enter.”

“Becky is my friend too.”

Mr. Carrisford stepped forward. “She will enter with Sara.”

Miss Minchin recognized him. Her voice changed.

“Mr. Carrisford.”

“Yes.”

“I did not know you were connected with Captain Crewe.”

“We were partners.”

Miss Minchin’s eyes moved to Sara. “I have cared for the child since she was seven.”

Sara remembered the first warm room, the private clothes, and the way Miss Minchin had smiled at her father. She also remembered the attic.

“You gave me a place when Papa paid for it,” Sara said. “Later, you gave me work when you thought no one would pay.”

Miss Minchin looked away.

Mr. Carrisford said, “Sara will not be returning as a pupil.”

“But the school has an important reputation.”

“Her home is with me.”

Sara touched his sleeve. “I want to see Ermengarde and Lottie.”

“Of course.”

Miss Minchin moved aside. She did not stop them.

### Lottie’s Question

Lottie ran to Sara and held her around the waist.

“You came back!”

“I promised I would.”

“Are you a princess?”

Sara looked at the old schoolroom. “I am Sara.”

“But you have a house.”

“A house does not answer the question.”

Lottie frowned. “Will you take me with you?”

Sara knelt. “I will visit. You will stay here for now because your lessons and your home are here.”

“Is Becky coming?”

“Becky has a room at my house.”

“Can I see it?”

“Yes.”

Lottie looked at Miss Minchin and lowered her voice. “Can I bring Emily?”

Sara held up the doll. “Emily is coming with me, but she may visit too.”

“She will miss me.”

“She has a very large heart.”

Lottie touched the doll’s hand. “Do not forget the memory garden.”

“I will not.”

Sara gave Lottie a small wooden box like the one she had made before. It was not the original box, but it held a ribbon, a feather, and a smooth stone.

“Add memories when you want,” Sara said.

Lottie hugged the box. “I will put a cake in it.”

“A paper cake.”

“A real one would be better.”

“That is true.”

### Ermengarde’s Good News

Ermengarde waited near the bookshelves. She had her purple notebook under one arm.

“I heard everything,” she said.

“Then you know the diamonds were waiting.”

“I know you are rich again.”

“Yes.”

“Are you happy?”

Sara thought. “I am happy about the house. I am sad about the time I lost. I am glad Becky is safe. I miss my father.”

Ermengarde opened her notebook. “I wrote a list.”

“Another list?”

“This one is about your new life.”

The list said:

- Warm room.
- Books.
- Food.
- Becky.
- Emily.
- Friends.
- Papa still remembered.

Sara touched the last line. “That is the most important one.”

Ermengarde added another line: Sara is still Sara.

“You have used that lesson before,” Sara said.

“It remains useful.”

Ermengarde’s father had told her that she could visit Sara. She was no longer afraid that Sara would forget her because rich people had better friends.

“Will you still help me study?” Ermengarde asked.

“Yes.”

“Even if you have a whole library?”

“Especially then. Libraries need people who remember the doors.”

Ermengarde smiled.

### The Compass Points Home

Before Sara left, she found her old compass in the attic room. She had thought it was lost. A maid had kept it with the few things that belonged to her.

Sara held it in her palm. The needle turned north.

“Papa gave me this,” she told Mr. Carrisford.

“I remember.”

“He said it could help me find home.”

Mr. Carrisford looked at the school and then at his house. “Perhaps it has.”

Sara did not think a compass had found the house. Kind people had done that. Ram Dass had watched. Becky had waited. Ermengarde had remembered. A monkey had escaped.

Still, Sara liked the idea that the little needle had kept pointing while she was lost.

She placed the compass beside Emily in the carriage. Becky sat with her. Mr. Carrisford followed in another carriage.

“Will there be rules in the new house?” Becky asked.

“Some.”

“Will there be bells?”

“Perhaps one.”

“Will it shout?”

“Only if someone drops a spoon.”

Sara looked out at the street. The school became smaller behind them. The attic window was only a dark square now.

She did not hate the place. It had held pain, but it had also held friends. Sara could leave without pretending that every memory was bad.

At the new house, a fire waited. A table held food. A room held a bed. Becky carried her small bundle upstairs.

Sara stood in the hall and listened. The house was quiet, but it did not feel empty.

Mr. Carrisford came to stand beside her.

“You may choose what happens next,” he said.

Sara thought about the bakery, the hungry children, and the paper star.

“Then I know where to begin.”

### The First Dinner

The first dinner in the new house had too many plates. Sara counted them twice.

“There are enough for a meeting,” Becky said.

“Perhaps the spoons are invited too.”

Ram Dass placed a bowl of soup on the table. Sita sat on a nearby chair and watched it closely.

“She is not at the table,” Sara said.

Sita moved one inch closer.

“She is still not at the table.”

Sita moved another inch.

Mr. Carrisford laughed. “She believes rules are paths that can be explored.”

“She will explore the kitchen if you let her.”

“That is why Ram Dass watches her.”

Sara sat with Becky. She expected the meal to feel strange because everything was fine. The soup was hot. The bread was fresh. The table was clean.

Becky held her spoon carefully.

“You may eat,” Sara said.

“I know.”

“You are looking at the soup as if it may ask a question.”

“It is a large bowl.”

“It has good manners.”

Becky took a spoonful. Her eyes widened.

“This soup has a warm coat.”

“Then it is dressed for dinner.”

Sara ate too. She tried not to eat quickly. She remembered the bakery boy hiding a bun and the days when a carrot had been a whole meal.

Mr. Carrisford watched her.

“Are you comfortable?”

“Yes.”

“Are you happy?”

Sara thought before answering. “I am safe. Happiness may need time to arrive.”

“It can take a carriage,” Becky said.

“Then we will leave a light on for it.”

The conversation became easy. Ram Dass told a story about Sita stealing a silver button. Sita denied it by hiding another button under the table.

“She is not a good witness,” Sara said.

“She is a very good thief,” Ram Dass answered.

After dinner, Sara asked to see the garden. It was dark, but a small path shone under the lamps.

“The garden is larger than the attic,” Becky said.

“Most places are.”

“Does it have a roof?”

“The sky.”

“The sky leaks.”

“The garden is prepared for it.”

Sara found a tree with a low branch. She touched it.

“This is like the tree in India.”

Mr. Carrisford stood beside her. “You may read here whenever you like.”

Sara looked up at the branches. “Then the garden has already become a friend.”

### Learning about the Fortune

The next day, Mr. Carrisford showed Sara the room where he kept the business papers. He had arranged the documents in clear piles.

“This pile is about the mine,” he said. “This one is about the workers. This one is about the money.”

Sara looked at the pile about workers. “Why is it smaller?”

“The papers are different.”

“Does that mean the workers are less important?”

Mr. Carrisford shook his head. “No. It means business people do not always write enough about people.”

“Then we should add more pages.”

“What would you write?”

“Whether they have safe homes. Whether they have food. Whether their children can learn.”

Mr. Carrisford listened. “You may help me make a better record.”

Sara sat at the desk. She did not understand every number, but she understood the questions behind them.

“What happens if the mine makes less money?”

“We spend less on things we do not need.”

“Such as?”

“Extra furniture. Unused rooms. Decorations.”

Sara looked toward the hall. “The house has many unused rooms.”

“Yes.”

“Could children read in one?”

“Yes.”

“Could families eat in another?”

“Perhaps.”

“Could Becky have a room where she teaches cups?”

Mr. Carrisford smiled. “I am not sure there is a school for cups.”

“There should be.”

They made plans for a reading room. Sara chose a low shelf so small children could reach the books. Becky chose a table that was easy to clean. Ram Dass chose a window that received good light.

Sita chose a cushion and dragged it into the middle of the room.

“The monkey has chosen a throne,” Sara said.

“She will not give it back,” Ram Dass warned.

“Then the reading room has a queen.”

### A Letter for Captain Crewe

Sara wanted to write to her father again. She knew he would never receive the letter, but she needed to tell him what had happened.

Dear Papa,

I found a friend of yours. His name is Mr. Carrisford. He told me about the mines. They were not lost forever. I have a warm room now, and Becky has one too. I still miss you. I am trying to use the good things you gave me well.

She stopped. The next sentence was difficult.

I wish you could see the bakery.

Sara wrote about Anne, Mrs. Brown, the food table, and the children who now had a place to sit. She wrote about the small coin.

I think you would say that a fortune should have a direction. Mine is going toward bread.

She folded the letter and placed it beside the compass. Mr. Carrisford saw it but did not read it.

“Would you like me to keep it safe?” he asked.

“No. I will keep it.”

“You may keep as many letters as you wish.”

Sara placed the letter in the box with the compass and the paper star.

### A Room for Stories

The reading room opened a week later. The shelves held books about gardens, animals, travel, history, and stories. There were cushions on the floor and a small table for drawing.

Ermengarde came first. She brought her purple notebook.

“Where is the shelf for difficult lessons?” she asked.

“This one,” Sara said. “It has many doors.”

Lottie came next with her memory box. She placed it beneath the table.

“The tree must have a quiet place,” she explained.

Becky arranged the cups for tea. She had learned to read a recipe and wanted to try it.

“It says two spoons of sugar,” Becky said.

“You have used four,” Sara answered.

“The tea looked lonely.”

“It will no longer be lonely.”

Ram Dass brought a tray. Sita followed him and took a biscuit.

“That biscuit is not for you,” he said.

Sita put it behind her back.

“Her back is full of secrets,” Sara said.

The girls read, drew, and told stories. The room became busy, but it did not become harsh. When someone made a mistake, another person helped.

Ermengarde taught Lottie a number game. Becky read a page aloud. Sara showed Anne how to find a word in a dictionary.

Anne looked at the shelves. “There are so many books.”

“You may choose one.”

Anne chose a book about bread.

“A wise choice,” Mrs. Brown said when she visited.

“Does it have cake?” Anne asked.

“Turn to the last page.”

The children gathered around the picture. Sita tried to turn the page and tore a corner.

She froze.

Sara looked at the torn page. “We can mend it.”

Sita offered the biscuit.

“That is not enough for a book repair.”

Sita offered a second biscuit.

“Now we can discuss the price.”

Ram Dass took the biscuits away. The page was repaired with paste. Sita watched, perhaps learning that mistakes did not always end in shouting.

### A New Kind of Rich

At the end of the day, Mr. Carrisford asked Sara whether the reading room had been worth the cost.

Sara looked at Ermengarde’s notebook, Lottie’s memory box, Becky’s cups, and Anne’s bread book.

“It cost money,” she said. “But it gave people a place.”

“Is a place worth money?”

“A safe place is worth a great deal.”

Mr. Carrisford nodded. “You are learning how to use the fortune.”

“I am learning how to listen before using it.”

“That is better.”

Sara touched the brass compass. The needle pointed north, but she knew her direction now. She would learn, remember, and help where she could.

The fortune had returned as money. Sara wanted it to return as warmth.

## Chapter 18 - “I Tried Not to Be”

News of Sara’s fortune spread quickly through the school. Miss Minchin came to Mr. Carrisford’s house with a sweet voice and a worried smile.

“Dear Sara,” she said, “I always cared for you.”

Sara remembered the cold attic, the empty plate, and the words about earning her food. She did not answer with anger. She simply did not believe Miss Minchin.

“I am going to live here,” Sara said. “Becky will come with me.”

Miss Minchin tried to speak about school rules and old promises. But Sara no longer depended on her. She could choose where to live and whom to trust.

Later, Ermengarde asked Sara how she had stayed kind during the hard months.

“You were always a princess,” Ermengarde said.

Sara shook her head. “I was angry many times. I was afraid. Sometimes I wanted to say cruel things.”

“But you did not.”

“I tried not to be cruel,” Sara replied. “That is all. Trying matters, even when a person does not do everything perfectly.”

She did not want anyone to think that courage meant never feeling pain. Courage was feeling pain and still looking for a better choice.

Sara moved into Mr. Carrisford’s home. Becky came too. Ermengarde and Lottie visited often. Emily received a clean dress and sat proudly on a new shelf.

Melchisedec remained in the attic, where he had a whole family to supervise. Sara made sure that food reached him.

Sara’s life had changed again. She had more clothes and more rooms than she had ever imagined. Yet she remembered the girl in the street, the cold attic, and the people who had treated servants as if they did not matter.

She decided that being rich must mean more than owning beautiful things. It must mean having the power to make life gentler for someone else.

### Miss Minchin’s New Voice

Miss Minchin did not sleep well after Sara left. She thought about the money that might have come to the school if Sara had remained. She thought about the visitors who would now speak of the school’s treatment of the child.

In the morning, she called for a new dress.

“What sort?” the seamstress asked.

“Something that makes the school look respectable.”

The seamstress looked at her. “A dress cannot repair a reputation.”

“It can help.”

“Only if the person wearing it behaves well.”

Miss Minchin did not enjoy this answer. She wanted Sara’s fortune to erase the cold attic, the unpaid work, and the empty plates. But money could not make the past forget itself.

She wrote a letter to Mr. Carrisford. In it, she said she had always cared for Sara and had protected her from the world.

She wrote that the attic had been temporary.

She wrote that Sara had misunderstood many things.

She did not write that she had called Sara a servant. She did not write that she had counted the cost of food.

Mr. Carrisford showed the letter to Sara.

“Do you want to answer?” he asked.

Sara read it. “She has used many words.”

“Yes.”

“None of them say sorry.”

“No.”

Sara took a clean sheet of paper. She wrote: I remember what happened. I hope the school learns to treat every child kindly.

Mr. Carrisford read it. “You could say more.”

“I could say many angry things.”

“Would that help?”

“It might make me feel better for one minute.”

“And after that?”

Sara folded the paper. “After that, I would still be angry.”

She decided not to send the letter. She did not owe Miss Minchin a speech. She owed herself the choice not to become cruel.

### The School Notices

The girls at the school heard that Sara had a fortune. Some believed she owned a diamond bigger than a house. One girl said Sara’s new bed had gold legs. Another said the monkey had given her a treasure map.

Lottie came to Sara’s new house and asked, “Do you have a diamond bed?”

“No. My bed has wooden legs.”

“Are they gold inside?”

“I have not asked them.”

Ermengarde brought her purple notebook. “I made a list of rumors.”

“How many?”

“Twenty-three.”

“That is too many for one girl.”

“Some say you have a palace under the house.”

“I have a cellar.”

“Some say you will never work again.”

Sara looked at Becky, who was carrying a tray of cups. “I still have work. It is simply work I choose.”

“Some say you will wear a crown.”

“Emily wears one sometimes.”

“Some say you forgot the school.”

Sara shook her head. “I remember it clearly.”

Ermengarde closed the notebook. “Should I add that?”

“Yes. Write: Sara remembers, but remembering is not the same as returning.”

### The New Household

Mr. Carrisford asked Sara what kind of home she wanted. He expected her to ask for rooms, toys, and fine clothes. She asked for a reading room, a warm kitchen, and a place where Becky could work if she wished.

“You may have all three,” he said.

“The kitchen needs a table large enough for children.”

“Why?”

“Because a kitchen should not only make food. It should make room.”

Mr. Carrisford gave her a notebook for household plans. Sara wrote a list.

Warm rooms for the people who live here.

Good food for workers and guests.

Books for children who want to read.

Fair pay for people who work.

Money for the bakery table.

Becky read the list.

“You have become a manager,” she said.

“I am managing a kitchen.”

“The kitchen is already larger than our attic kingdom.”

“Then it needs more rules.”

They made rules for the new house. Nobody was to shout at servants. Everyone who worked was to be thanked. Children could ask questions. Visitors could use the reading room. The best chair could belong to the tiredest person.

Mr. Carrisford read the rules and laughed.

“You have put a rat’s law in a fine house.”

“It is a good law.”

“Yes.”

### Sara’s Angry Day

Sara did not become cheerful every day just because she was safe. One afternoon, a man at the bank spoke about her fortune as if it were a prize he had won.

“The child is fortunate,” he said. “She need not worry about anything.”

Sara felt heat rise in her face.

“I worry about many things.”

The man smiled. “You are young. You will forget.”

Sara wanted to tell him about the empty plate, the wet shoes, and the days when her body had felt too tired to climb the stairs. She wanted him to understand that a fortune could return and still leave memories behind.

Instead, she left the room.

Becky found her in the garden.

“What happened?”

“He said I will forget.”

“Will you?”

“No.”

“Then he is wrong.”

Sara kicked a small stone. “I hate that people think money can repair everything.”

“Can it repair shoes?”

“Yes.”

“Can it repair a torn apron?”

“Yes.”

“Can it make the tear never happen?”

Sara stopped. “No.”

“Then money repairs some things. Friends help with the rest.”

Sara sat beneath a tree. She cried, then became angry that she was crying, then laughed because her feelings seemed to be taking turns like children in a line.

“I am being a very poor princess,” she said.

“You are being a real person.”

“That sounds less grand.”

“Real people need lunch.”

“Then let us have lunch.”

They went inside. Sara told Mr. Carrisford that she had been angry.

“You are allowed,” he said.

“I tried not to be cruel.”

“That is enough for today.”

### A Visit to the Attic

Sara asked whether she could visit the old attic. Mr. Carrisford went with her. The room looked smaller than she remembered.

The cracked wall was still there. The loose board still moved. The window still showed a slice of sky.

“I thought it was larger,” Sara said.

“You were smaller.”

She touched the wall. “It held many stories.”

Melchisedec came out. He looked older and more important.

“Mayor,” Sara said. “How is the kingdom?”

The rat sniffed her fine coat.

“He thinks I have changed.”

Becky placed a crumb near him. He took it.

“He accepts the new clothes,” Becky said.

“He accepts the food.”

“That is the same thing.”

Sara looked at the nest. It held cloth, a blue button, and the corner of her letter to the rat council.

“He kept it.”

“The council saves important papers.”

Sara placed a new piece of cloth near the nest. “For the cold.”

Mr. Carrisford watched her. “Would you like to change the room?”

Sara thought. “I would like to make it safe for whoever lives here next.”

They repaired the window, fixed the loose board, and brought a proper blanket. The school had not yet decided who would use the room. Sara did not want another child to be punished by cold.

“A palace can be made from a poor room,” Sara said, “but it should still have a good roof.”

Mr. Carrisford agreed. He ordered repairs for the attic rooms and the workers’ beds.

Sara did not erase the old room. She made it kinder.

### Ermengarde Learns to Choose

Ermengarde asked Sara how to spend a fortune.

“You should ask someone who knows money,” Sara said.

“I am asking someone who knows what money feels like.”

Sara thought. “First, you make sure people have what they need. Then you may choose what brings joy.”

Ermengarde opened her notebook. “What do people need?”

“Food, shelter, warmth, safety, learning, and someone who listens.”

“That is a long list.”

“People are complicated.”

“What brings joy?”

“Books. Gardens. Cakes. Monkeys, if they are well behaved.”

“That last one may be impossible.”

“Then we may need more books.”

Ermengarde wrote everything down. She still liked lists, but now the lists were not only about her failures. They were about choices.

### The Promise to Lottie

Lottie asked Sara to promise that she would never disappear.

Sara knelt beside her. “I cannot promise never to leave. People travel, and days change.”

Lottie’s face fell.

“I can promise to come back when I say I will. I can write if I cannot come. I can remember you even when I am far away.”

“Will you remember the memory garden?”

“Yes.”

“And the cake?”

“Especially the cake.”

Lottie held Sara’s hand. “That is a good promise.”

Sara understood that an honest promise was stronger than a perfect one. She could not control every day. She could choose to be truthful about what she could do.

### What It Means to Try

At the new house, Sara placed a card above her desk. It said: Try not to be cruel.

The card did not say, Never be angry. It did not say, Never be afraid. It did not demand that Sara become a smiling statue.

It reminded her that every feeling gave her a choice. Anger could become a sharp word, or it could become a request for change. Fear could become hiding, or it could become a hand held by a friend. Sadness could become silence, or it could become a story that helped another child.

Sara did not always choose perfectly. Once, she spoke sharply to a servant and apologized. Another time, she refused to share a cake because she wanted the largest piece. Becky gave her a look.

“The princess has forgotten Rule four,” Becky said.

Sara sighed. “The princess is learning.”

She cut the cake into equal pieces.

“That is better,” Becky said.

“It is also smaller.”

“Kindness is not always a large piece.”

Sara smiled. She kept the card above her desk. It was not a medal. It was a direction.

Miss Minchin had believed a girl’s value rose and fell with her money. Sara had learned something different. A person could be poor and generous, rich and selfish, frightened and brave, angry and still trying.

Sara wanted to keep trying.

### Miss Minchin Visits Again

Miss Minchin visited the new house a second time. She brought a box of papers and a smile that looked carefully prepared.

“I have come to discuss Sara’s future,” she said.

Sara sat near the window. Becky stood beside her.

“My future is already being discussed,” Sara answered.

Miss Minchin looked at Mr. Carrisford. “The child needs discipline.”

“She needs education and care,” he said.

“She had both at my school.”

Sara remembered the attic. She also remembered Monsieur Dufarge, Ermengarde, Lottie, and the girls who had become her friends.

“The school gave me some good things,” she said. “It also gave me hard things.”

Miss Minchin opened the box of papers. “There were costs.”

“You have told me that.”

“Your father promised—”

“My father believed he was paying for a school, not buying a child.”

The room became quiet.

Miss Minchin’s face turned red. “You are ungrateful.”

Sara felt anger rise. She wanted to say that gratitude was not a chain. She wanted to tell Miss Minchin that a warm room did not excuse a cold heart.

She took one breath.

“I am grateful for the lessons I received. I am not grateful for being treated as less than a person.”

Miss Minchin closed the box. “You have become difficult.”

“I have become clear.”

Mr. Carrisford stood. “The visit is over.”

Miss Minchin left with her papers. She did not receive money from Sara. She received a truth she did not want.

### How to Say Sorry

After Miss Minchin left, Sara was quiet.

“I was not cruel,” she said.

“You were firm,” Becky answered.

“I spoke sharply.”

“Sometimes a sharp truth is still true.”

“Should I apologize?”

“For what?”

Sara thought. “For the words that were sharp. Not for remembering.”

She wrote another letter to Miss Minchin. It said: I am sorry that my words hurt you. I am not sorry that I told the truth. I hope you will treat the children at the school with more kindness.

She showed it to Mr. Carrisford.

“That is honest,” he said.

“It may make her angry.”

“She is already angry.”

“Then the letter will have company.”

Sara sent it. She did not expect an answer.

The next week, a message came from the school. Miss Minchin had begun changing some rules. The youngest children received warmer blankets. The servants were allowed to sit for meals. No one knew whether she had changed because of Sara’s letter or because she feared more complaints.

Sara did not claim the change as a victory. A kinder rule mattered even if the person who made it did not say thank you.

### The House Rules

The new house rules became part of daily life. Sara placed them in the reading room.

1. Speak to workers with respect.

2. Give children time to learn.

3. Ask before taking.

4. Share food fairly.

5. Repair what can be repaired.

6. Tell the truth kindly.

7. Rest before a person falls.

Becky read the rules aloud. She stopped at respect.

“Re-spect,” Sara said.

“It has two parts.”

“Many good words do.”

Lottie raised her hand. “What if a person breaks a rule?”

“We talk about it,” Sara said.

“What if the person is a monkey?”

“We talk loudly.”

Sita pulled a pencil from the table.

“She has broken rule three,” Becky said.

Sara held out her hand. “Please return the pencil.”

Sita placed it on the table, then took a biscuit.

“She has asked with her face,” Ram Dass said.

“Her face is not a question.”

Sita held the biscuit out and then pulled it away.

Sara laughed. “Very well. She may have it.”

The rules did not make every day smooth. They made it easier to repair a rough day.

### A Lesson for Adults

Mr. Carrisford asked Sara to join a meeting with the people who helped manage the house. He wanted her to learn how choices were made.

One man said, “Children do not understand money.”

Sara looked at the papers. “Children understand hunger.”

Another man said, “The bakery project may cost too much.”

Sara answered, “Then show us the numbers. We can decide what is needed.”

The men looked at each other. Mr. Carrisford smiled.

Sara did not pretend to understand every number. She asked questions. How much was spent on food? How much was spent on repairs? Who received pay? What happened when the harvest was bad?

The meeting took a long time. Sita entered twice. The second time, she carried a paper with a large number on it.

“The monkey has joined the meeting,” Becky said.

“She may sit if she does not eat the accounts,” Mr. Carrisford replied.

Sita sat on a cushion and watched.

At the end, the adults agreed to make clear records and set aside money for food and learning. Sara had not made every decision. She had helped make better questions.

“You should become a banker,” Mr. Carrisford said.

“No. Bankers have too many papers.”

“You already have many notebooks.”

“Mine have drawings.”

“That is the difference.”

### A Day for Friends

Sara invited the school girls for a day in the garden. Ermengarde brought her notebook. Lottie brought her memory box. Jessie brought the scarf she had once shared.

The girls played a map game. Sara hid paper stars around the garden. Each star held a task: find a leaf, tell a true story, help a friend, or make someone laugh.

Lottie found the star that said help a friend. She took it to a girl who was afraid to climb the small hill.

“I will go with you,” Lottie said.

“You may fall.”

“Then we will sit down.”

They climbed together.

Ermengarde found the star that said tell a true story. She told the story of the king with boots on his hands. The girls laughed.

Jessie found the star that said make someone laugh. She put a leaf on her head and pretended to be a very serious tree.

“This tree is late for a meeting,” Sara said.

Jessie answered, “It has roots in another room.”

Becky served tea. Sita tried to take a cake. Ram Dass caught her gently.

“The cake is for the girls.”

Sita pointed at Sara.

“Sara is a girl,” Becky said.

Sita pointed at herself.

“You are a monkey.”

Sita looked wounded.

Sara cut a tiny piece of cake for her. “A monkey piece.”

Sita accepted it and returned to her cushion.

The garden day was not grand because of expensive decorations. It was grand because the girls were together without fear.

### The Question of Perfect Kindness

Ermengarde asked Sara whether she was now always kind.

Sara shook her head. “No.”

“You have money, friends, and a warm house.”

“Those things do not make a person perfect.”

“What do you do when you fail?”

“I notice it. I say sorry when I should. I try again.”

Ermengarde wrote the answer in her notebook.

“Trying again is becoming a very large part of your story.”

“It is a large part of everyone’s story.”

Lottie came running from the garden. “I broke a pot!”

Sara followed her outside. The pot had fallen beside the memory tree. Soil lay across the path. The little green plant leaned to one side.

Lottie’s face was full of fear.

“I am sorry.”

“Did you do it on purpose?”

“No.”

“Then we repair it.”

They lifted the plant and put it into a new pot. Sara held it while Lottie pressed the soil around the roots.

“It may live,” Lottie said.

“It may.”

“You use that word when you do not know.”

“It is an honest word.”

They watered the plant carefully. Lottie used a small cup.

“Not a river,” Sara reminded her.

“A little lake.”

“A very little lake.”

The plant stood again. It was not perfect. It was growing.

Sara looked at the card above her desk: Try not to be cruel. She added another card below it: Repair what you can.

The cards did not make her a perfect princess. They helped her become a kinder person one choice at a time.

## Chapter 19 - Anne

Sara remembered the bakery and the hungry girl who had eaten her buns. She wanted to know what had happened to Anne.

She returned to the shop with Becky. The bakery woman recognized her at once.

“You are the child who shared her food,” the woman said.

Sara smiled. “And you are the woman who gave me more when I had nothing.”

The bakery woman led Sara to the back room. Anne was there. She wore a clean dress and held a basket of bread.

“Anne has a home now,” the woman explained. “She helps here, and she eats with us.”

Anne looked shyly at Sara. “You gave me the buns.”

“You needed them.”

“You needed them too.”

Sara nodded. “Yes. That is why I remember.”

Sara spoke with the bakery woman about hungry children in the neighborhood. She arranged for the shop to prepare bread and warm food each day. Sara would pay for it, but the food would be given freely to children who needed it.

“No child should stand outside and watch other people eat,” Sara said.

The bakery woman agreed. Anne helped carry the baskets. Soon, children came to the shop for bread, soup, and a safe place to sit.

Sara visited whenever she could. She did not forget the days when her own hands had been empty. She also did not forget the kindness of people who shared what they had.

Ermengarde once asked her why she gave away so much.

Sara looked at the children eating together. “I know what hunger feels like. If I can help, I must.”

She had been called a princess because of her father’s money. In the end, she became a princess because of what she chose to do with her good fortune.

Her life held sorrow, fear, friendship, and wonder. The Magic had not been only blankets and food. It had also been kindness passed from one person to another.

And Sara, who had once needed help, now used her bright new life to help others.

### Back to the Bakery

Sara visited the bakery many times. She liked the warm windows, the wooden tables, and the way the smell of bread reached the street before the people did.

Mrs. Brown had a place for the children near the back wall. It held a long bench, a water jug, and a shelf for cups. Anne kept the cups in a neat row.

“They are looking at me,” Anne said one morning.

“Cups cannot look.”

“These can.”

Sara studied the cups. They had small blue flowers painted on them.

“They are listening,” she decided.

Anne poured milk. She did not spill any.

“You are a fine keeper of cups,” Sara said.

“The cups know where they belong.”

“Do people always know where they belong?”

Anne looked at the children entering the shop. “Not always.”

“Then we must make more benches.”

Mrs. Brown brought a new basket of bread. The basket was so full that one loaf leaned over the side.

“That loaf is trying to escape,” Becky said.

“It is not a loaf,” Anne answered. “It is a traveler.”

“Where is it going?”

“To someone hungry.”

The loaf slipped onto the floor. It rolled beneath the table.

“The traveler has chosen a tunnel,” Sara said.

A small boy crawled under the table and brought it back. He held it up like a prize.

“The traveler is safe,” he announced.

Mrs. Brown cut the loaf. The boy received the first piece.

### Anne’s First Busy Day

Anne had to learn more than counting cups. She learned how to greet children who were shy, how to serve soup without spilling it, and how to tell Mrs. Brown when the flour was low.

One afternoon, three children arrived at the same time. One wanted bread. One wanted milk. One wanted to know whether the monkey lived in the bakery.

Anne answered the last question first.

“No.”

The child looked disappointed.

“But we have a picture of a monkey,” Anne added.

She pointed to a small drawing above the shelf. Sita had once visited the bakery with Sara and had pulled a ribbon from a basket. Mrs. Brown had drawn the memory afterward.

“Does the picture monkey eat bread?”

“Only in the drawing.”

“Does the real monkey eat bread?”

“He tries.”

The child laughed. Anne gave him a piece of bread.

The other two children waited. Anne asked them to stand in a line. She gave milk to the smallest child first. Then she gave bread to the next child. The first child received a bun.

“Why did I get a bun?” he asked.

“Because you helped me answer the monkey question.”

“Can I answer another?”

“Only if you have one.”

“Where do buns sleep?”

Anne thought. “In the bakery basket.”

“Do they dream?”

“They dream of butter.”

The children laughed. Anne felt proud. She had not only carried food. She had made the waiting easier.

### A Child Who Hid His Bun

The boy with the large cap came again. He stood near the door and watched the bread.

“You may come in,” Anne said.

He shook his head.

“You do not have to sing.”

“I know.”

“You do not have to tell a story.”

“I know.”

“You do have to stop standing in the cold.”

He came in.

Anne gave him a bun. He hid it inside his coat.

Mrs. Brown noticed but did not scold him.

“Would you like soup too?” Anne asked.

The boy stared at her. “If I say yes, will you take the bun?”

“No.”

“If I say no?”

“I will believe you.”

“Yes.”

Anne gave him soup. He ate quickly and held the bowl with both hands.

Sara sat beside him.

“The bun is safe,” she said.

“I have had food taken away.”

“Not here.”

“How do you know?”

“Because Mrs. Brown made the rule. Because Anne is keeping the cups. Because I will ask if anyone tries.”

The boy looked at Anne.

“I keep cups,” Anne said. “I can also keep rules.”

The boy took the bun from his coat. He wrapped it in paper for later.

“Bread deserves a better pocket,” he said.

Sara smiled. Her words had traveled farther than she knew.

### The Food Ledger

Sara kept a ledger for the bakery. It showed how much food came in, how much went out, and what Mrs. Brown needed to buy.

“You are making another list,” Becky said.

“This one helps the bread.”

“Bread cannot read.”

“People read the list. People bring the bread.”

Anne learned to write names. Her letters were neat. Becky helped with numbers. Sara checked the sums.

One day, the numbers did not match.

“We should have twelve loaves,” Becky said.

“There are eleven,” Anne answered.

“One loaf is hiding again,” Sara said.

They searched under the table. They looked near the oven. They checked the shelves.

The boy with the large cap stood near the door. He looked at the floor.

“Did you see anything?” Anne asked.

He shook his head.

Sara noticed a round mark of flour on his coat.

“The traveler has chosen your pocket,” she said.

The boy pulled out a small loaf. His face became red.

“I was going to take it home.”

Mrs. Brown came closer. “You should have asked.”

“If I ask, it may be no.”

“If you do not ask, you do not know.”

The boy held the loaf tightly. “I have a little sister.”

Sara looked at Mrs. Brown. Mrs. Brown looked at the ledger.

“Write his sister’s name,” Sara said.

Anne opened the book. “What is it?”

The boy told them. Anne wrote it down.

“He can take the loaf,” Mrs. Brown said. “But next time, he asks.”

The boy nodded. Sara gave him paper to wrap it.

“Bread deserves a better pocket,” she said.

He smiled. “I know.”

The missing loaf became a new rule: a child may ask for food for someone at home. The bakery would make a note and plan for it.

### The Bench by the Window

Sara asked Mr. Carrisford to pay for a second bench. She wanted children to have a place to sit while they ate.

“How many benches?” he asked.

“Two for now.”

“Why not ten?”

“The room has only two walls that are straight.”

He laughed. “Then we will repair the walls and add more benches.”

The new bench was placed near the window. It was made of smooth wood. Anne rubbed it with a cloth until it shone.

“It is not as soft as the chair in the attic,” she said.

“A bench is meant for many people,” Sara replied.

“Then it must be strong.”

The children sat side by side. Some talked. Some were quiet. Some watched the street.

Sara sat with them. She did not want the food table to become a place where rich people looked at poor people from a distance. She wanted it to be a place where people shared a meal.

Mrs. Brown placed a bowl of apples on the table.

“One each,” she said.

The smallest girl looked at the largest apple.

“You may choose first,” Sara told her.

The girl chose a small apple.

“Why not the large one?” Anne asked.

“The large one might be heavy.”

Sara cut the large apple into pieces. Everyone received some.

“Now the apple is not heavy,” she said.

“It is many apples,” the girl replied.

“That is what happens when people share.”

### A Visit from Ermengarde

Ermengarde visited the bakery with her purple notebook. She had written a new list.

- Food for today.
- Food for tomorrow.
- Food for children at home.
- Cups that need washing.
- Buns that must not escape.

Sara read it. “You have become very organized.”

“I learned from the bread.”

“Bread is a strict teacher.”

Ermengarde helped Anne count the cups. She made one mistake and counted the same cup twice.

“There are thirteen,” she said.

Anne looked at the table. “There are twelve.”

“The blue cup is two cups.”

“It is one cup with a flower.”

“The flower is very large.”

“It is still one cup.”

Ermengarde crossed out the thirteen and wrote twelve.

“I am learning.”

“So are the cups,” Anne said.

Ermengarde asked Sara whether being rich felt different.

“It feels different when I look at a table,” Sara answered.

“How?”

“Before, I looked at a table and thought about whether there was enough for me. Now I look at it and ask whether there is enough for everyone who is waiting.”

Ermengarde wrote that down. “That is a very long lesson.”

“Some lessons need a large page.”

### The Bakery Woman’s Choice

Mrs. Brown worried that she could not keep the food table open every day. Flour had become expensive. The oven needed repair. The shop roof leaked when rain was heavy.

“You should use Sara’s money for the children,” Anne said.

Mrs. Brown shook her head. “I need money for the shop too.”

Sara heard this. “The shop is part of the plan. If the bakery cannot work, the food cannot reach the children.”

She asked Mr. Carrisford to pay for the oven repair and the roof. He agreed, but Sara made him write down the cost.

“Why?” he asked.

“So we know what we are doing.”

“You do not trust me?”

“I trust you. A clear list helps trust stay clear.”

Mr. Carrisford signed the paper. Mrs. Brown read it.

“This is more than a gift,” she said. “It is a promise to keep the bakery open.”

“Then we must keep our promises,” Sara answered.

The oven was repaired. The roof was fixed. Rain no longer fell into the flour room.

“The house is wearing a new hat,” Anne said.

“A roof is a very useful hat,” Sara replied.

### A Day of Plenty

On a clear Saturday, the bakery table was full. There were loaves, buns, soup, milk, apples, and a little cake. The children arrived in a line that went almost to the corner.

Sara worried that the food would not be enough. Mrs. Brown had planned carefully. Anne counted each bowl. Becky carried water.

The boy with the large cap helped carry bread. His little sister came with him. She held the broken wooden horse from the first day.

“The horse is guarding the table again,” Becky said.

“He has been promoted,” the boy answered.

Anne gave the little girl a cup of milk.

“The horse does not drink,” the girl said.

“Then he may guard the milk.”

Children sat at both benches. Some ate silently. Some talked about school, weather, and a dog that had chased a hat.

Sara sat at the end of the table. She saw Anne move from child to child. Anne no longer looked like the starving girl outside the bakery. The memory was still true, but it was not the whole of her life.

Mrs. Brown placed a small cake in front of Sara.

“For you.”

Sara shook her head. “For everyone.”

“It is not large enough.”

“Then cut it thin.”

Mrs. Brown cut the cake into many pieces. Each piece was small, but each child received one.

“It tastes like a birthday,” Lottie said, though she was visiting with Ermengarde.

“A bakery birthday,” Sara replied.

### What Anne Remembered

After the children left, Anne sat by the oven. She held the old coin in her hand.

“Mrs. Brown keeps it here,” she explained.

“It started a very long story,” Sara said.

Anne looked at the coin. “You gave me food when you were hungry.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I saw you.”

“Now I see other children.”

Sara smiled. “Then the story is moving.”

Anne put the coin back in its box. “Will I always work here?”

“You may choose when you are older. You can learn more too.”

“What can I learn?”

“Reading, numbers, cooking, business, anything that helps you choose your path.”

Anne looked at the ledger. “I want to learn the whole book.”

“Then we will find you a teacher.”

“Can Becky help?”

“Becky knows about cups.”

“And lists.”

“And lists.”

Becky came from the kitchen. “I heard my name.”

Anne held up the ledger. “Teach me the numbers.”

Becky sat beside her. Sara watched them begin. The children who had once been helped were now helping one another.

### The Princess’s Last Question

That evening, Sara returned home. She passed the warm rooms, the books, and the comfortable chair. She placed Emily on the shelf beside the compass.

“What is a princess?” she asked.

Emily did not answer.

Sara thought of the word as she had first understood it. A princess was not a child with many clothes. She was not a child who received every gift. A princess was someone who tried to be kind, especially when kindness was difficult.

But Sara did not want the word to become too serious. She picked up Emily and placed a paper crown on her head.

“A princess also needs a hat,” she said.

Becky entered with tea. “Emily is wearing a crown.”

“She is attending a royal meeting.”

“What is the meeting about?”

“Buns, cups, benches, and the proper size of cake.”

“A very important meeting.”

Sara looked out the window. The city was still large. Many children still needed help. Her fortune could not fix every sorrow, but it could open some doors.

“We will make another plan tomorrow,” she said.

“What plan?”

“We will see what is needed.”

Becky poured the tea. “That is not a very exciting ending.”

Sara smiled. “It is not the ending. It is the next page.”

She remembered India, Captain Crewe, the cold attic, Melchisedec, the Magic, Mr. Carrisford, Ram Dass, Ermengarde, Lottie, Becky, Anne, and the bakery woman. Each person had added something to her life. Each kindness had crossed a wall.

Sara lifted her cup.

“To the next page,” she said.

Emily looked serious. Becky raised her cup. Outside, the city moved through the night. Inside, a warm room held a girl who had suffered, learned, and chosen to help.

Before bed, Sara checked the bakery ledger one more time. Anne had written the names neatly. Becky had checked the numbers. Mrs. Brown had marked tomorrow’s bread order.

“The next page is ready,” Becky said.

Sara closed the book. “Then tomorrow can begin.”

She placed the ledger beside the compass. One showed where help was needed. The other showed a direction. Sara knew she would still feel fear and anger sometimes. She also knew she did not have to face those feelings alone.

In the quiet room, Emily wore her paper crown. The compass pointed north. The house held its warmth. Sara fell asleep thinking of bread, books, friends, and the many doors that kindness could open.

In the morning, Sara woke before everyone else. She opened the ledger and wrote one final note beneath the bakery rules: “A princess is not someone who stands above people. A princess is someone who notices when another person is standing alone.”

She read the sentence aloud. Becky pretended to be asleep, but one eye opened.

“That is a very serious breakfast,” Becky said.

“We can have toast with it.”

“Much better.”

Emily came running in with the paper crown. The crown had lost one point during the night. Sara repaired it with the red ribbon.

Outside, Anne opened the bakery door. Mrs. Brown set bread on the table. Somewhere in the city, a child who had once been alone was walking toward a warm place.

Sara took the compass, the ledger, and a fresh piece of paper. There would be more stories, more mistakes, and more doors. She was ready to meet them with her friends.

She wrote the first words of a new story before breakfast: “Once there was a girl who thought she was alone. Then she looked again.”

Emily leaned over the table. “What happens next?”

Sara smiled. “We will find out.”

And so the house began another day: with bread to share, lessons to learn, and friends close enough to hear when someone needed them.
